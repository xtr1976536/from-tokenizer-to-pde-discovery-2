A new method is developed for finding exact solitary wave solutions of
a generalized Korteweg-de Vries equation with $p$ -power nonlinearity
coupled to a linear Schrödinger equation
arising in many different physical applications.
This method yields 22 solution families, with $p=1,2,3,4$ .
No solutions for $p>1$ were known previously in the literature.
For $p=1$ ,
four of the solution families contain bright/dark Davydov solitons of the 1st and 2nd kind,
obtained in recent literature by basic ansatze applied to the ODE system for travelling waves.
All of the new solution families have interesting features,
including bright/dark peaks
with (up to) $p$ symmetric pairs of side peaks in the amplitude
and a kink profile for the nonlinear part in the phase.
The present method is fully systematic and involves several novel steps
which reduce the travelling wave ODE system to a single nonlinear base ODE
for which all polynomial solutions are found by symbolic computation.
It is applicable more generally to other coupled nonlinear dispersive wave equations
as well as to nonlinear ODE systems of generalized Hénon-Heiles form. 1. Introduction A wide class of physical wave propagation phenomena are modeled by
nonlinear dispersive systems.
The simplest model that combines dispersion and weak nonlinearity is given by
the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation $u_{t}+\alpha uu_{x}+\beta u_{xxx}=0$ for uni-directional wave motion $u(t,x)$ ,
which describes [ 1 , 2 ] surface waves in shallow water,
internal waves in stratified fluids,
ion-acoustic waves in plasmas,
atmospheric Rossby waves,
compression waves in granular chains,
deformations of long-chain molecules and anharmonic lattices,
and numerous other physical applications.
Higher-order nonlinearities are of physical interest in several of these applications [ 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ] . In more complex physical systems,
nonlinear dispersive wave motion is coupled to high-frequency waves,
as modeled by a linear Schrödinger equation (LS) $i\psi_{t}+\kappa\psi_{xx}=\sigma u\psi$ for $\psi(t,x)$ in which $u(t,x)$ acts as a potential well.
Physical applications include
electron propagation coupled to nonlinear ion-acoustic waves in a collisionless plasma [ 7 , 8 ] ,
resonant interaction of capillary-gravity waves and internal water waves [ 9 ] ,
electron propagation along deformable molecular chains [ 10 , 11 , 12 ] ,
and energy transport by electrons in anharmonic lattices [ 13 , 14 ] .
See Ref. [ 15 , 16 ] for further discussion. This motivates the study of a general coupled gKdV-LS system $\displaystyle u_{t}+\alpha u^{p}u_{x}+\beta u_{xxx}$ $\displaystyle=\gamma(|\psi|^{2})_{x},$ (1.1) $\displaystyle i\psi_{t}+\kappa\psi_{xx}$ $\displaystyle=\sigma u\psi,$ (1.2) where $p>0$ is the nonlinearity power and $\alpha$ is the nonlinearity coefficient, $\beta$ and $\kappa$ are the dispersion coefficients,
while $\gamma$ and $\sigma$ are the coupling constants.
All of these parameters are non-zero, real constants.
Here, $u(t,x)$ is the (real) gKdV field and $\psi$ is the (complex) LS field,
which respectively describe the wave amplitude or lattice deformation,
and the electron wave function or envelope of high-frequency waves. One main question is understanding the nature of
coherent propagating structures in this system, in particular,
solitary wave solutions of different shapes and subsonic/supersonic speeds.
Such solutions have the form of phase-modulated travelling waves $u=U(\xi),\quad\psi=e^{-i\omega t}\Psi(\xi),\quad\xi=x-ct,$ (1.3) where $c$ is the wave speed, and $\omega$ is the frequency.
In physical applications,
a travelling wave is supersonic if $c>0$ and subsonic if $c<0$ [ 11 , 13 , 16 ] .
Here $U$ is a real function and $\Psi$ is a complex function,
which satisfy a coupled nonlinear system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) $\beta U^{\prime\prime\prime}+(\alpha U^{p}-c)U^{\prime}+\gamma(|\Psi|^{2})^{\prime}=0,\quad\kappa\Psi^{\prime\prime}-ic\Psi^{\prime}+(\sigma U+\omega)\Psi=0.$ (1.4) Solitary wave solutions require that $U$ decays exponentially for large $|\xi|$ to a constant, $b$ ,
and likewise that $|\Psi|$ has a similar decay. For a KdV nonlinearity, $p=1$ ,
some exact solutions are known
which were obtained by use of elementary ODE methods combined with trial ansatze [ 7 , 13 ] and also by the bi-linear ansatz method [ 17 ] ;
for a mKdV nonlinearity, $p=2$ ,
only approximate solutions have been found [ 14 ] .
No work has been done to-date on higher nonlinearities. The primary goal of the present paper is to develop and apply a new general method
that yields numerous exact families of solitary wave solutions ( 1.3 ).
In total,
nine solution families are obtained for $p=1$ ,
eight families for $p=2$ ,
four families for $p=3$ ,
and a single family for $p=4$ .
All of the solutions are non-singular.
The gKdV component $U(\xi)$ has • sech-squared profile • bright/dark peak with exponential decay • width $w$ and height/depth $h$ related by $w^{2}h\propto|\alpha|^{\frac{1}{p}}|\beta|^{\frac{1}{p}-2}|\kappa|^{3-\frac{2}{p}}/|\sigma|$ • zero or non-zero (asymptotic) background, $b$ More interestingly,
the magnitude of the LS component $|\Psi(\xi)|$ has • bright/dark central peak • asymptotic tail which is bright/dark relative to the central peak • in some cases, symmetric pairs of side peaks (up to $p$ in total) while the LS phase $\arg(\Psi(\xi))$ has • a linear part $\frac{c}{2}\xi$ • in some cases, a nonlinear part with a kink profile For $p=2,3,4$ , the solutions are new.
For $p=1$ , five families are new,
while the other four families contain the previously known solutions
in Ref. [ 7 , 13 ] as particular subfamilies.
Qualitatively novel features are the kink part in the LS phase
and the presence of multiple symmetric side peaks in the LS amplitude. The new method consists of the following main steps: (1) express the form of the travelling waves as a symmetry-invariant solution
and obtain first integrals for the ODE system by use of multi-reduction symmetry theory [ 18 ] ; (2) change to polar variables and apply a hodograph transformation that
leads to a triangular (decoupled) system in which the base ODE has a polynomial form; (3) employ a power-balance technique to determine the general form for polynomial solutions of the base ODE; (4) characterize conditions under which such solutions yield solitary waves; (5) with those conditions, solve an overdetermined algebraic system
for the coefficients in the polynomial solutions
along with the power $p$ and the parameters $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\kappa,\sigma$ in the original gKdV-LS system
in addition to $c,\omega,b$ in the form of the travelling wave. This algebraic system is nonlinear, with $p$ appearing in exponents,
and so it is very non-trivial to solve.
All solutions are found by employing the symbolic computer algebra package Crack [ 24 ] which is able to do non-polynomial case distinctions and splittings systematically.
There is a further non-trivial aspect, stemming
from the conditions for a solution to yield a solitary wave.
Some of these conditions consist of inequalities on the parameters in the solution,
while each solution has a different subset of the parameters that are free,
up to reparameterization freedom.
An optimal choice of free parameters must be found so that
all of the inequalities can be solved explicitly,
thereby yielding the allowed kinematic region in parameter space.
This is carried out by use of Maple,
which is also used to verify the final simplified explicit form
for each family of solitary wave solutions. Some additional results are obtained.
All Lie point symmetries and low-order conservation laws are derived for
the coupled gKdV-LS system ( 1.1 )–( 1.2 ).
Through these conservation laws,
a variational principle for phase-modulated travelling waves ( 1.3 ) is found
and conserved quantities describing mass, charge, energy, and momentum
are evaluated for each family of solitary waves.
The variational principle provides a starting point for analysis of stability of the solitary waves. The present method is applicable more generally to
other coupled nonlinear dispersive wave equations
as well as to nonlinear ODE systems of generalized Hénon-Heiles form. The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
Section 2 discusses the Hamiltonian and Lagrangian formulation of
the coupled gKdV-LS system ( 1.1 )–( 1.2 ),
and gives the dimensionless form of this system which is used for subsequent results.
Section 3 gives the derivation of the Lie point symmetries and low-order conservation laws
and describes the physical meaning of the conserved quantities.
Section 4 states the variational principle for phase-modulated travelling waves
and sets up the first two steps in the new method for finding solitary wave solutions.
The remaining three steps in this method are carried out in section 5 ,
and a classification of all of the solution families is provided.
Section 6 outlines the main features of each family,
including a few remarks on stability for these solitary wave solutions.
Finally, some concluding remarks are made in section 7 . Computational steps of the method are given in Appendix A ;
the output for each case $p=1,2,3,4$ is shown in Appendix B ,
which also contains the solved inequalities giving the allowed kinematic region in parameter space for each solution family. 2. Hamiltonian structure and dimensionless form The gKdV-LS system ( 1.1 )–( 1.2 )
has a Hamiltonian structure $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
\psi\end{pmatrix}=\mathcal{D}\begin{pmatrix}\delta H/\delta u\\
\delta H/\delta\bar{\psi}\end{pmatrix}$ (2.1) in which $\mathcal{D}={\rm diag}(D_{x},-\tfrac{\sigma}{\gamma}i)$ (2.2) is a Hamiltonian operator,
and $H=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\beta u_{x}^{2}-\tfrac{1}{(p+1)(p+2)}\alpha u^{p+2}+(\kappa\gamma/\sigma)|\psi_{x}|^{2}+\gamma u|\psi|^{2}\big)\,dx$ (2.3) is the Hamiltonian functional.
The associated Poisson bracket is given by $\{F,G\}={\rm Re}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\begin{pmatrix}\delta F/\delta u\\
\delta F/\delta\bar{\psi}\end{pmatrix}^{\dagger}\mathcal{D}\begin{pmatrix}\delta G/\delta u\\
\delta G/\delta\bar{\psi}\end{pmatrix}\,dx,$ (2.4) where $F$ and $G$ are arbitrary functionals of $u$ , $\psi$ , $\bar{\psi}$ and their $x$ -derivatives.
This bracket is skew and satisfies the Jacobi identity
as a consequence of $\mathcal{D}$ being a Hamiltonian operator,
as follows from general results in the theory of Hamiltonian PDEs [ 20 ] . A Lagrangian formulation can be obtained via introduction of a potential by $u=\theta_{x}$ .
The system ( 1.1 )–( 1.2 ) then takes the form $\displaystyle\theta_{tx}+\alpha\theta_{x}^{p}\theta_{xx}+\beta\theta_{xxxx}$ $\displaystyle=\gamma(|\psi|^{2})_{x},$ (2.5) $\displaystyle i\psi_{t}+\kappa\psi_{xx}$ $\displaystyle=\sigma\theta_{x}\psi,$ (2.6) which is readily seen to be the Euler-Lagrange equations of the Lagrangian $L=-\tfrac{1}{2}\theta_{t}\theta_{x}-\tfrac{1}{(p+1)(p+2)}\theta_{x}^{p+2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\beta\theta_{xx}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\gamma/\sigma)i(\bar{\psi}_{t}\psi-\bar{\psi}\psi_{t})+(\gamma\kappa/\sigma)|\psi_{x}|^{2}+\gamma\theta_{x}|\psi|^{2}.$ (2.7) Specifically, $\delta L/\delta\theta=\theta_{tx}+\alpha\theta_{x}^{p}\theta_{xx}+\beta\theta_{xxxx}-\gamma(|\psi|^{2})_{x}$ and $i(\sigma/\gamma)\delta L/\delta\bar{\psi}=i\psi_{t}+\kappa\psi_{xx}-\sigma\theta_{x}\psi$ . 2.1. will provide a foundation for the subsequent analysis.
Generators of continuous point symmetries can be obtained by
the standard Lie symmetry method [ 20 , 21 ] ,
which yields the following result. Proposition 2 . (i)
The continuous symmetries of the gKdV-LS system ( 2.8 )–( 2.9 )
for general $p\neq 0$ are generated by $\mathbf{X}_{1}=\partial_{t},\quad\mathbf{X}_{2}=\partial_{x},\quad\mathbf{X}_{3}=i\psi\partial_{\psi}-i\bar{\psi}\partial_{\bar{\psi}}.$ (3.1) Respectively, they comprise time-translations, space-translations, and phase rotations, $t\to t+\epsilon_{1},\quad x\to x+\epsilon_{2},\quad\psi\to\exp(i\epsilon_{3})\psi,$ (3.2) where $\epsilon_{1}$ , $\epsilon_{2}$ , $\epsilon_{3}$ $\in{\mathbb{R}}$ are group parameters.
(ii)
Additional continuous symmetries arise only for $p=1$ .
They are generated by $\mathbf{X}_{4}=t\partial_{x}+\partial_{u}+(s_{1}kt+\tfrac{1}{2}x)(i\psi\partial_{\psi}-i\bar{\psi}\partial_{\bar{\psi}}),$ (3.3) which constitutes a Galilean boost $\quad x\to x+\epsilon_{4}t,\quad u\to u+\epsilon_{4},\quad\psi\to\exp\big(i(\epsilon_{4}(kt+\tfrac{1}{2}x)+\epsilon_{4}{}^{2}\tfrac{1}{4}t)\big)\psi$ (3.4) with group parameter $\epsilon_{4}$ $\in{\mathbb{R}}$ . These transformations ( 3.2 ) and ( 3.4 )
generate, respectively,
a three-dimensional abelian symmetry group and a four-dimensional non-abelian symmetry group with the commutator structure $[\mathbf{X}_{1},\mathbf{X}_{4}]=\mathbf{X}_{2}+s_{1}k\mathbf{X}_{3},\quad[\mathbf{X}_{2},\mathbf{X}_{4}]=\mathbf{X}_{3}.$ (3.5) The gKdV-LS system ( 2.8 )–( 2.9 ) also possesses
a discrete point symmetry $t\to-t$ , $x\to-x$ , $\psi\to\bar{\psi}$ ,
which is a space-time reflection combined with complex conjugation. 3.1. Conservation laws A conservation law is a continuity equation $D_{t}T+D_{x}X=0$ (3.6) that holds for all formal solutions $(u(t,x),\psi(t,x))$ of the gKdV-LS system ( 2.8 )–( 2.9 ),
where $D_{t}$ and $D_{x}$ denote total derivatives with respect to $t$ and $x$ ,
which act by the chain rule [ 20 , 21 ] .
Here $T$ is the conserved density and $X$ is the spatial flux; the pair $(T,X)$ is called the conserved current.
In general, $T$ and $X$ can be functions of $t$ , $x$ , $u$ , $\psi$ , $\bar{\psi}$ , and $x$ -derivatives of $u$ , $\psi$ , $\bar{\psi}$ ,
with $t$ -derivatives being eliminated through the equations ( 2.8 ) and ( 2.9 ). Conservation laws in which $T=D_{x}\Theta$ and $X=-D_{t}\Theta$ hold
for all formal solutions $(u(t,x),\psi(t,x))$ are trivial,
since the continuity equation reduces to an identity via commutativity of derivatives.
Trivial conservation laws thus contain no information about solutions.
Any conservation laws that differ just by a trivial conservation law
can be viewed as being locally equivalent.
Consequently,
only non-trivial conservation laws up to local equivalence are of interest. Any non-trivial conservation law yields a corresponding conserved integral $\int_{\Omega}T\,dx$ which satisfies $\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}T\,dx=-X|_{\partial\Omega}$ ,
on any given spatial domain $\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}$ of interest.
Under appropriate boundary conditions posed at $\partial\Omega$ for solutions $(u(t,x),\psi(t,x))$ ,
the conserved integral will be time-independent.
In the situation when $\Omega={\mathbb{R}}$ is the real line,
the necessary boundary conditions take the form of spatial decay conditions on $u(t,x)$ and $|\psi(t,x)|$ as $|x|\to\infty$ , so that the spatial flux $X\to 0$ , whereby $\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}}T\,dx=0.$ (3.7) Note that the conserved integral $\int_{\mathbb{R}}T\,dx$ is unchanged
if $T$ is replaced by a locally equivalent conserved density,
satisfying suitable spatial decay conditions.
The differential order of a conserved integral will refer to
the minimum of the differential orders of all conserved densities
that are locally equivalent to $T$ .
Conserved integrals of most physical interest typically have a differential order
that is no higher than the differential order of the Hamiltonian. For the method applied in subsequent sections for deriving
explicit solutions $(u(t,x),\psi(t,x))$ of the gKdV-LS system ( 2.8 )–( 2.9 ),
it will be essential to know both the conserved density $T$ and the spatial flux $X$ in a conservation law. The generators of continuous point symmetries can be used to obtain
conservation laws of the gKdV-LS system ( 2.8 )–( 2.9 )
through the Hamiltonian version of Noether’s theorem.
Additional conservation laws arise from the Casimirs of the Hamiltonian structure. Alternatively, conservation laws can be obtained directly by use of the standard multiplier method [ 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 ] ,

Two remarks on these conserved integrals will be worthwhile. The energy (Hamiltonian) will be non-negative when $s_{1}=-1$ , $s_{2}={\rm sgn}(k)=-{\rm sgn}(u)$ ,
and otherwise it will have an indefinite sign.
The sign of $s_{1}$ controls the gKdV portion of the energy,
while the signs of $s_{2}$ and $k$ control the LS portion.
When the sign is indefinite,
conservation of energy does not preclude blow-up of $|u|$ , $|u_{x}|$ , $|\psi|$ , $|\psi_{x}|$ caused by concentration of the energy density, which is referred to as focusing ;
in contrast, when the sign is non-negative,
conservation of energy prevents such blow-up, which is referred to as defocusing (see e.g. Ref. [ 25 ] ). The Galilean momentum can be expressed as ${G}=kt(s_{2}{P}+s_{1}{J})-\chi(t)$ where $\chi(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(s_{1}s_{2}ku-\tfrac{1}{2}|\psi|^{2})x\,dx$ is like a center of mass-charge.
In particular,
under decay conditions such that ${G}$ , ${P}$ , ${J}$ are conserved integrals, $\chi(t)$ satisfies $\frac{d}{dt}\chi(t)=k(s_{2}{P}+s_{1}{J})$ .
This implies $\chi(t)=\chi(0)+tV$ , where $V=k(s_{2}{P}+s_{1}{J})$ is the speed
at which the center of mass-charge moves. 4. ODE system for phase-modulated travelling waves Phase-modulated travelling waves ( 1.3 )
have a symmetry characterization that they are invariant under
a Galilean transformation $x\to x+c\epsilon$ , $t\to t+\epsilon$ to a reference frame moving with speed $c$ ,
combined with a phase rotation $\psi\to e^{-i\omega\epsilon}\psi$ having angular speed $\omega$ .
Specifically, the symmetry generator $\mathbf{X}=\partial_{t}+c\partial_{x}-i\omega\psi\partial_{\psi}+i\omega\bar{\psi}\partial_{\bar{\psi}}$ (4.1) has the invariants $U=u$ , $\Psi=e^{i\omega t}\psi$ , $\xi=x-ct$ ,
and hence a phase-modulated travelling wave is an invariant solution of
the gKdV-LS system ( 2.8 )–( 2.9 )
under this symmetry. Substitution of the invariant form ( 1.3 ) into the coupled equations ( 2.8 ) and ( 2.9 )
yields a coupled nonlinear ODE system for $U(\xi)$ and $\Psi(\xi)$ : $\displaystyle U^{\prime\prime\prime}+(s_{1}U^{p}-c)U^{\prime}+s_{2}(|\Psi|^{2})^{\prime}=0,$ (4.2a) $\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime\prime}-ic\Psi^{\prime}+(kU+\omega)\Psi=0.$ (4.2b) It will be useful to consider the polar form of this system,
which arises from expressing $\Psi$ in amplitude-phase form $\Psi=A\exp(i\Phi).$ (4.3) The polar variables $A(\xi)$ and $\Phi(\xi)$ , along with $U(\xi)$ , satisfy

Variational formulation and symmetries The ODE system ( 4.4 ) for phase-modulated travelling waves
has a variational formulation in terms of the conserved integrals

for mass, charge, momentum, and energy
evaluated for solutions $U(\xi)$ , $A(\xi)$ , $\Phi(\xi)$ .

with $C_{1}$ being the constant of integration. This variational formulation is a generalization of the known variational formulations

a translation symmetry and a phase-shift symmetry $\mathbf{X}_{\rm trans.}=\partial_{\xi},\quad\mathbf{X}_{\rm phas.}=\partial_{\Phi}$ (4.11) from the symmetries of the gKdV-LS system ( 2.8 )–( 2.9 ).

in explicit form for $U(\xi)$ , $A(\xi)$ , $\Phi(\xi)$ .
Note that physically non-trivial solutions must have $U^{\prime}\not\equiv 0$ and $A^{\prime}\not\equiv 0$ . This system consists of one third-order ODE ( 4.4a ) and two second-order ODEs ( 4.4b ) and ( 4.4c ).
Therefore, finding the general solution would require a total of seven integrations.
As noted in the work Ref. [ 13 , 14 ] ,
inspection of the ODEs suggest that only a few explicit integrations are possible. Nevertheless, remarkably,
the system ( 4.4 ) can be reduced to a single second-order ODE
from which $U(\xi)$ , $A(\xi)$ , $\Phi(\xi)$ can be obtained by quadratures.
This reduction is found by the following new method which involves two main steps. In the first step, symmetry multi-reduction is applied,
which gives a set of three functionally independent first integrals.
These first integrals arise from conservation laws of
the gKdV-LS system ( 2.8 )–( 2.9 )
such that the conservation law is invariant (up to local equivalence)
under the symmetry ( 4.1 ) which characterizes
phase-modulated travelling waves.
The multi-reduction method yields the first integrals directly,
without the need to know in advance any conservation laws.
From the first integrals,
an explicit reduction to a system of one second-order ODE and two first-order ODEs
is obtained. The second step starts from observing that
the resulting second-order ODE in the first step
can be expressed in the form of a nonlinear oscillator equation.
This equation admits an energy-type first integral,
which can be combined with the remaining two first-order ODEs to get
a decoupled second-order ODE by employing a hodograph transformation.
The resulting system of ODEs is triangular such that every solution of
the transformed second-order ODE yields a solution $(U(\xi)$ , $A(\xi)$ , $\Phi(\xi))$ by quadratures.
Furthermore,
two of these quadratures can be carried out explicitly to obtain $U(\xi)$ and $A(\xi)$ ,
while $\Phi(\xi)$ is expressed as a final quadrature in terms of $(U(\xi),A(\xi))$ . The preceding steps will be carried out in the next subsections. 4.3. First integrals from symmetry multi-reduction The first step consists of deriving first integrals of the ODE system ( 4.4 )
by use of the method of symmetry multi-reduction [ 18 ] .
This method relies on the property if a conservation law ( 3.6 )
of the gKdV-LS system ( 2.8 )–( 2.9 )

then the conserved density $T$ and the flux $X$ yield a first integral $\tfrac{dC}{d\xi}=0$ given by $C=(X-cT)|_{u=U,\psi=e^{i\omega t}\Psi}=\text{const.}$ (4.13) for all solutions $(U(\xi)$ , $A(\xi)$ , $\Phi(\xi))$ .
This expression ( 4.13 ) has the physical meaning of the net flux

Specifically, there is an explicit condition characterizing multipliers that yield symmetry-invariant conservation laws. For the gKdV-LS system ( 2.8 )–( 2.9 ),
a multiplier is a pair of functions $(Q^{u},Q^{\psi})$ of $t$ , $x$ , $u$ , $\psi$ , $\bar{\psi}$ , and $x$ -derivatives of $u$ , $\psi$ , $\bar{\psi}$ ,

Since the system ( 2.8 )–( 2.9 ) is comprised of evolution equations,

every non-trivial conservation law (up to local equivalence)

and that, secondly,

Hence, only multipliers given by functions of $t$ , $x$ , $u$ , $\psi$ , $\bar{\psi}$ , and $x$ -derivatives of $u$ , $\psi$ , $\bar{\psi}$ up to second-order

Polynomial solutions of the decoupled ODE The second-order ODE ( 4.33 ) for $G(U)$ is quadratic in $G$ , $G^{\prime}$ , $G^{\prime\prime}$ , and also is polynomial in $U$ when $p$ is a positive integer. All polynomial solutions for $G(U)$ can be obtained by the following analysis. First, the possible degree of a polynomial solution
is determined by balancing powers in the ODE ( 4.33 ).
Suppose that the highest power of $U$ in $G(U)$ is $U^{q}$ ,
with $q>1$ due to condition ( 4.36 ).
Then, on the lefthand side in the ODE ( 4.33 ),
the terms containing highest powers of $U$ are $G(U)G^{\prime\prime}(U)^{2}$ and $U^{p+2}G^{\prime\prime}(U)^{2}$ , since $p\geq 1$ .
Their leading powers are, respectively, $3q-4$ and $2q+p-2$ .
On the righthand side, the terms containing highest powers of $U$ are $UG^{\prime}(U)^{2}$ and $G(U)G^{\prime}(U)$ ,
both of which have the leading power $2q-1$ .
There are now several different possibilities for balancing powers. The first possibility is to balance the leading powers on opposite sides.
If $2q+p-2>3q-4$ ,
then this balance is given by $2q+p-2=2q-1>3q-4$ ,
which implies $p=1$ and $q<3$ .
If $2q+p-2\leq 3q-4$ ,
then the balance is $3q-4=2q-1\geq 2q+p-2$ which implies $q=3$ and $p\leq 1$ .
Thus, a consistent balance holds only for $p=1$ and $q\leq 3$ . The second possibility is that the two terms with leading powers $2q+p-2$ and $3q-4$ cancel on the lefthand side, whereby the term with the subleading power
must be balanced by the leading power term on the righthand side.
In this case, equality of the powers $2q+p-2=3q-4$ implies $q=p+2$ .
Suppose the subleading term in $G(U)$ has power $l<q$ , $l\geq 0$ .
Then the subleading term on the lefthand side will be
the leading term in $(G(U)+s_{1}s_{2}\tfrac{1}{(p+1)(p+2)}U^{p+2})G^{\prime\prime}(U)^{2}$ ,
with corresponding highest power $l+2q-4=l+2p$ .
The leading terms on the righthand side have highest power $2q-1=2p+3$ .
Hence, the balance gives $l=3$ , with no restriction on $p$ . The last possibility is that the two terms with leading power $2q-1$ on the righthand side cancel each other,
whereby the term with the subleading power
must be balanced by the leading power term on the lefthand side.
In this case, the cancellation requires that the highest power term in $kUG^{\prime}(U)^{2}-kG(U)G^{\prime}(U)=kG^{\prime}(U)(UG^{\prime}(U)-G(U))$ vanishes.
Clearly, this implies that $q=1$ , and thus no balance exists with $q>1$ . The preceding possibilities can be merged into the form $G(U)=-s_{1}s_{2}\tfrac{1}{(p+1)(p+2)}U^{p+2}+\tfrac{1}{3}g_{3}U^{3}+\tfrac{1}{2}g_{2}U^{2}+g_{1}U+g_{0},$ (5.1) where the coefficients $g_{0},g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}$ are constant parameters to be determined.
When this polynomial form is substituted in the ODE ( 4.33 ),
a polynomial equation of degree $2p+3$ in $U$ is obtained.
All solutions of interest will be listed after the discussion in the next subsection. 5.2. Classification of solitary wave solutions Rather than systematically work out all solutions $(U(\xi),A(\xi),\Phi(\xi))$ produced by the polynomial form ( 5.1 ) for $G(U)$ ,

which leads to a singularity at $\xi=0$ .
Likewise,
convergence for $|\xi|\to\infty$ requires $A(\pm\infty)^{2}>0$ ,
since otherwise a singularity occurs due to $A(\xi)^{2}\sim O(e^{-a|\xi|})$ as $\xi\sim\pm\infty$ . Therefore, the conditions $\displaystyle A(\pm\infty)^{2}>0,\quad A(0)^{2}>0,\quad\text{ if }\mu\neq 0,$ (5.14a) $\displaystyle A(\pm\infty)^{2}\geq 0,\quad A(0)^{2}\geq 0,\quad\text{ if }\mu=0,$ (5.14b) together with $h>0,\quad a>0,\quad\mu\geq 0$ (5.15) are necessary.
Note that if $\mu=0$ and $A(0)=A(\pm\infty)=0$ then the full condition ( 5.12 ) must be checked. Now, the final step will be to solve the second-order ODE ( 4.33 )
with $G(U)$ having the polynomial form ( 5.1 ).
Hereafter, it will be convenient to re-parameterize this polynomial by
using the solitary wave parameters $b,h,a,E$ , and $s_{3}=\pm 1$ : $g_{0}=s_{2}(\tilde{C}_{4}-E),\quad g_{1}=s_{2}(C_{1}+2bha+s_{3}3b^{2}a),\quad g_{2}=s_{2}(c-2(h-s_{3}3b)a),\quad g_{3}=s_{2}s_{3}3a.$ (5.16) It will also be useful to put $\omega=\varpi-\tfrac{1}{4}c^{2}$ (5.17) in terms of a modified frequency parameter $\varpi$ .
Substitution of the re-parameterized polynomial expression ( 5.1 ) and ( 5.16 )–( 5.17 )
into the ODE ( 4.33 ) yields a polynomial equation in $U$ with powers $1$ , $2$ , $3$ , $4$ , $5$ , $p$ , $p+1$ , $p+2$ , $p+3$ , $p+4$ , $2p$ , $2p+1$ , $2p+2$ , $2p+3$ ,
and with coefficients that involve the constants $C_{1},\tilde{C}_{3},E$ (cf ( 5.4 )),
the solitary wave parameters $b,h,a$ ,
the physical constants $p,k,\varpi,c,\mu$ ,
and the signs $s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}$ ,
which are subject to the inequalities $p\neq 0$ , $k\neq 0$ , $c\neq 0$ , $h\neq 0$ , $a\neq 0$ as well as the sign conditions ( 2.10 ) and ( 5.8 ). To solve the resulting polynomial equation,
it must be split with respect to all distinct powers of $U$ .
Since $p$ is an unknown,
case distinctions arise when any two powers with at least one involving $p$ are equal.
In each such case,
the splitting gives an overdetermined algebraic system for the unknowns $C_{1},\tilde{C}_{3},E,p,k,b,h,a,c,\varpi,\mu$ , and $s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}$ .
All of the case distinctions and subsequent splitting and solving of the algebraic system
have been carried out by using the computer algebra package Crack [ 24 ] .
Further computational remarks are summarized in Appendix A . This computational analysis shows that solutions exist only for $p=1,2,3,4$ .
For each case,
explicit expressions for $g_{1}$ , $g_{2}$ , $g_{3}$ , $E$ ,
and the determined parameters among $b,h,a,\mu,\varpi,c,k$ and also the signs $s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}$ ,
are given in Appendix B . Simplification of the solutions
followed by analysis of the conditions ( 5.14 ) and ( 5.15 )
has been done in Maple.
This step is highly non-trivial and relies heavily on the use of
the parameterization in terms of $b,h,a,c,\varpi,\mu$ .
A key aspect is finding an optimal subset of these parameters for each solution
such that, firstly, there are no unsolved algebraic equations,
and secondly, conditions ( 5.14 ) and ( 5.15 )
reduce to a simple form characterized by
a minimal set of inequalities holding on the free parameters.
In some cases, a case splitting arises from this reduction,
through the two subcases $\mu\neq 0$ and $\mu=0$ in the conditions ( 5.14 ),
since the solution of the inequalities can have disconnected components when $\mu=0$ .
The best outcome turns out to be achieved by
giving a lower priority for $b$ to be solved for among the set of parameters
rather than assuming that $c$ should be an arbitrary free parameter.
This analysis is shown in Appendix B . A classification of the resulting set of solution families is summarized in Table 1 .

the limitation of considering only a polynomial form ( 5.1 ) for $G(U)$ .
It should be possible to extend the computational analysis to
a rational form for $G(U)$ ,
which may yield additional solutions, possibly involving higher powers for $p$ .

Phase-modulated solitary waves From the results in section 5 ,
the explicit solitary wave solutions ( 4.35 ) of the gKdV-LS system ( 2.8 )–( 2.9 )
will now be presented and their kinematic features will be discussed.
These solutions hold for the nonlinearity powers $p=1,2,3,4$ ,
covering all physically interesting cases for the nonlinearity in the gKdV-LS system. The gKdV variable $u=U(\xi)$ is given by the sech-squared expression ( 5.6 ).
Physically, it describes a solitary wave on a background $b$ ,
with a height/depth $h>0$ ,
where the wave is bright when $s_{3}>0$ and dark when $s_{3}<0$ .
Its width is proportional to $w=2/\sqrt{a}$ in terms of the parameter $a>0$ . The LS variable $\psi=A(\xi)\exp(i(\omega t+\Phi(\xi))$ is given by
the amplitude expression ( 5.9 ) and the phase expression ( 5.10 ).
These expressions exhibit a wide variety of features. The amplitude $A(\xi)$ has the form of a solitary wave
which is the square-root of a linear combination of $U(\xi)$ , $U(\xi)^{2}$ , $U(\xi)^{p+1}$ terms plus a constant term.
Note that the resulting profile for $A(\xi)$ can contain up to $2p+1$ local peaks
on a tail (background) $A_{\infty}=A(\pm\infty)$ ,
with the central peak at $\xi=0$ having the amplitude $A_{0}=A(0)$ ,
as given by expressions ( 5.13 ).
This central peak will be bright/dark if $A^{\prime\prime}(0)\gtrless 0$ respectively,
while the tail will be called bright/dark if $A_{\infty}\gtrless A_{0}$ .
These features are illustrated in Fig. 3 . Figure 3. Bright/dark peaks and tails for $A(\xi)$ Side peaks occur in symmetrical pairs.
Their location and height can be found straightforwardly by considering $B(z)=A\big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{a}}{\rm arccosh}(1/\sqrt{z})\big)^{2}=g_{1}+g_{2}(b+s_{3}hz)+g_{3}(b+s_{3}hz)^{2}-s_{1}s_{2}\tfrac{1}{p+1}(b+s_{3}hz)^{p+1},$ (6.1) which is polynomial of degree $p+1$ in $z={\rm sech}(\sqrt{a}\,\xi)^{2}$ with $0<z\leq 1$ .
In this interval, each non-zero root $z=z^{*}$ of $B^{\prime}(z)=s_{3}h(g_{2}+2g_{3}(b+s_{3}hz)-s_{1}s_{2}(b+s_{3}hz)^{p})=0$ (6.2) determines that a pair of side peaks exist at $\xi^{*}=\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{a}}{\rm arccosh}(1/\sqrt{z^{*}})\big)^{2}$ .
These peaks will be bright/dark if $H^{\prime\prime}(z^{*})\gtrless 0$ respectively.
The case of one pair of side peaks is illustrated in Fig. 3 . The phase $\Phi(\xi)$ is given by a linear term in $\xi$ plus a quadrature term that is odd in $\xi$ with a coefficient $\pm\sqrt{\mu}$ .
In the case when this coefficient vanishes, the phase is thereby linear in $\xi$ $\Phi(\xi)=\frac{c}{2}\xi+\phi,\quad\text{ if }\mu=0.$ (6.3) In the general case, $\mu>0$ ,
the quadrature term in the phase can be expressed more conveniently
as the integral of a rational function, based on the identities ${\rm sech}^{2}({\rm arctanh}(\zeta))=1-\zeta^{2}$ and ${\rm arctanh}^{\prime}(\zeta)=1/(1-\zeta^{2})$ : $\int_{0}^{\xi}\frac{d\xi}{A(\xi)^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\int_{0}^{\tanh(\sqrt{a}\,\xi)}\frac{d\zeta}{(1-\zeta^{2})B(1-\zeta^{2})},$ (6.4) where the degree of $B(1-\zeta^{2})$ as a polynomial in $\zeta^{2}$ is $p+1$ .
For $\xi\to\pm\infty$ , this quadrature has the asymptotic behaviour $\int_{0}^{\xi}\frac{d\xi}{A(\xi)^{2}}\sim\frac{1}{A_{\infty}^{2}}\,\xi+{\rm sgn}(\xi)\int_{0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{A(\xi)^{2}}-\frac{1}{A_{\infty}^{2}}\Big)d\xi,$ (6.5) where $A_{\infty}\neq 0$ due to condition ( 5.14 ).
This motivates a decomposition of $\Phi(\xi)$ into linear and nonlinear terms: $\Phi(\xi)=\Phi_{\text{lin.}}(\xi)+\Phi_{\text{nonlin.}}(\xi)+\phi\quad\text{ if }\mu>0,$ (6.6) where $\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{lin.}}(\xi)=$ $\displaystyle\Big(\pm\frac{\sqrt{\mu}}{A_{\infty}^{2}}+\frac{c}{2}\Big)\xi,$ (6.7) $\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{nonlin.}}(\xi)=$ $\displaystyle\frac{\pm\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{a}}\int_{0}^{\tanh(\sqrt{a}\,\xi)}\Big(\frac{1}{B(1-\zeta^{2})}-\frac{1}{A_{\infty}^{2}}\Big)\frac{d\zeta}{(1-\zeta^{2})}.$ Evaluation of $\Phi_{\text{nonlin.}}(\xi)$ will yield a linear combination of
arctan and arctanh (or ln) functions in terms of $\zeta=\tanh(\sqrt{a}\,\xi)$ .
Note that its graph will be kink shaped, since $\Phi_{\text{nonlin.}}(\infty)=-\Phi_{\text{nonlin.}}(-\infty)$ and $\Phi_{\text{nonlin.}}(0)=0$ .
The sign of the kink is determined by the slope of $\Phi_{\text{nonlin.}}(\xi)$ at $\xi=0$ : ${\rm sgn}(\Phi_{\text{nonlin.}}(\infty))={\rm sgn}(\Phi_{\text{nonlin.}}^{\prime}(0))=\pm{\rm sgn}(A_{\infty}-A_{0})$ (6.8) using condition ( 5.14 ),
where “ $\pm$ ” refers to the sign in expressions ( 6.7 ).
Consequently, the asymptotic behaviour of the phase is $\Phi(\xi)\sim\phi+\Phi_{\text{lin.}}(\xi)\pm\Phi_{\text{nonlin.}}(\infty)\text{ as }\xi\to\pm\infty$ (6.9) in terms of the asymptote $\Phi_{\text{nonlin.}}(\infty)\neq 0$ of the kink. All of these features,

Conserved integrals All of the solitary wave solutions ( 4.35 )
with expressions ( 5.6 ), ( 5.9 ), ( 5.10 )
have a total mass, charge, momentum, and energy
given respectively by the four conserved integrals ( 4.5 )–( 4.8 ).
The latter two integrals ( 4.7 )–( 4.8 ) can be simplified
by use of the ODEs ( 4.23 ) for $U(\xi)$ and ( 4.24 ) for $\Phi(z)$ . Each integral will be finite if $u$ has a zero background and $|\psi|$ has a zero tail,
namely $b=0$ and $g_{1}=0$ ,
since then the density goes to $0$ exponentially in $|\xi|$ . If a density otherwise has a non-zero asymptotic tail as $|\xi|\to\infty$ ,
the conserved integral will diverge.
Nevertheless, the divergent contribution can be removed by subtraction of
the asymptotic tail in the density, yielding a regularized integral
which is conserved and finite. The regularized integrals for mass, charge, momentum, and energy are given by $\displaystyle\tilde{M}[U]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(U-b)\,d\xi,$ (6.10) $\displaystyle\tilde{J}[A]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tfrac{1}{2}(A^{2}-A_{0}^{2})\,d\xi,$ (6.11) $\displaystyle\tilde{P}[U,A,\Phi]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}(U^{2}-b^{2})-s_{2}\tfrac{c}{2k}(A^{2}-A_{0}^{2})\big)\,d\xi,$ (6.12) $\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \tilde{H}[U,A,\Phi]=&\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}U^{\prime}{}^{2}+s_{2}\tfrac{1}{k}A^{\prime}{}^{2}-s_{1}\tfrac{1}{(p+1)(p+2)}(U^{p+2}-b^{p+2})\\
&\qquad+s_{2}\tfrac{c^{2}}{4k}(A^{2}-A_{0}^{2})-s_{2}(UA^{2}-bA_{0}^{2})\big)\,d\xi\\
=&\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}(U^{2}-b^{2})+C_{1}(U-b)-s_{1}\tfrac{2}{(p+1)(p+2)}(U^{p+2}-b^{p+2})\\
&\qquad-s_{2}\tfrac{\omega}{k}(A^{2}-A_{0}^{2})-s_{2}2(UA^{2}-bA_{0}^{2})\big)\,d\xi,\end{aligned}$ (6.13) which are finite for all solitary wave solutions ( 5.6 ), ( 5.9 ), ( 5.10 ). It is straightforward to evaluate the mass integral ( 6.10 ): $\tilde{M}[U]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}s_{3}h\,{\rm sech}^{2}(\sqrt{a}\,\xi)d\xi=s_{3}\frac{2h}{\sqrt{a}}.$ (6.14) This is the same general expression as the mass of the mKdV solitary wave
and depends on $p$ only implicitly through $h$ when it is evaluated for
a specific solution family. It is similarly straightforward to evaluate the charge integral ( 6.11 )
for each power $p=1,2,3,4$ ,
where the density is given by $\frac{1}{2}g_{3}h^{2}\,{\rm sech}^{4}(\sqrt{a}\,\xi)+s_{3}\frac{1}{2}(2bg_{3}+g_{2})h\,{\rm sech}^{2}(\sqrt{a}\,\xi)+s_{1}s_{2}\frac{1}{2(p+1)}\big(b^{p+1}-\big(s_{3}h\,{\rm sech}^{2}(\sqrt{a}\,\xi)+b\big)^{p+1}\big)$ .
Alternatively, a single expression that holds for all $p$ can be obtained
by applying the following integration steps to the third term in the density.
Make a binomial expansion of this term
and change the integration variable via ${\rm sech}(\sqrt{a}\,\xi)=2y/(1+y^{2})$ .
Since the density is even in $\xi$ , the charge integral can be expressed as
twice the expanded density integrated over $y$ from $0$ to $1$ .
Each term is given by an associated Legendre function and the sum of all the terms
can be shown to yield a generalized hypergeometric function ${}_{3}F_{2}$ [ 29 ] .
When combined with the integral of the first and second terms in the density,

under perturbations.
For a parametric family of solutions, a stronger notion is orbital stability.
Both notions are related to convexity of the integral quantity ( 4.9 )
in the variational principle ( 4.10 ) for the ODE system ( 4.4 )
that determines solutions.
In the case of solutions that have a non-zero background,
a regularized version of this integral quantity can be used, $\widetilde{W}[U,A,\Phi]=\tilde{H}[U,A,\Phi]+c\tilde{P}[U,A,\Phi]-s_{2}(2\omega/k)\tilde{J}[A]+C_{1}\tilde{M}[U],$ (6.18) where the conserved integrals ( 4.5 )–( 4.8 ) are replaced by
their regularized counterparts ( 6.10 )–( 6.13 ),
which does not change the Euler-Lagrange ODEs. Convexity is determined by the eigenvalues of the Hessian operator of
the regularized integral quantity ( 6.18 ).
After reverting from amplitude and phase variables to the LS variable ( 4.3 ),
the Hessian is given by matrix operator $\displaystyle\partial^{2}\widetilde{W}[U,\Psi,\bar{\Psi};p]=$ (6.19) $\displaystyle-\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}-c+s_{1}U^{p}&s_{2}\bar{\Psi}&s_{2}\Psi\\
s_{2}\Psi&(s_{2}/k)\Big(\dfrac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}-ic\dfrac{d}{d\xi}+\omega\Big)+s_{2}U&0\\
s_{2}\bar{\Psi}&0&(s_{2}/k)\Big(\dfrac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}+ic\dfrac{d}{d\xi}+\omega\Big)+s_{2}U\end{pmatrix}.$ This operator is formally self-adjoint with respect to a joint $L^{2}$ inner product for the $U$ -component
and a hermitian $L^{2}$ inner product for the $\Psi,\bar{\Psi}$ -components.
It can be brought to a diagonal form by the change of variables $\Psi=s_{2}(k/2)e^{i\frac{c}{2}\xi}(\Theta_{1}+i\Theta_{2}),\quad\bar{\Theta}_{1}=\Theta_{1},\quad\bar{\Theta}_{2}=\Theta_{2},$ (6.20) yielding ${\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{I}}\dfrac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}+{\mathcal{V}}$ (6.21) with ${\mathcal{I}}={\rm diag}\big(1,s_{2}k/2,s_{2}k/2\big),\quad{\mathcal{V}}=\begin{pmatrix}-c+s_{1}U^{p}&s_{2}(k^{2}/2)\Theta_{1}&s_{2}(k^{2}/2)\Theta_{2}\\
s_{2}(k^{2}/2)\Theta_{1}&s_{2}(k/2)(\varpi+kU)&0\\
s_{2}(k^{2}/2)\Theta_{2}&0&s_{2}(k/2)(\varpi+kU)\end{pmatrix},$ (6.22) where $\varpi$ is expression ( 5.17 ).
This is a linear self-adjoint matrix operator with respect to a real $L^{2}$ inner product
for the real components $(U,\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})$ . From Proposition 4 ,
there are three infinitesimal symmetries ( 4.11 )–( 4.12 )
of the Euler-Lagrange ODEs ( 4.4 ).
A standard argument shows that the translation and phase rotation symmetries
will each give rise to a zero eigenvalue of the operator ( 6.21 ).
The key question for determining stability is whether
this operator has a negative eigenvalue [ 30 ] .
This will be addressed in subsequent work. 7. Concluding remarks The new method developed in the present work
has yielded 22 families of exact solitary wave solutions ( 1.3 )
for the coupled nonlinear gKdV-LS system ( 1.1 )–( 1.2 )
with nonlinearity powers $p=1,2,3,4$ .
No solutions for $p>1$ were known previously. For $p=1$ ,
four of the solution families contain the solitary waves that were first found
in Ref. [ 7 , 13 ] .
The former one arose in the context of nonlinear plasma waves
and coincides with the first of the two families 1-i.1 (i.e. dark LS peak).
The latter ones come from nonlinear waves in anharmonic lattices
and were named bright/dark Davydov solitons of the 1st and 2nd kind:
they are respectively given by
the case $s_{2}=-1$ of families 1-ii with $\mu\neq 0$ and 1-ii.1;
and the case $k=1/6$ of family 1-i with $\mu=0$ and $c<c_{\text{crit.}}$ and the case $k=1/6$ of the second family 1-i.1 (i.e. bright LS peak).
These particular solitary waves are characterized by respective relations $|\psi|^{2}\propto$ $(u-b)(u-u_{0})$ , $u-b$ , $(u-u_{0})^{2}$ ,
for some constant $u_{0}$ ,
which allows them to be derived by basic ODE methods.
The corresponding relations for the travelling wave ODE system ( 1.4 )
have the form $A^{2}\propto$ $(U-b)(U-U_{0})$ , $U-b$ , $(U-U_{0})^{2}$ in terms of the variables ( 4.3 ). The additional five solution families for $p=1$ describe new solitary waves.
Physical properties and applications,
as well as stability analysis based on the variational principle ( 4.10 ),
for all of the new solution families for $p=1,2,3,4$ will be discussed elsewhere. The main results obtained here illustrate the power of
modern symmetry analysis methods combined with advanced symbolic computation packages
for the study of exact solitary wave solutions of coupled nonlinear ODE systems. As further applications,
one direction would be a generalization of the gKdV-LS system to include
a self-interaction $\nu|\psi|^{q}\psi$ in equation ( 1.2 ) for the Schrodinger field $\psi$ ,
where $q>0$ is a nonlinearity power.
Only a few exact solutions are known for this nonlinear coupled system [ 31 ] .
Another direction would be to consider a coupled Boussinesq-LS system [ 32 ] and its $p$ -power generalizations,
for which exact solitary wave solutions are of physical interest. A more general direction would be to extend the present method to the study of
nonlinearly generalized Hénon-Heiles ODE systems,

the computation involves solving a polynomial equation obtained by
substitution of the general polynomial ansatz ( 5.1 )
into the ODE ( 4.33 ) for $G(U)$ .
This polynomial equation has 436 terms,
containing 16 powers $0,1,2,3,4,5,p,p+1,p+2,p+3,p+4,2p,2p+1,2p+2,2p+3$ .
There are 14 unknowns, consisting of $p$ , $k$ , $b$ , $h$ , $a$ , $c$ , $\varpi$ , $\mu$ , $C_{1}$ , $\tilde{C}_{3}$ , $E$ ,
and $s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}$ ,
which are required to obey 8 conditions, $p\neq 0,-1$ , $k\neq 0$ , $h\neq 0$ , $a\neq 0$ , $c\neq 0$ ,
and $s_{1}{}^{2}=s_{2}{}^{2}=s_{3}{}^{2}=1$ .
These conditions together with the polynomial equation
constitute the algebraic system to be solved.
This is a non-polynomial algebraic problem, because $p$ appears in exponents
and because products of the unknowns (including $p$ ) appear in the coefficients of some terms. The computer algebra package Crack [ 24 ] , written in REDUCE,
is able to solve nonlinear problems (algebraic as well as differential).
Specifically, it is able to split equations with respect to independent variables, including when a variable appears as an exponent.
It is also able to make case distinctions as needed.
In an automatic run, all splitting with respect to the independent variables
has a high priority. The system here has a single independent variable, $U$ .
Crack splits the system with respect to $U$ if all exponents are known to be different.
Since $p$ is an unknown that appears in exponents,
Crack proceeds by systematically taking the difference between two exponents,
with at least one of the exponents containing $p$ ,
and starting a case distinction in which the pivot is given by the expression
for the difference being zero or non-zero.
In the non-zero case, this inequality is simply appended to the system.
In the zero case,
Crack determines $p$ and then substitutes it back into the system.
If the resulting system either has all exponents of $U$ being distinct,
or has no exponents of $U$ involving $p$ ,
then it can be split into a system of algebraic equations that involve only the unknowns.
Crack is able to solve these equations straightforwardly.
If an equation factorizes,
then a further case distinction is made with respect to each factor.
This process is repeated until all possible case distinctions have been considered,
and the system has been either fully solved or reduced to irreducible equations
such as high degree polynomials in the unknowns.
In the system under consideration,
every case leads to an algebraic system that is explicitly solved
in the sense that its solution is given by explicit expressions
together with at most low-degree polynomials whose solution set is non-empty. The final result is 56 solutions that can be merged into non-overlapping families
