We consider cross-diffusion systems describing evolution of two species $u$ and $v$ moving according to Darcy’s law with the pressure law $p(s)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}s^{\alpha-1}$ where $s=u+v$ . One of the most challenging questions in the field is the construction of solutions to the problem in the presence of additional advection fields, without imposing any artificial structure on the fields or the initial conditions. Although advection arises naturally in these models, it breaks the symmetry of the system and prevents application of techniques developed in recent years. Here, we provide a new approach to construct solutions in one space dimension that works in a unified manner for all pressure exponents $\alpha\in(0,\infty)$ and for arbitrary initial data. In particular, in the regime $\alpha>1$ , this yields the first existence result of its kind, obtained without any structural assumptions. We construct the solutions as a limit of a vanishing viscosity approximation $(u_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon})$ . The main challenge is to identify the limit of $u_{\varepsilon}\,\partial_{x}p(s_{\varepsilon})$ , where $s_{\varepsilon}=u_{\varepsilon}+v_{\varepsilon}$ . The key new insight is that possible oscillations of $u_{\varepsilon}$ and $\partial_{x}p(s_{\varepsilon})$ are correlated, simplifying the Young measure analysis in the compensated compactness argument and allowing identification of the limit. Somewhat surprisingly, in contrast to the theory of $2\times 2$ hyperbolic systems, the argument relies on only three entropy-entropy flux pairs. This is particularly useful for $\alpha>2$ , where it is unclear whether additional entropies are available. Key words and phrases: cross-diffusion systems, global weak solutions,

Introduction Cross-diffusion systems have attracted considerable attention in recent years due to their analytical challenges [ 25 , 40 , 20 , 6 , 35 ] and their applications in the applied sciences [ 16 , 30 , 44 ] . These partial differential equations, first formulated in the 1970s and 1980s [ 34 , 8 , 53 , 9 ] , still present many challenging and open mathematical problems. The aim of the present paper is to address one of them, namely, the existence of weak solutions to the following cross-diffusion system: $\begin{split}\partial_{t}u&=\partial_{x}(u\,\partial_{x}p(s))+\partial_{x}(u\,\partial_{x}(V^{1}+K^{1,1}\ast u+K^{1,2}\ast v)),\\
\partial_{t}v&=\partial_{x}(v\,\partial_{x}p(s))+\partial_{x}(v\,\partial_{x}(V^{2}+K^{2,1}\ast u+K^{2,2}\ast v)),\end{split}$ (1.1) describing the evolution of two species on $\mathbb{R}$ having nonnegative densities $u$ , $v$ , where $s=u+v.$ Writing these equations as continuity equations $\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}(u\,\mathcal{W}^{1})=0$ , $\partial_{t}v+\partial_{x}(v\,\mathcal{W}^{2})=0$ , we recognize that the velocities $\mathcal{W}^{1}$ and $\mathcal{W}^{2}$ are given by $\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{1}$ $\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}p(s)-\partial_{x}(V^{1}+K^{1,1}\ast u+K^{1,2}\ast v),$ $\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{2}$ $\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}p(s)-\partial_{x}(V^{2}+K^{2,1}\ast u+K^{2,2}\ast v).$ The first term corresponds to Darcy’s law [ 22 ] with the pressure $p(s)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\alpha-1}s^{\alpha-1}&\mbox{ if }\alpha\neq 1,\\
\log s&\mbox{ if }\alpha=1,\end{cases}\qquad\qquad\mbox{ for }\alpha\in(0,\infty),$ (1.2) and it models the fact that species avoid overcrowded regions. The case $\alpha>1$ seems to be more relevant from the point of view of applications as the total density $s$ is expected to move with finite speed of propagation by an analogy to the porous medium equation [ 59 , Chap. 14] . On the other hand, for $\alpha\leq 1$ one expects that $s$ is immediately globally supported as for heat equation or fast-diffusion equation, see [ 36 , Theorem 2.4] and [ 58 ] . Moreover, the so-called incompressible limit $\alpha\to\infty$ shows that these models are related to the free boundary models of tissue growth for large $\alpha$ [ 47 , 33 ] . The additional terms with functions $V^{i}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ , $K^{i,j}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ are responsible for an independent advection and nonlocal interaction between the densities. We recall that $K^{i,j}~\ast~u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K^{i,j}(x-y)\,u(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y$ and analogously for $K^{i,j}\ast v$ . The inclusion of these effects in the models is classical: nonlocal interaction terms account for repulsion and attraction between species [ 3 , 57 , 43 , 30 ] , while advection captures spatial heterogeneity. We do not assume any particular structure of these functions except some mild regularity listed in Assumption 1.1 . It will be convenient to define $\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{1}[u,v]$ $\displaystyle=V^{1}+K^{1,1}\ast u+K^{1,2}\ast v,$ $\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{2}[u,v]$ $\displaystyle=V^{2}+K^{2,1}\ast u+K^{2,2}\ast v,$ so that the system ( 1.1 ) can be written as $\begin{split}\partial_{t}u&=\partial_{x}(u\,\partial_{x}p(s))+\partial_{x}(u\,\partial_{x}\mathcal{V}^{1}[u,v]),\\
\partial_{t}v&=\partial_{x}(v\,\partial_{x}p(s))+\partial_{x}(v\,\partial_{x}\mathcal{V}^{2}[u,v]).\end{split}$ (1.3) If one would like to think about something simpler, the system $\displaystyle\partial_{t}u$ $\displaystyle=\partial_{x}(u\,\partial_{x}(u+v))-\partial_{x}u,$ $\displaystyle\partial_{t}v$ $\displaystyle=\partial_{x}(v\,\partial_{x}(u+v))+\partial_{x}v,$ i.e. $p(s)=s$ , $\alpha=2$ , $K^{i,j}=0$ , $V^{1}(x)=-x$ and $V^{2}(x)=x$ , is covered by the general theory developed in this paper and already presents several of the main difficulties that we need to address (though not all of them, since the case $\alpha>2$ is more involved; see the proof of Proposition 5.2 for $\alpha\leq 2$ in Section 5.4 and Section 5.2 for the treatment of arbitrary $\alpha$ ). The target of this work is to construct weak solutions without imposing any structural conditions on $V^{i}$ , $K^{i,j}$ or conditions guaranteeing that $u$ , $v$ or $u+v$ are strictly positive. In particular, we do not make any assumption on the support of the initial conditions, the pressure law exponent and velocity fields (except mild regularity assumptions which could be probably improved). We also do not include the self-diffusion terms $\partial^{2}_{x}u$ , $\partial^{2}_{x}v$ in ( 1.3 ). The standard approach to construct weak solutions is to consider some regularization of ( 1.3 ), say by adding an artificial viscosity term $\begin{split}\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}&=\partial_{x}(u_{\varepsilon}\,\partial_{x}p(s_{\varepsilon}))+\partial_{x}(u_{\varepsilon}\,\partial_{x}\mathcal{V}^{1}[u_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}])+\varepsilon\,\partial^{2}_{x}u_{\varepsilon},\\
\partial_{t}v_{\varepsilon}&=\partial_{x}(v_{\varepsilon}\,\partial_{x}p(s_{\varepsilon}))+\partial_{x}(v_{\varepsilon}\,\partial_{x}\mathcal{V}^{2}[u_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}])+\varepsilon\,\partial^{2}_{x}v_{\varepsilon},\end{split}$ (1.4) with $s_{\varepsilon}=u_{\varepsilon}+v_{\varepsilon}$ and study the limit $\varepsilon\to 0$ . The difficult terms are nonlinear ones, namely $u_{\varepsilon}\,\partial_{x}p(s_{\varepsilon})$ , $v_{\varepsilon}\,\partial_{x}p(s_{\varepsilon})$ . Without advection (i.e. $\mathcal{V}^{1}=\mathcal{V}^{2}=0$ ) the system has attracted a large group of researchers and the understanding is now quite complete in arbitrary number of dimensions. In particular, there are several techniques to construct weak solutions: Aronson-Benilan estimates [ 4 ] can be used to obtain a uniform bound on $\partial^{2}_{x}p(s_{\varepsilon})$ [ 35 ] , BV estimates on $\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}}$ , $\frac{v_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}}$ coming from a transport equation satisfied by these quantities lead to strong compactness of $u_{\varepsilon}$ and $v_{\varepsilon}$ in one dimension (probably this argument was used in [ 7 ] for the first time but see [ 11 , 12 ] as well) while direct energy considerations show that $\{\partial_{x}p(s_{\varepsilon})\}$ is strongly compact in $L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})$ for $\alpha\geq 1$ [ 23 , 50 , 28 ] . In contrast, for a single equation rather than a system, the problem with advection is well understood [ 24 ] . The additional advection in ( 1.3 ) prevents application of any of these arguments and new ideas are needed. To the best of our knowledge, the following are the only results available in the literature for ( 1.3 ). All of them hold only in one space dimension. • [ 37 ] constructs weak solutions to ( 1.3 ) with $\alpha>1$ without nonlocal interaction $K^{i,j}=0$ . The initial conditions are initially separated and the Authors impose an inequality between the velocities $\partial_{x}V^{1}$ and $\partial_{x}V^{2}$ , which prevents the two populations from mixing. • [ 41 ] constructs for the first time weak solutions to ( 1.3 ) with $\alpha=1$ allowing arbitrary velocity field (nonlocal interactions are not explicitly mentioned there but it does not seem a difficult issue to include them in the analysis). The main drawback of the work is the assumption on the initial conditions $\log(u^{0}/v^{0})\in BV(\mathbb{T})$ (so both $u^{0}$ and $v^{0}$ have to be supported on the same set). The main observation of [ 41 ] is that $f=\log(u/v)$ solves a transport equation so the BV regularity of $f$ is propagated in time. This gives strong convergence of $u$ and $v$ which is sufficient to conclude the proof of existence of solutions. • [ 27 ] extends the work of [ 41 ] to the fast-diffusion case $\alpha\in(0,1)$ under similar assumptions on the initial conditions. • Shortly before the submission of this preprint, the Authors of [ 41 ] posted a new work [ 42 ] in which the assumption $\log(u^{0}/v^{0})\in BV(\mathbb{T})$ is removed, thereby allowing for general initial configurations in the regime $\alpha\leq 1$ , which is a significant extension. We remark that all these works cannot deal with the case $\alpha>1$ where the populations evolve with finite speed of propagation which is expected in mathematical biology as explained above. On the other hand, the work presented here covers the whole regime $\alpha\in(0,\infty)$ and all initial configurations by a completely different method than the one of [ 42 , 27 , 41 ] . In the present work we do not address the problem of obtaining uniform BV estimates for ${u_{\varepsilon}}$ and ${v_{\varepsilon}}$ , which appears to be a very challenging and worthwhile problem in its own right. Instead, we prove directly that the nonlinear terms $u_{\varepsilon}\,\partial_{x}p(s_{\varepsilon})$ and $v_{\varepsilon}\,\partial_{x}p(s_{\varepsilon})$ converge weakly to $u\,\partial_{x}p(s)$ and $v\,\partial_{x}p(s)$ , respectively; that is, we identify their weak limits. We use the approach of compensated compactness developed by Tartar and Murat in the context of hyperbolic conservation laws [ 45 , 56 ] . This is formulated in the language of Young measures (introduced in Section 4.1 ) which encode weak limits of nonlinear functions. The general idea is to derive additional PDEs satisfied by nonlinear functions of the solution, the so-called entropy-entropy flux pairs. Applying the Div-Curl Lemma (see Section 4.2 ) then yields constraints on the Young measure which, provided sufficiently many such pairs are available, allow one to deduce convergence. We remark that the approach has been successfully applied (among many others) to hyperbolic conservation laws [ 26 , 38 , 39 ] , mixed-type elliptic-hyperbolic systems [ 19 ] , fast-reaction limits in reaction-diffusion systems [ 48 , 54 ] , and regularization of forward-backward diffusion problems [ 49 , 54 ] . Last but not least, we remark that Young measures are not only used to identify the limit, but also to define the concept of solutions (measure-valued solutions), where the limit cannot be identified [ 18 , 10 , 31 ] . In contrast to the aforementioned works, the present paper requires significant new developments in how the compensated compactness framework is exploited. The Young measure considered here captures oscillations of two sequences, ${u_{\varepsilon}/s_{\varepsilon}}$ and ${\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}}$ , which do not converge strongly and for which there is no simple relationship (for instance, via the divergence). Therefore, the system under consideration should be compared with $2\times 2$ hyperbolic conservation laws [ 26 , 38 , 39 ] , where the argument typically relies on countably many entropy-entropy flux pairs.
In our setting, such a family exists (Remark 3.9 ), but only for $\alpha\leq 2$ (Remark 3.8 ), which excludes many cases of interest; in particular, large values of $\alpha$ are important in tumor-growth models [ 47 , 33 ] . Consequently, we carry out a careful analysis of the identities obtained via compensated compactness in order to complete the argument. A more detailed sketch of the proof is provided in Section 2 . We will make the following assumptions in all results throughout the paper. They are certainly not optimal. For instance, one could easily allow for time-dependent $V^{i}$ and $K^{i,j}$ by assuming additional time regularity. Depending on $\alpha$ , one could also relax some assumptions on the kernels $K^{i,j}$ by using additional integrability information on the solution, see e.g. [ 13 ] for $\alpha=1$ . Assumption 1.1 . We assume that: • $\partial_{x}V^{i}$ , $\partial_{x}K^{i,j}$ , $\partial^{2}_{x}V^{i}$ , $\partial^{2}_{x}K^{i,j}$ are in $L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})$ , • the initial conditions $u^{0},v^{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})$ and $u^{0}\,|x|,v^{0}\,|x|\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})$ . The weak solutions to ( 1.3 ) that we construct are defined as follows. We interpret the terms $u\,\partial_{x}p(s)$ , $v\,\partial_{x}p(s)$ as $u\,\partial_{x}p(s)=u\,p^{\prime}(s)\,\partial_{x}s=u\,s^{\alpha-2}\,\partial_{x}s=\frac{u}{s}\,s^{\alpha-1}\,\partial_{x}s=\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\frac{u}{s}\,\partial_{x}s^{\alpha},$ (1.5) since in our analysis it is useful to deal with derivatives of terms which are not singular (this is helpful mostly in the proof of Proposition 3.10 ). Above, we always define $\frac{u}{s}=0$ on the set $\{s=0\}$ . In fact, this does not matter because we show that on the set $\{s=0\}$ we have in fact $\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}=0$ cf. (C6) in Proposition 3.4 . Definition 1.2 . We say that a pair $u,v\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}))$ with $s:=u+v$ is a weak solution to ( 1.3 ) with initial conditions $u^{0},v^{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})$ if $\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ and for all $\varphi,\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ we have $\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u\,\partial_{t}\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{0}(x)\,\varphi(0,x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\frac{u}{s}\,\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}+u\,\partial_{x}\mathcal{V}^{1}[u,v]\right)\,\partial_{x}\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,$ $\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}v\,\partial_{t}\phi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\mathbb{R}}v^{0}(x)\,\phi(0,x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\frac{v}{s}\,\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}+v\,\partial_{x}\mathcal{V}^{2}[u,v]\right)\,\partial_{x}\phi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.$ The main result of the paper reads: Theorem 1.3 . Under Assumption 1.1 , there exists a weak solution to ( 1.3 ) in the sense of Definition 1.2 which satisfies $u,v\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}))$ for all $p\in[1,\infty]$ , $\partial_{x}s^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ and $s\,|x|\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}))$ . It is obtained as a limit of solutions to ( 1.4 ). More precisely, there exists a subsequence (not relabelled) such that • $u_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup u$ , $v_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup v$ weakly/weakly ∗ in $L^{p}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ for all $p\in[1,\infty]$ , • $s_{\varepsilon}\to s$ a.e. and strongly in $L^{p}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ for all $p\in[1,\infty)$ , • $\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{{\alpha}}\to\partial_{x}s^{{\alpha}}$ strongly in $L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ , • $u_{\varepsilon}\to u$ , $v_{\varepsilon}\to v$ strongly in $L^{p}(\mathcal{S})$ for all $p\in[1,\infty)$ and a.e. on $\mathcal{S}$ , where the set $\mathcal{S}:=[0,T]~\times~\mathbb{R}\setminus\{(t,x):s(t,x)>0\mbox{ and }\partial_{x}\mathcal{V}^{1}[u,v]=\partial_{x}\mathcal{V}^{2}[u,v]\}$ . The same result can be proved on a periodic domain $\mathbb{T}$ . It is also not difficult to extend this result to systems with additional growth terms of the form $\begin{split}\partial_{t}u&=\partial_{x}(u\,\partial_{x}p(s))+\partial_{x}(u\,\partial_{x}\mathcal{V}^{1}[u,v])+u\,G^{1}(s),\\
\partial_{t}v&=\partial_{x}(v\,\partial_{x}p(s))+\partial_{x}(v\,\partial_{x}\mathcal{V}^{2}[u,v])+v\,G^{2}(s),\end{split}$ (1.6) where $G^{i}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}$ and $G^{i}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})$ . In tissue-growth processes, the additional source term acts to inhibit cell proliferation at high pressure levels [ 47 ] . We stress, however, that we cannot handle the case where $G$ depends on the individual species $u$ and $v$ , as our method does not yield strong compactness of $\{u_{\varepsilon}\}$ and $\{v_{\varepsilon}\}$ , unless the set $\mathcal{S}$ is the whole space $[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}$ (this is the case, for instance, when $\partial_{x}\mathcal{V}^{1}=-1$ and $\partial_{x}\mathcal{V}^{2}=1$ ). We briefly outline the necessary modifications required to extend Theorem 1.3 to equation ( 1.6 ) in Section 5.5 . Remark 1.4 . We may also consider Darcy’s law ( 1.2 ) with the pressure depending not on the total density $s=u+v$ , but instead on a weighted sum $\widetilde{s}=c_{u}\,u+c_{v}\,v$ . This formulation is motivated by the fact that pressure may reflect physical characteristics such as cell size. For instance, five cells of type $u$ may occupy the same volume as ten cells of type $v$ ; see [ 15 ] . This case can be reduced to the standard setting $c_{u}=c_{v}=1$ . Indeed, if $u,v$ solve the system where the pressure depends on the weighted sum $\widetilde{s}$ with kernels $\widetilde{K^{i,j}}$ and velocities $\widetilde{V^{i}}$ , then $c_{u}\,u,c_{v}\,v$ solve the original system ( 1.1 ) with kernels ${K^{i,1}}=\frac{1}{c_{u}}\widetilde{K^{i,1}},{K^{i,2}}=\frac{1}{c_{v}}\widetilde{K^{i,2}}$ (the velocity fields $\widetilde{V^{i}}$ do not change). Notation. We use standard notation for function spaces. For $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}$ and $1~\leq~p,q~\leq~\infty$ , we denote by $L^{p}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega))$ the usual Bochner-Lebesgue space. More generally, for an open set $U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$ , we write $L^{p}(U)$ and $W^{1,p}(U)$ for the Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces, with negative-order spaces $W^{-1,p}(U)$ defined as a dual space of $W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{0}(U)$ where $\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1$ ; as usual, $H^{k}(U)=W^{k,2}(U)$ . In particular, we shall use spaces on space–time domains such as $L^{p}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ or $H^{-1}((0,T)~\times~\mathbb{R})$ . If $1~<~p,q~<~\infty$ , bounded sequences in these spaces are weakly compact; if $p=\infty$ and/or $q=\infty$ , they are weakly ∗ compact. In the case $p=1$ or $q=1$ , boundedness is not sufficient and we use the Dunford–Pettis theorem [ 2 , Theorem 1.38] to obtain weak compactness. We denote by $\mathcal{D}^{\prime}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ the space of distributions and by $\mathcal{M}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})$ the space of bounded Radon measures, identified with the dual of $C^{0}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})$ (space of continuous functions vanishing at infinity). In this space, bounded sets are weakly ∗ compact. We write $L^{p}(0,T;L^{q}_{\loc}(\Omega))$ for functions belonging to $L^{p}(0,T;L^{q}(K))$ for every compact $K\subset\Omega$ . Local versions in time and/or space are defined analogously for Lebesgue, Sobolev (e.g. $H^{-1}_{\loc}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ ), and measure spaces (e.g. $\mathcal{M}_{{\loc}}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R})$ ). Weak and strong convergence in these local spaces are understood in the usual sense, i.e. convergence holds in the corresponding space restricted to every compact subset. Whenever the sequence depends on the parameter $\varepsilon$ , we always assume that $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ and write $\{u_{\varepsilon}\}$ , omitting the subscript (that is, we do not write $\{u_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}$ ). Many sequences will converge only weakly, so the limits of nonlinear expressions cannot, in general, be identified with the nonlinear function of the limit; to denote such weak limits, we use the overline notation. For example, if $u_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup u$ weakly, we write $\overline{f(u)}$ to denote the weak limit of $\{f(u_{\varepsilon})\}$ , which in general differs from $f(u)$ ; see Proposition 3.4 for more examples. Finally, if $E$ is a Banach space, $E^{*}$ denotes its dual and $C(0,T;E)$ is a Banach space of continuous functions from $[0,T]$ to $E$ . Structure of the paper. Sketch of the proof First of all, in Section 3.1 we obtain standard a priori estimates for solutions to ( 1.4 ). Namely, $\{u_{\varepsilon}\}$ and $\{v_{\varepsilon}\}$ are bounded in $L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}))$ for all $p\in[1,\infty]$ , while $\{\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}\}$ is bounded in $L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ . Then, in Section 3.2 , the Aubin-Lions lemma yields strong compactness of $\{s_{\varepsilon}\}$ in $L^{p}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ for all $p\in[1,\infty)$ . On the other hand, the Banach-Alaoglu theorem provides weak compactness of $\{u_{\varepsilon}\}$ , $\{v_{\varepsilon}\}$ , and $\{\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}\}$ in their respective spaces. The remaining difficulty is to pass to the limit in the term $\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}}\,\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}$ , which is the product of two sequences that converge only weakly. It seems quite challenging to obtain better estimates that would justify the convergence directly. Therefore, we will try to identify the limit without deriving stronger estimates. We consider the Young measure $\pi_{t,x}$ of the sequence $(\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}}-\frac{u}{s},\,\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}-\partial_{x}s^{\alpha})$ , see Section 4.1 for a rigorous explanation. For each $(t,x)$ , this is a measure on $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ , which is the set of possible values of the sequence. Roughly speaking, whenever $\{f(t,x,\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}}-\frac{u}{s},\,\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}-\partial_{x}s^{\alpha})\}$ is weakly compact in $L^{1}_{\loc}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ , we have the representation (up to a subsequence) $f\left(t,x,\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}}-\frac{u}{s},\,\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}-\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}\right)\rightharpoonup\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(t,x,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{t,x}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\mbox{ weakly in }L^{1}_{\loc}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}).$ (2.1) This type of difference appears naturally in studies of how much the sequences fail to converge strongly, see e.g. the defect measure in [ 29 ] . To motivate our choice of the sequence, we write $\displaystyle\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}}\,\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}-\frac{u}{s}\,\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}$ $\displaystyle=\left(\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}}-\frac{u}{s}\right)\,\left(\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}-\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}\right)+\,\left(\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}-\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}\right)\,\frac{u}{s}+\left(\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}}-\frac{u}{s}\right)\,\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}$ $\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lambda_{1}\,\lambda_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{t,x}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}),$ where the last two terms converge to zero by weak convergence (some care is needed if $s=0$ , but in that case we prove that $\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}=0$ , see (C6) in Proposition 3.4 ). Hence, the proof of existence is concluded once we show that $\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lambda_{1}\,\lambda_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{t,x}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=0$ for a.e. $(t,x)$ . In order to prove this identity, we will use the equations satisfied by $u_{\varepsilon}$ , $v_{\varepsilon}$ , and some nonlinear functions of them to obtain information on $\pi_{t,x}$ . Roughly speaking, the product of weakly converging sequences is usually not the weak limit of the product, but certain combinations of products may have this property. The theory of compensated compactness collects results in this spirit; see Section 4.2 for a more in-depth discussion. For instance, consider two continuity equations $\partial_{t}U_{\varepsilon}=\partial_{x}F_{\varepsilon}+R_{\varepsilon},\qquad\partial_{t}V_{\varepsilon}=\partial_{x}G_{\varepsilon}+S_{\varepsilon}\qquad\mbox{ on }(0,T)\times\mathbb{R},$ with $\{R_{\varepsilon}\}$ and $\{S_{\varepsilon}\}$ compact in $H^{-1}_{\loc}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ . Under some integrability assumptions, the celebrated Div-Curl Lemma implies that $F_{\varepsilon}\,V_{\varepsilon}-U_{\varepsilon}\,G_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\overline{F}\,\overline{V}-\overline{U}\,\overline{G}$ in the sense of distributions, where $\overline{F}$ , $\overline{V}$ , $\overline{U}$ , $\overline{G}$ are the weak limits in $L^{2}_{\loc}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ of $\{F_{\varepsilon}\}$ , $\{V_{\varepsilon}\}$ , $\{U_{\varepsilon}\}$ , $\{G_{\varepsilon}\}$ , respectively. Our strategy is to express all the weak limits in terms of the Young measure in order to obtain constraints on it. The key observation proved in Section 5.1 is that, at points where the integral does not vanish, the Young measure associated with the sequence is supported on at most a straight line passing through $(0,0)$ . Intuitively, this means that oscillations (preventing strong compactness) of $\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}}$ and $\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}$ are correlated. This property follows from two observations: • $\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lambda_{1}\,\lambda_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{t,x}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=\mathcal{F}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lambda_{1}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{t,x}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})$ , where $\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}(t,x)$ is defined in ( 5.1 ). This follows from the compensated compactness approach applied directly to the two PDEs in ( 1.4 ). • $\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lambda_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{t,x}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\leq\mathcal{F}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lambda_{1}\,\lambda_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{t,x}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})$ , which follows from the fact that the weak limit of $|\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}-\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}|^{2}$ coincides with the weak limit of $\mathcal{F}\,\left(\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}}\,\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}-\frac{u}{s}\,\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}\right)$ ; see Section 3.5 . The latter essentially exploits the equation for the sum $s_{\varepsilon}$ , in which we can pass to the limit $\varepsilon\to 0$ (the limit of every term can be identified). These two observations imply that there is equality in the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality $\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lambda_{1}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{t,x}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lambda_{2}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{t,x}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lambda_{1}\,\lambda_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{t,x}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\right|^{2},$ which implies that either $\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lambda_{1}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{t,x}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=0$ or $\lambda_{2}=\mathcal{F}\,\lambda_{1}$ . In order to prove that $\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lambda_{1}\,\lambda_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{t,x}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=0$ , we only need to consider the case $\lambda_{2}=\mathcal{F}\,\lambda_{1}$ , where $\mathcal{F}$ is as above. This imposes a restriction on the support and has important consequences for the analysis of weak limits. By the representation ( 2.1 ), we have that the sequences $\left\{f\left(t,x,\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}}-\frac{u}{s},\,\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}-\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}\right)\right\},\left\{f\left(t,x,\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}}-\frac{u}{s},\mathcal{F}\,\left(\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}}-\frac{u}{s}\right)\right)\right\},$ have the same weak limits whenever they are weakly compact in $L^{1}_{\loc}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ . In particular, we can eliminate $\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}$ from the identities. We exploit this observation in Section 5.2 , together with a new conservation law from Section 3.3 , in the form $\partial_{t}E_{\varepsilon}-\partial_{x}\big((E_{\varepsilon}+s_{\varepsilon})\,\partial_{x}p(s_{\varepsilon})+u_{\varepsilon}\log u_{\varepsilon}\,\partial_{x}\mathcal{V}^{1}[u_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}]+v_{\varepsilon}\log v_{\varepsilon}\,\partial_{x}\mathcal{V}^{2}[u_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}]\big)=g_{\varepsilon}+h_{\varepsilon},$ (2.2) where $E_{\varepsilon}=u_{\varepsilon}\log u_{\varepsilon}+v_{\varepsilon}\log v_{\varepsilon}$ , $h_{\varepsilon}\to 0$ in $L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}))$ and $\{g_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}$ is bounded in $L^{1}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ . We apply compensated compactness again as explained above (although now the remainder is not compact in $H^{-1}_{\loc}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ and one has to use Theorem 4.3 ). The resulting identity is technically challenging to analyze due to its nonlinear structure arising from the logarithmic terms. We therefore perform several decompositions and apply delicate estimates to the identity obtained via compensated compactness. Combining these with the relation $\lambda_{2}=\mathcal{F}\,\lambda_{1}$ , we conclude that the Young measure is in fact a Dirac mass concentrated at $(0,0)$ . When $\alpha\leq 2$ , we can use another conservation law (see Section 3.4 ) $\partial_{t}\left(\frac{u_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{s_{\varepsilon}}\right)-\partial_{x}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\frac{u_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{s_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\,\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}\!+\!\frac{u_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{s_{\varepsilon}}\,\partial_{x}\mathcal{V}^{1}[u_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}]\!-\!\frac{u_{\varepsilon}^{3}}{3\,s_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\,\partial_{x}(\mathcal{V}^{1}[u_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}]\!-\!\mathcal{V}^{2}[u_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon}])\right)\!=\!g_{\varepsilon}+h_{\varepsilon},$ (2.3) where a small difference here is that $\{g_{\varepsilon}\}$ is bounded only in $L^{1}(0,T;L^{1}_{\loc}(\mathbb{R}))$ . The computations leading to the final conclusion, exploiting ( 2.3 ), are substantially simpler (see Section 5.4 for this alternative argument). The final proof of Theorem 1.3 is presented in Section 5.3 . Finally, once we know that $\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\lambda_{1}\,\lambda_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{t,x}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=0$ , we can prove the convergences claimed in Theorem 1.3 by the usual energy considerations; see Section 4.3 . Last but not least, we remark that this programme succeeds only because the Young measure was shown to be supported on a line, which substantially simplifies the identities under consideration. Without this observation, the computations would fail, since we would be unable to characterize the weak limits of joint nonlinear functions of $\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}}$ and $\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}$ . 3. ∎ Remark 3.8 . We remark that the restriction $\alpha\leq 2$ is only needed to ensure that $\{g_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}$ is bounded in $L^{1}_{\loc}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ . This is achieved by proving that $\{\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}$ is bounded in $L^{1}(0,T;L^{1}_{\loc}(\mathbb{R}))$ . If such a bound could be established for $\alpha>2$ , the conclusion of Proposition 3.7 would also hold in this case. Furthermore, the proof of Proposition 5.2 presented in Section 5.4 would also be valid, yielding an alternative (and simpler) proof of Theorem 1.3 . It is not clear whether the range $\alpha\leq 2$ is optimal, and this question appears to be delicate even for the pure porous medium equation $\partial_{t}s=\partial_{x}^{2}s^{\alpha}$ on $\mathbb{R}$ without advection (see, e.g., [ 55 , 32 ] ). A naive approach to prove that $\partial_{x}s\in L^{1}_{\loc}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ in this case is to use the identity $-{\alpha\,\kappa}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s^{\alpha+\kappa-2}\,|\partial_{x}s|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau=\frac{1}{1+\kappa}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(s(t,x)^{1+\kappa}-s(0,x)^{1+\kappa})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,$ (3.25) where $\kappa\in(-1,0]$ . By splitting the spatial integral into the regions $|x|\leq 1$ and $|x|~>~1$ , the terms on the right-hand side can be controlled using the mass $\int_{\mathbb{R}}s(t,x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x$ and a sufficiently high moment $\int_{\mathbb{R}}s(t,x)\,|x|^{\sigma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x$ , since $1+\kappa\in(0,1]$ . Here we use that, for the porous medium equation, all moments are controlled provided the initial data is bounded and has finite moments of all orders. We conclude that $\partial_{x}s\in L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ provided $\alpha+\kappa-2<0$ , which can be achieved when $\alpha<3$ . This does not seem to be optimal, since for the Barenblatt’s solution $B(t,x)=t^{-1/(\alpha+1)}\,F(x\,t^{-1/(\alpha+1)})$ with $F(y)=(C-y^{2})_{+}^{1/(\alpha-1)}$ , a direct computation shows that $B(t,x)\in L^{1}((\tau,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ for all $\tau>0$ . Regarding our problem ( 1.4 ), the argument exploiting ( 3.25 ) cannot be adapted, since due to the advection we obtain an additional term on the (RHS) of ( 3.25 ), which is roughly $\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s_{\varepsilon}^{\kappa}\,|\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau$ . By writing $s_{\varepsilon}^{\kappa}\,|\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}|=s_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\kappa+\alpha-2}{2}}\,|\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}|\,s_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\kappa-\alpha+2}{2}}$ , we see that this term can be controlled by $\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha+\kappa-2}\,|\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tau$ only if $\kappa-\alpha+2\geq 0$ , i.e. $\alpha\leq 2$ . Young measures We recall that we define $\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{{s_{\varepsilon}}}=0$ when $s_{\varepsilon}=0$ and $\frac{u}{s}=0$ when $s=0$ (this does not matter much because when it comes to identification of the limit $\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}}\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}$ , the case $s(t,x)=0$ is easy and we will restrict the reasoning almost immediately to the case $s(t,x)>0$ , see Step 1 in Proposition 5.1 ). For each $(t,x)$ we define the measure $\pi_{t,x}$ on $\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$ to be the Young measure (see [ 60 ] for the original paper by Young, who addressed the lack of strong convergence in problems of the calculus of variations; [ 5 ] for the work of Ball, who introduced this concept into the study of PDEs; and [ 46 , Chapter 6] and [ 51 , Chapter 4] for more comprehensive treatments) of the sequence $\left(\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{{s_{\varepsilon}}}-\frac{u}{{s}},\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}-\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}\right).$ (4.1) To say that $\pi_{t,x}$ is the Young measure of the sequence in ( 4.1 ) means that: • for any Carathéodory function $f(t,x,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}):[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}$ (i.e. measurable in $(t,x)$ and continuous in $(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})$ ) we have that $(t,x)\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(t,x,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{t,x}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\mbox{ is (Lebesgue) measurable,}$ • for any Carathéodory function $f(t,x,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}):[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}$ such that $\Big\{f\Big(t,x,\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{{s_{\varepsilon}}}-\frac{u}{{s}},\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}-\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}\Big)\Big\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}$ is weakly compact in $L^{1}_{\loc}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ , we have $f\left(t,x,\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{{s_{\varepsilon}}}-\frac{u}{{s}},\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}-\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}\right)\rightharpoonup\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(t,x,\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi_{t,x}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})$ (4.2) weakly in $L^{1}_{\loc}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ after passing to a converging subsequence. Div-Curl Lemma and compensated compactness The idea is now to find some constraints on $\pi_{t,x}$ . The main tool will be a version of Div-Curl Lemma [ 29 , p.54] (see also [ 45 , 56 ] for the original references) whose original statement is recalled below: Theorem 4.2 (Div-Curl Lemma) . Let $\{\bf a_{\varepsilon}\}$ , $\{\bf b_{\varepsilon}\}$ be two sequences converging weakly in $L^{2}_{\loc}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{k})$ to some ${\bf a}$ , ${\bf b}$ for some $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}$ . Suppose moreover that $\{\DIV{\bf a_{\varepsilon}}\}$ and $\{\CURL{\bf b_{\varepsilon}}\}$ lie in a compact subset of $H^{-1}_{\loc}(\Omega)$ . Then, $a_{\varepsilon}\cdot b_{\varepsilon}\to a\cdot b$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$ in the sense of distributions. Typically, for two continuity equations $\displaystyle\partial_{t}U_{\varepsilon}$ $\displaystyle=\partial_{x}F_{\varepsilon}+S_{\varepsilon},$ $\displaystyle\partial_{t}V_{\varepsilon}$ $\displaystyle=\partial_{x}G_{\varepsilon}+R_{\varepsilon},$ with $\{U_{\varepsilon}\}$ , $\{V_{\varepsilon}\}$ , $\{F_{\varepsilon}\}$ , $\{G_{\varepsilon}\}$ bounded in $L^{2}_{\loc}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ and $\{S_{\varepsilon}\}$ , $\{R_{\varepsilon}\}$ strongly compact in $H^{-1}_{\loc}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R})$ , one applies Theorem 4.2 with $a_{\varepsilon}=(U_{\varepsilon},-F_{\varepsilon}),\qquad b_{\varepsilon}=(G_{\varepsilon},V_{\varepsilon}).$ We will apply these ideas twice and for the second application we need to slightly relax the assumptions of Theorem 4.2 . The following variant is proved in [ 21 ] . Theorem 4.3 . Let $\{\bf a_{\varepsilon}\}$ , $\{\bf b_{\varepsilon}\}$ be two sequences converging weakly in $L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{k})$ to some ${\bf a}$ , ${\bf b}$ for a bounded Lipschitz set $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}$ . Suppose moreover that $\{\DIV{\bf a_{\varepsilon}}\}$ and $\{\CURL{\bf b_{\varepsilon}}\}$ lie in a compact subset of $(W^{1,\infty}_{0}(\Omega))^{*}$ and that the sequence $\{{\bf a_{\varepsilon}}\cdot{\bf b_{\varepsilon}}\}$ is uniformly integrable in $L^{1}(\Omega)$ : $\forall\delta>0\quad\exists\kappa>0:\quad A\subset\Omega,|A|\leq\kappa\implies\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}\int_{A}|{\bf a_{\varepsilon}}\cdot{\bf b_{\varepsilon}}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\delta.$ Then, ${\bf a_{\varepsilon}}\cdot{\bf b_{\varepsilon}}\to{\bf a}\cdot{\bf b}$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$ in the sense of distributions. 4.3. Extension to the nonconservative case ( 1.6 ) We briefly outline the necessary modifications in order to treat the nonconservative system ( 1.6 ). Sections 3.1 and 3.2 : A priori estimates and compactness. The proofs of (E1) , (E2) , and (E7) are essentially identical. First, the mass $M$ is no longer fixed, but it can be controlled by a simple Grönwall argument. Therefore, in what follows, we still assume that $\int_{\mathbb{R}}s_{\varepsilon}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq M$ for all $t\in[0,T]$ . The identity ( 3.2 ) has an additional term on the (RHS) $(\|G^{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|G^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}})\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u_{\varepsilon}|\log u_{\varepsilon}|+v_{\varepsilon}|\log v_{\varepsilon}|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,$ which can be estimated in terms of the integrals of $u_{\varepsilon}\log u_{\varepsilon}$ , $u_{\varepsilon}\,|x|$ and $e^{-|x|}$ by Lemma 3.3 . Similarly, the identity ( 3.4 ) gains an additional term on the (RHS) of the form $(\|G^{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|G^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}})\int_{\mathbb{R}}s_{\varepsilon}\,|x|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.$ This still permits closing the Grönwall-type argument for $\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u_{\varepsilon}|\log u_{\varepsilon}|+v_{\varepsilon}|\log v_{\varepsilon}|+s_{\varepsilon}|x|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x$ when $\alpha\leq 1$ . For $\alpha>1$ , the identity ( 3.7 ) gets an additional term on the (RHS) of the form $(\|G^{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|G^{2}\|_{L^{\infty}})\int_{\mathbb{R}}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,$ so Grönwall’s inequality can be applied once again. The main modification concerns the $L^{\infty}$ bound on $s_{\varepsilon}$ obtained via Alikakos’ method. Indeed, the key identity for $\partial_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x$ used there, namely ( 3.9 ), has an additional term $C\,\gamma\int_{\mathbb{R}}s_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x$ and its dependence on $\gamma$ requires some care. Nevertheless, this term still allows one to conclude that $\{s_{\varepsilon}\}$ is bounded in $L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}))$ for all $1\leq p<\infty$ by the argument following ( 3.9 ). In particular, ( 3.10 ) remains valid. Proceeding to the iteration scheme, it relies on estimating the (RHS) of ( 3.9 ). The key observation is that the new term $\gamma\int_{\mathbb{R}}s_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x$ can be handled in the same way as the term $\frac{\gamma\,(\gamma-1)}{2\,\delta_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s_{\varepsilon}^{-\alpha+\gamma+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x$ , which already appears there. Repeating the computation in ( 3.11 ), we write $\int_{\mathbb{R}}s_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}s_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\gamma+\alpha}{2}+1}\,s_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\gamma-\alpha}{2}-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\left\|s_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\gamma+\alpha}{2}+1}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\int_{\mathbb{R}}s_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\gamma-\alpha}{2}-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.$ The first factor $\|s_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\gamma+\alpha}{2}+1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}$ has already been estimated in ( 3.12 ). For the second factor, since $\frac{\gamma-\alpha}{2}-1<\frac{\gamma}{2}$ , we can estimate $\int_{\mathbb{R}}s_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\gamma-\alpha}{2}-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq(\int_{\mathbb{R}}s_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x)^{\beta}\,M^{1-\beta}$ with $\beta=\frac{\frac{\gamma-\alpha}{2}-2}{\frac{\gamma}{2}-1}$ , analogously to ( 3.14 ). Note that $\beta\in[\frac{4}{5},1]$ for $\gamma$ sufficiently large. Therefore, we obtain an inequality analogous to ( 3.15 ), which allows us to apply the Alikakos’ method. The remaining estimates, as well as the convergence results in Section 3.2 , are unaffected by the additional term. Sections 3.3 and 3.4 : Conservation laws. Only minor modifications are required here. In Proposition 3.6 , the function $g_{\varepsilon}$ contains an additional term that reads $u_{\varepsilon}\,(1+\log u_{\varepsilon})\,G^{1}(s_{\varepsilon})+v_{\varepsilon}\,(1+\log v_{\varepsilon})\,G^{2}(s_{\varepsilon})$ , which belongs to the desired space by (E5) . Similarly, in Proposition 3.7 , $g_{\varepsilon}$ contains an additional term $\frac{2\,u_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{s_{\varepsilon}}\,G^{1}(s_{\varepsilon})-\frac{u_{\varepsilon}^{3}}{s_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\,G^{1}(s_{\varepsilon})-\frac{u_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,v_{\varepsilon}}{s_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\,G^{2}(s_{\varepsilon})$ , which again belongs to the desired space by (E4) . Section 3.5 : The limit of $|\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}-\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}|^{2}$ . The identification of the limit relies on the PDE for $s_{\varepsilon}$ , integrated in space and multiplied by $\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}$ . The additional terms to be handled are $\left[\int_{-\infty}^{x}u_{\varepsilon}\,G^{1}(s_{\varepsilon})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right]\,\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}$ , $\left[\int_{-\infty}^{x}v_{\varepsilon}\,G^{2}(s_{\varepsilon})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right]\,\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}$ . A standard argument, based on integration by parts and the strong convergence of $\{s_{\varepsilon}\}$ , shows that these terms converge to $\left[\int_{-\infty}^{x}u\,G^{1}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right]\,\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}$ , $\left[\int_{-\infty}^{x}v\,G^{2}(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right]\,\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}$ . Consequently, they do not contribute to the limit of $|\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}-\partial_{x}s^{\alpha}|^{2}$ . Sections 4 , 5.1 , 5.2 , and 5.4 : Young measures and compensated compactness. There are no changes needed there. The inclusion of the new terms in the function $g_{\varepsilon}$ (in Sections 3.3 and 3.4 ) above makes the additional terms completely invisible in the compensated compactness argument. Section 5.3 : Proof of Theorem 1.3 . The only required modification arises in the proof of the strong convergence of $\{\partial_{x}s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}\}$ . In ( 5.13 ), an additional term $u_{\varepsilon}\,G^{1}(s_{\varepsilon})\,s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}+v_{\varepsilon}\,G^{2}(s_{\varepsilon})\,s_{\varepsilon}^{\alpha}$ appears, whose weak limit is $u\,G^{1}(s)\,s^{\alpha}+v\,G^{2}(s)\,s^{\alpha}$ . This is precisely the extra term that arises in ( 5.14 ). Consequently, these new contributions cancel within the energy argument of Section 5.3 , and the same conclusion follows.
