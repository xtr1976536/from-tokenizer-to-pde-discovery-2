Front propagation in hybrid reaction-diffusion epidemic models with spatial heterogeneity. Part II: Pulsating traveling waves Quentin Griette Laboratoire de Mathématiques Appliquées du Havre, Université Le Havre Normandie, EA 3821, FR CNRS 3335 25, rue Philippe Lebon, 76600 Le Havre, France quentin.griette@univ-lehavre.fr Hiroshi Matano Meiji Institute for Advanced Study of Mathematical Sciences, Meiji University, 4-21-1 Nakano, Tokyo 164-8525, Japan matano@meiji.ac.jp August 24, 2026 Abstract We consider a two-species reaction-diffusion system in one space dimension that is derived from an epidemiological model in a spatially periodic environment with two types of pathogens: the wild type and the mutant.
The system is of a hybrid nature, partly cooperative and partly competitive, but neither of these entirely.
As a result, the comparison principle does not hold.
In the previous work, we studied the propagation properties of the solutions to the Cauchy problem for this system and showed, among other things, that the spreading speeds of the fronts to the right and to the left directions, denoted by $c^{*}_{R}$ and $c^{*}_{L}$ , can be characterized by using certain principal eigenvalues, and studied the homogenization limit as the spatial period $L$ tends to $0$ , and also discussed the long-time behavior of solutions behind the fronts. In the present paper we prove the existence of pulsating traveling waves in the right direction (resp. left direction) with speed $c$ for any $c\geq c^{*}_{R}$ (resp. $c\geq c^{*}_{L}$ ), where $c^{*}_{R}$ and $c^{*}_{L}$ denote the aforementioned spreading speeds in the right and left directions. We also prove that the leading edge of any traveling wave has the exponential decay rate that is anticipated from formal linear analysis, thus extending part of the results of Hamel 2008 to systems of equations.
Finally, we present an example in which the two speeds $c^{*}_{R}$ and $c^{*}_{L}$ are different. This is done by considering a multi-scale singular limit problem. This result highlights a marked difference between our system and scalar KPP type equations. 1 Introduction In this paper we consider the following reaction-diffusion system: $\left\{\begin{aligned} {}&u_{t}=\big(\sigma(x)u_{x}\big)_{x}+\big(r_{u}(x)-\kappa_{u}(x)(u+v)\big)u+\mu_{v}(x)v-\mu_{u}(x)u,&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},\\
&v_{t}=\big(\sigma(x)v_{x}\big)_{x}+\big(r_{v}(x)-\kappa_{v}(x)(u+v)\big)v+\mu_{u}(x)u-\mu_{v}(x)v,&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{aligned}\right.$ (1.1) Here $u(t,x)$ , $v(t,x)$ stand for the density of a population of individuals living in a periodically heterogeneous environment, $r_{u}(x)\in\mathbb{R}$ and $r_{v}(x)\in\mathbb{R}$ represent the intrinsic growth rate (which are not necessarily positive), $\kappa_{u}(x)>0$ and $\kappa_{v}(x)>0$ represent the intensity of the competition, and $\mu_{u}(x)>0$ , $\mu_{v}(x)>0$ denote the mutation rates between the two populations.
We assume that these coefficients, including the diffusion coefficient $\sigma(x)$ , are all $L$ -periodic. The above problem is motivated partly by the study of SIS epidemiological models describing the propagation of pathogens that are subject to mutations, such as the following: $\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}S_{t}=\partial_{x}(\sigma(x)\partial_{x}S)-\big(\beta_{1}(x)I_{1}+\beta_{2}(x)I_{2}\big)S+\gamma_{1}(x)I_{1}+\gamma_{2}(x)I_{2},&&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},\\
&\partial_{t}I_{1}=\partial_{x}(\sigma(x)\partial_{x}I_{1})+\beta_{1}(x)SI_{1}-\gamma_{1}(x)I_{1}+\mu_{2}(x)I_{2}-\mu_{1}(x)I_{1},&&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},\\
&\partial_{t}I_{2}=\partial_{x}(\sigma(x)\partial_{x}I_{2})+\beta_{2}(x)SI_{2}-\gamma_{2}(x)I_{2}+\mu_{1}(x)I_{1}-\mu_{2}(x)I_{2},&&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{aligned}\right.$ (1.2) Here the $\beta_{1},\beta_{2}$ denote the infection rates, $\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}$ the recovery rates, and $\mu_{1},\mu_{2}$ stand for the mutation rates between the pathogens. It is not difficult to show that the quantity $N(t,x):=S(t,x)+I_{1}(t,x)+I_{2}(t,x)$ satisfies a pure diffusion equation $\partial_{t}N=\partial_{x}\left(\sigma(x)\partial_{x}N\right)$ . Therefore, if we assume
that $N(0,x)$ is constant in $x$ ,
then $N(t,x)$ remains constant in $t$ and $x$ .
Thus we obtain that $u=I_{1}$ , $v=I_{2}$ satisfy ( 1.1 ) with $r_{*}(x):=N\beta_{i}(x)-\gamma_{i}(x)$ and $\kappa_{i}(x):=\beta_{i}(x)$ , where $i=1,2$ and $*=u,v$ .
Hence the propagation dynamics of ( 1.2 ) is equivalent to that of ( 1.1 ). System ( 1.2 ) describes the propagation of a genetically unstable pathogen in a population of hosts which exhibit heterogeneity in space. This heterogeneity represents the spatially heterogeneous environment that affects the behavior of individuals depending on where they are. Spatial heterogeneity in the use of antibiotics, fungicides or insecticides affects the transmission of pathogens and pests and is explored as a way to minimize the risk of emergence of drug resistance [ 7 ] .
Beaumont et al [ 4 ] study a related model of propagation of salmonella in an industrial hen house. In their study the heterogeneity comes from the alignment of cages separated by free space that allow farmers to take care of the animals. Griette et al [ 13 ] studied the propagation properties of a closely related model in the context of the evolution of drug resistance. The propagation speed of the solutions of reaction-diffusion equations is often linked to special solutions called traveling wave solutions , that are particular solutions that propagate at a prescribed speed. There exists a large literature on traveling wave solutions and the propagation dynamics of solutions to reaction-diffusion equations and systems, see [ 19 , 8 , 2 , 3 , 30 , 22 , 27 , 20 , 21 ] among others.
When the coefficients depend periodically on the spatial variable such as ( 1.1 ), the traveling waves are often called pulsating traveling waves , see [ 26 , 32 , 5 , 31 ] among others. Our system has a rather intriguing character in the sense that it is cooperative when $(u,v)$ is small while the competitive nature becomes dominant when $(u,v)$ is large. Therefore the standard comparison principle does not apply to the entire system. Such a system has been studied by Wang [ 28 ] , Wang and Castillo-Chavez [ 29 ] , Griette and Raoul [ 16 ] , Girardin [ 12 , 11 ] , and Morris, Börger and Crooks [ 23 ] , when the coefficients are homogeneous in space.
However, in
our case, the coefficients are spatially periodic.
As far as scalar equations are concerend, there
is a large literature on equations with periodic coefficients, notably [ 26 , 32 , 5 , 31 , 21 ] .
As for systems,
Alfaro and Griette [ 1 ] constructed a traveling wave for a related system that travels at the expected minimal speed. The special case where the coefficients are spatially homogeneous has been treated in [ 28 , 29 , 16 , 12 , 11 , 23 ] , In our previous work [ 15 ] , we considered system ( 1.1 ) under the initial condition $u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),\ \ v(0,x)=v_{0}(x),\quad\ x\in\mathbb{R},$ (1.3) where $u_{0},v_{0}$ are bounded nonnegative functions on $\mathbb{R}$ whose supports are, in most cases, localized in space.
Among other things, we proved that the solutions fronts propagate to the right and to the left asymptotically at constant speeds, that can be computed from a family of linearized eigenproblems around the leading edge. We also studied the behavior of the solution far behind the propagation front and proved the convergence to the unique steady state in the case where the coefficients are spatially homogeneous or rapidly oscillating. Finally, we studied the homogenization problem as $L\to 0$ and determined the effective coefficients of the homogenized limit. In the present paper, we focus on traveling waves and study their existence and qualitative properties.
We first prove the existence of pulsating traveling waves in the right (resp. left) direction for any speed $c\geq c^{*}_{R}$ (resp. $c\geq c^{*}_{L}$ ).
We also show that the leading edge of any traveling wave decays exponentially
as $x\to\infty$ (or quasi-exponentially in the critical case where $c=c^{*}_{R}$ or $c=c^{*}_{L}$ ). This result implies that all the pulsating traveling waves fulfill the ansatz that is
anticipated from formal linear analysis. This extends part of the results of Hamel [ 17 ] to systems of equations. We note that our existence proof for the critical case $c=c^{*}_{R}$ or $c=c^{*}_{L}$ does not rely on the usual limiting argument $c\to c^{*}_{R},c^{*}_{L}$ . We instead give a direct proof of the existence for the critical case by constructing super- and sub-solutions that are different from the non-critical case $c>c^{*}_{R},c^{*}_{L}$ . Next, we consider the case where the coefficients are spatially homogeneous or rapidly oscillating, and prove that the profile of the traveling wave far behind its front converges to the unique periodic (or homogeneous) steady state. Finally, we present an example where $c^{*}_{R}\neq c^{*}_{L}$ . This is done by a multi-scale singular limit analysis. We remark that, in the scalar KPP equation, the left and right critical wave speeds are always equal, even if the coefficients are not symmetric in $x$ , which is a consequence of the Fredholm alternative. This last result highlights a marked difference between scalar equations and systems. Our paper is organized as follows. In section 2 we first recall key mathematical notions such as principal eigenvalues of various kinds, left and right spreading speeds, and state their basic properties. This will be done in subsection 2.1 . Then, in subsections 2.2 and 2.3 , we present our main results including the existence of critical and non-critical traveling waves (Theorem 2.13 ), decay properties along the leading edge (Theorem 2.17 ), convergence to the steady states in the spatially homogeneous and rapidly oscillating cases (Theorem 2.20 ), and the construction of an example with asymmetric propagation speeds (Theorem 2.22 ).
The proofs of Theorems 2.13 , 2.17 , 2.20 and 2.22 will be given in sections 3 , 4 , 5 and 6 , respectively. 2 Main results Throughout this article we make the following assumption on the coefficients of ( 1.1 ). Assumption 1 (Cooperative-competitive system) . They will be used in our later arguments. The linearized system associated with ( 1.1 ) is the following. $\left\{\begin{aligned} {}&u_{t}=\big(\sigma(x)u_{x}\big)_{x}+r_{u}(x)u+\mu_{v}(x)v-\mu_{u}(x)u,&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},\\
&v_{t}=\big(\sigma(x)v_{x}\big)_{x}+r_{v}(x)v+\mu_{u}(x)u-\mu_{v}(x)v,&t>0,x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{aligned}\right.$ (2.4) Note that this is a cooperative system.
We first define the notions of periodic principal, $\lambda$ -periodic principal and Dirichlet principal eigenelements as follows. Definition 1 (Periodic principal eigenpair) . By a periodic principal eigenpair associated with ( 2.4 ) we mean any pair $\big(\lambda_{1}^{per},(\varphi(x),\psi(x))\big)$ where $\lambda_{1}^{per}\in\mathbb{R}$ , $\varphi(x)$ and $\psi(x)$ are positive $L$ -periodic smooth functions that satisfy $\left\{\begin{aligned} {}&L^{1}[\varphi,\psi](x):=\big(\sigma(x)\varphi_{x}\big)_{x}+r_{u}(x)\varphi+\mu_{v}(x)\psi-\mu_{u}(x)\varphi=\lambda_{1}^{per}\varphi,\\
&L^{2}[\varphi,\psi](x):=\big(\sigma(x)\psi_{x}\big)_{x}+r_{v}(x)\psi+\mu_{u}(x)\varphi-\mu_{v}(x)\psi=\lambda_{1}^{per}\psi.\end{aligned}\right.$ (2.5) We call $\lambda_{1}^{per}$ the periodic principal eigenvalue and $(\varphi,\psi)$ a principal eigenvector . It follows from the Krein-Rutman Theorem that $\lambda_{1}^{per}$ is unique, and that $(\varphi,\psi)$ is unique up to multiplication by a positive scalar. Definition 2 ( $\lambda$ -periodic principal eigenpair) . For $\lambda>0$ , by a $\lambda$ -periodic principal eigenpair associated with ( 2.4 ) we mean any pair $\big(k(\lambda),(\varphi(x),\psi(x))\big)$ where $k(\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}$ , $\varphi(x)$ and $\psi(x)$ are positive $L$ -periodic smooth functions that satisfy $\left\{\begin{aligned} {}&L^{1}_{\lambda}[\varphi,\psi](x):=e^{\lambda x}L^{1}[e^{-\lambda x}\varphi,e^{-\lambda x}\psi](x)=k(\lambda)\varphi,\\
&L^{2}_{\lambda}[\varphi,\psi](x):=e^{\lambda x}L^{2}[e^{-\lambda x}\varphi,e^{-\lambda x}\psi](x)=k(\lambda)\psi.\end{aligned}\right.$ (2.6) or, equivalently, $\left\{\begin{aligned} {}&\big(\sigma(x)\varphi_{x}\big)_{x}-2\lambda\sigma(x)\varphi_{x}+\big(\lambda^{2}\sigma(x)-\lambda\sigma_{x}(x)+r_{u}(x)\big)\varphi+\mu_{v}(x)\psi-\mu_{u}(x)\varphi=k(\lambda)\varphi,\\
&\big(\sigma(x)\psi_{x}\big)_{x}-2\lambda\sigma(x)\psi_{x}+\big(\lambda^{2}\sigma(x)-\lambda\sigma_{x}(x)+r_{v}(x)\big)\psi+\mu_{u}(x)\varphi-\mu_{v}(x)\psi=k(\lambda)\psi.\end{aligned}\right.$ ( 2.6 ′ ) We call $k(\lambda)$ the $\lambda$ -periodic principal eigenvalue and $(\varphi,\psi)$ a $\lambda$ -periodic principal eigenvector . Again by the Krein-Rutman Theorem, for each $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$ , $k(\lambda)$ is unique and that $(\varphi,\psi)$ is unique up to multiplication by a positive scalar. Clearly we have $k(0)=\lambda_{1}^{per}$ . Definition 3 (Dirichlet principal eigenpair) . Let $R>0$ be given. By a Dirichlet principal eigenpair on $(-R,R)$ associated with ( 2.4 ) we mean any pair $\big(\lambda_{1}^{R},(\varphi(x),\psi(x))\big)$ where $\lambda_{1}^{R}\in\mathbb{R}$ , $\varphi(x)$ and $\psi(x)$ are positive smooth functions on $[-R,R]$ that satisfy $\left\{\begin{aligned} {}&\big(\sigma(x)\varphi_{x}\big)_{x}+r_{u}(x)\varphi+\mu_{v}(x)\psi-\mu_{u}(x)\varphi=\lambda_{1}^{R}\varphi,\\
&\big(\sigma(x)\psi_{x}\big)_{x}+r_{v}(x)\psi+\mu_{u}(x)\varphi-\mu_{v}(x)\psi=\lambda_{1}^{R}\psi,\end{aligned}\right.$ (2.7a) and $\varphi(-R)=\psi(-R)=0\text{ and }\varphi(R)=\psi(R)=0.$ (2.7b) We call $\lambda_{1}^{R}$ the principal eigenvalue and $(\varphi,\psi)$ a Dirichlet principal eigenvector . As before, the Krein-Rutman theorem ensures that $\lambda_{1}^{R}$ is unique and $(\varphi,\psi)$ is unique up to multiplication by a positive scalar. Proposition 2.3 (Properties of $k(\lambda)$ [ 15 , Proposition 1] ) . Let Assumption 1 hold true. Then: (i) For each $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$ , there exists a $\lambda$ -periodic principal eigenpair $\big(k(\lambda),(\varphi,\psi)\big)$ with $\varphi(x)>0$ and $\psi(x)>0$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$ , which solves ( 2.6 ), and $(\varphi,\psi)$ is unique up to the multiplication by a positive scalar. In addition, the right-hand side has a unique minimizer up to multiplication by a positive scalar, which coincides with the principal eigenvector of the problem ( 2.6 ). the following inequalites hold: $\sigma_{\min}\lambda^{2}+r_{\min}\leq k(\lambda)\leq\sigma_{\max}\lambda^{2}+r_{\max}\quad\hbox{for all}\ \ \lambda\in\mathbb{R}.$ (2.9) Remark 2.4 . Throughout the rest of this section, we assume, as in Theorem 2.7 , that $\lambda_{1}^{per}>0$ . This condition implies that $(u,v)=(0,0)$ is unstable under positive $L$ -periodic perturbations. Without this condition, any solution of ( 1.1 ) with small nonnegative initial data decays to $0$ as $t\to\infty$ uniformly on $\mathbb{R}$ . First we recall the notion of traveling wave solutions under a spatially periodic environment. We distinguish the “right traveling waves” and “left traveling waves”. Definition 6 (Traveling wave solutions) . Let $c>0$ be given and $\big(u(t,x),v(t,x)\big)$ be an entire solution to ( 1.1 ), i.e. a solution that is defined for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$ and $x\in\mathbb{R}$ . We say that $\big(u(t,x),v(t,x)\big)$ is a right traveling wave propagating at speed $c$ if it satisfies $u\left(t+\frac{L}{c},x\right)=u(t,x-L),\;v\left(t+\frac{L}{c},x\right)=v(t,x-L),\ \ \text{for all}\ \ (t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},$ (2.18) as well as the following asymptotics at $x=\pm\infty$ : $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}u(t,x)=0,\;\lim_{x\to+\infty}v(t,x)=0,\ \ \text{for all}\ \ t\in\mathbb{R},$ (2.19a) $\displaystyle\liminf_{x\to-\infty}u(t,x)>0,\;\liminf_{x\to-\infty}v(t,x)>0,\ \ \text{for all}\ \ t\in\mathbb{R}.$ (2.19b) The left traveling wave is defined the same way by reversing the direction of the $x$ -axis, namely $u\left(t+\frac{L}{c},x\right)=u(t,x+L),\;v\left(t+\frac{L}{c},x\right)=v(t,x+L),\ \ \text{for all}\ \ (t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2},$ (2.20) with reversed asymptotics at $x=\pm\infty$ . Next we define the notion of right and left stationary wave solutions. Stationary waves, which we may also call “stationary fronts”, are the natural extension of traveling waves to the case $c=0$ . Definition 7 (Stationary wave solutions) . Let $\big(u(t,x),v(t,x)\big)=\big(u(x),v(x)\big)$ be a stationary solution to ( 1.1 ), i.e. a solution that is constant in time. We say that $\big(u(x),v(x)\big)$ is a right stationary wave (or a right stationary front ) if it satisfies
the asymptotics ( 2.19 ) at $x=\pm\infty$ .
A left stationary wave (or a left stationary front ) is defined the same way by reversing the direction of the $x$ -axis, namely, it satisfies the asymptotics $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}u(x)=0,\;\lim_{x\to-\infty}v(x)=0,$ (2.21a) $\displaystyle\liminf_{x\to+\infty}u(x)>0,\;\liminf_{x\to+\infty}v(x)>0,\ \ \text{for all}\ \ t\in\mathbb{R}.$ (2.21b) Our first main result is concerned with the existence of traveling waves. Here, the symbols $c^{*}_{R}$ and $c^{*}_{L}$ denote, respectively, the right and left spreading speeds defined in Definition 5 . Theorem 2.13 (Existence of traveling waves) . Let Assumption 1 hold true and let $\lambda_{1}^{per}>0$ . Assume that $c^{*}_{R}>0$ . Then there exists a right traveling wave $\big(u(t,x),v(t,x)\big)$ for ( 1.1 ) with speed $c$ if and only if $c\geq c^{*}_{R}$ . Similarly, if $c^{*}_{L}>0$ , then there exists a left traveling wave $\big(u(t,x),v(t,x)\big)$ for ( 1.1 ) with speed $c$ if and only if $c\geq c^{*}_{L}$ . Remark 2.14 . The above theorem, together with Theorem 2.7 , shows that the right (resp. left) spreading speeds for solutions of ( 1.1 ) with front-like initial data coincide with the minimal speed of the right (resp. left) traveling waves provided that $c^{*}_{R}>0$ (resp. $c^{*}_{L}>0$ ). Note also that if $c^{*}_{R}>0$ and $c^{*}_{L}>0$ , then these values coincide with the right and left spreading speeds for solutions with compactly supported initial data. Theorem 2.15 (Existence of stationary and traveling waves) . Let Assumption 1 hold true and let $\lambda_{1}^{per}>0$ . Assume that $c^{*}_{R}=0$ . Then there exists a right stationary wave for ( 1.1 ). Furthermore, there exists a traveling wave $\big(u(t,x),v(t,x)\big)$ with speed $c$ if and only if $c>c^{*}_{R}=0$ . Similarly, if $c^{*}_{L}=0$ , then there exists a right stationary wave for ( 1.1 ). Furthermore, there exists a traveling wave $\big(u(t,x),v(t,x)\big)$ with speed $c$ if and only if $c>c^{*}_{L}=0$ . Remark 2.16 . If $c^{*}_{R}=0$ , the propagation of any compactly supported initial data is blocked in the right direction. This is easily seen by using the exponential upper barriers ( 3.9 ) with $\lambda=\lambda^{*}$ and $c=c^{*}_{R}$ , which do not move in space, A similar statement holds true in the left direction if $c^{*}_{L}=0$ . The proof of Theorems 2.13 and 2.15 involves a fixed-point argument at its core. More precisely, for each speed $c\geq c^{*}_{R}$ , we first restrict the system ( 1.1 ) on the moving interval $-M+ct\leq x<+\infty$ and prove the existence of a traveling wave for this restricted system by applying the Schauder fixed-point theorem, then we let $M\to\infty$ to obtain a traveling wave of the original system. In order to apply the fixed-point theorem, we construct upper and lower barriers for our system ( 1.1 ) that have the same designated decay rate as $x\to\infty$ . This can be done despite the absence of the comparison principle for the entire system. The upper barrier is easy to construct, as it is simply a solution of the linearized system.
The construction of the lower barrier is much more involved. In the supercritical case $c>c^{*}_{R}$ it uses the difference between two exponential functions. This method is inspired by the paper of Hamel [ 17 ] for scalar equations. However, unlike the case of scalar equations, simply taking the maximum between $(0,0)$ and a sub-solution does not necessarily provide a lower barrier. Therefore, we need more subtle estimates in the case of systems, particularly when one of the components becomes negative while the other is still positive.
In the critical case $c=c^{*}_{R},c^{*}_{L}$ our construction of the lower barrier is different from that of [ 17 ] . As mentioned earlier, our existence proof for the critical speed $c=c_{R}^{*},c_{L}^{*}$ does not depend on the limiting argument $c\to c_{R}^{*},c_{L}^{*}$ . Instead, we prove the existence for the critical case by constructing upper and lower barriers that are different from those for the super-critical case. ∎ 3.2 Proof of existence The goal of this section is to prove Theorems 2.13 and 2.15 on the existence of traveling waves and stationary waves.
We consider only the right traveling waves and stationary waves, as the left ones can be treated precisely the same way. We seek traveling waves that have a precise decay rate when $x\to+\infty$ , that is anticipated by the linear analysis near the leading edge. Later, in Theorem 2.17 , we will see that these decay properties are universal for all traveling waves. As we mentioned earlier, the existence will be proved by a fixed-point argument. More precisely, for each speed $c\geq c^{*}_{R}$ , we first restrict the system ( 1.1 ) on the moving interval $-M+ct\leq x<+\infty$ and prove the existence of a traveling wave for this restricted system by applying the Schauder fixed-point theorem, then we let $M\to\infty$ to obtain a traveling wave of the original system. In order to apply the fixed-point theorem, we need to construct upper and lower barriers. The upper barrier is easy to construct, as it is simply a solution of the linearized system.
On the other hand, the construction of the lower barrier is much more involved. Our construction of lower barriers for $c>c^{*}_{R}$ is partly adapted from [ 17 ] on scalar equations, but the lower barrier for $c=c^{*}_{R}$ is different from [ 17 ] and is new, even for scalar KPP equations. As a matter of fact, it is possible to prove the existence of traveling waves for the critical case $c=c^{*}_{R}$ by a limiting argument, namely by taking the limit as $c\to c^{*}_{R}$ of the traveling waves of speed $c>c^{*}_{R}$ .
Such an approach is more common in the study of scalar equations, but it does not give detailed information about the spatial decay rate of the critical traveling wave as $x\to+\infty$ .
Our approach, on the other hand, gives an optimal decay rate directly for the case $c=c^{*}_{R}$ . Now, for each fixed $M\geq 0$ and $c\in\mathbb{R}$ , let us consider the following system, which is obtained by restricting the system ( 1.1 ) on the interval $-M+ct\leq x<+\infty$ : $\left\{\begin{aligned} {}&u_{t}=\big(\sigma(x)u_{x}\big)_{x}+\big(r_{u}(x)-\kappa_{u}(x)(u+v)\big)u+\mu_{v}(x)v-\mu_{u}(x)u,&t>0,\,x>-M+ct,\\
&v_{t}=\big(\sigma(x)v_{x}\big)_{x}+\big(r_{v}(x)-\kappa_{v}(x)(u+v)\big)v+\mu_{u}(x)u-\mu_{v}(x)v,&t>0,\,x>-M+ct,\\
&u_{x}(t,-M+ct)=v_{x}(t,-M+ct)=0\quad\ (t>0).\end{aligned}\right.$ (3.5) The global existence and the uniqueness of solutions of system ( 3.5 ) for nonnegative bounded continuous initial data $\big(u_{0}(x),v_{0}(x)\big)$ is classical.
We next consider the following auxiliary system which is obtained by restricting ( 3.2 ) on the interval $-M+ct\leq x<+\infty$ : $\left\{\begin{aligned} &\tilde{u}_{t}=\big(\sigma(x)\tilde{u}_{x}\big)_{x}+\big(r_{u}(x)-\kappa_{u}(x)(\tilde{u}+\tilde{v})-\beta\tilde{u}\big)\tilde{u}+\mu_{v}(x)\tilde{v}-\mu_{u}(x)\tilde{u},\\

&\tilde{u}_{x}(t,-M+ct)=\tilde{v}_{x}(t,-M+ct)=0\quad(t>0).\end{aligned}\right.$ (3.6) Then equivalents of Proposition 2.2 and Lemma 3.1 hold for this system, with virtually the same proof except for a minor modification at the boundary $x=-M+ct$ .

and similarly we conclude that $v^{2}(t,x)>v^{1,\gamma}(t-t_{*},x)$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$ . In view of this, ( 3.36 ) and the definition of $t_{*}$ , there exists a sequence $x_{n}\to+\infty$ such that either $\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{u^{1,\gamma}(t-t_{*},x_{n})}{u^{2}(t,x_{n})}=1$ , or $\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{v^{1,\gamma}(t-t_{*},x_{n})}{v^{2}(t,x_{n})}=1$ .
In both cases, we obtain $1=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\gamma\alpha_{1}e_{\lambda}\big(x_{n}-c(t-t_{*})\big)}{\alpha_{2}e_{\lambda}(x_{n}-ct)}=\gamma\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}e^{\lambda ct_{*}},$ so that $t_{*}=\frac{1}{\lambda c}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\gamma\alpha_{1}}\right).$ By taking the limit $\gamma\to 1$ and up to redefining $t_{*}$ , we have shown the property that $\big(u^{1}(t-t^{\prime},x),v^{1}(t-t^{\prime},x)\big)\leq\big(u^{2}(t,x),v^{2}(t,x)\big),\qquad^{\forall}t^{\prime}\geq t_{*}:=\frac{1}{\lambda c}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}}\right).$ (3.38) Exchanging the roles of $\big(u^{1}(t,x),v^{1}(t,x)\big)$ and $\big(u^{2}(t,x),v^{2}(t,x)\big)$ and arguing similarly, we obtain $\big(u^{1}(t,x),v^{1}(t,x)\big)\geq\big(u^{2}(t-t^{\prime},x),v^{2}(t-t^{\prime},x)\big),\qquad^{\forall}t^{\prime}\geq t^{\prime}_{*}:=\frac{1}{\lambda c}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}\right)=-t_{*}.$ (3.39) This proves that $\big(u^{1}(t-t_{*},x),v^{1}(t-t_{*},x)\big)\equiv\big(u^{2}(t,x),v^{2}(t,x)\big)$ , for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$ , and consequently this equality holds for any $t\in\mathbb{R}$ . This proves uniqueness up to a time shift. Now, we apply ( 3.38 ) to the pair $\big(u^{1}(t,x),v^{1}(t,x)\big)=\big(u^{2}(t,x),v^{2}(t,x)\big)$ . In this case $\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}$ , therefore we have $t_{*}=0$ . Hence ( 3.38 ) shows that $\big(u^{1}(t,x),v^{1}(t,x)\big)$ is time-increasing.
This finishes the proof or Theorem 2.19 .
∎ 4 Exponential decay of traveling waves In this section we focus on the exponential decay of traveling waves along the leading edge, as stated in Theorem 2.17 .
Throughout this section we
fix $c>0$ .
Our argument is largely inspired by the scalar case studied by Hamel [ 17 ] .
As in [ 17 ] , we make
a change of variable to obtain compactness in the “space variable”, by introducing the following functions: $U(t,x)=u\left(\frac{t+x}{c},x\right);\ V(t,x)=v\left(\frac{t+x}{c},x\right).$ (4.1) It is easily seen from the pulsating relation ( 2.18 ) that the new functions $U$ and $V$ are $L$ -periodic with respect to the $x$ variable and satisfy $\begin{split}cU_{t}-\sigma(x)\big(U_{tt}-2U_{tx}+U_{xx}\big)+\sigma_{x}(x)\big(U_{t}-U_{x}\big)&=f_{u}(x,U,V),\\
cV_{t}-\sigma(x)\big(V_{tt}-2V_{tx}+V_{xx}\big)+\sigma_{x}(x)\big(V_{t}-V_{x}\big)&=f_{v}(x,U,V).\end{split}$ (4.2) Moreover, given $U(t,x)$ and $V(t,x)$ , we can recover the original functions by the change of variables $u(t,x)=U\left(ct-x,x\right)$ and $v(t,x)=V\left(ct-x,x\right)$ .
With these functions $U,V$ , the statements ( 2.22 ), ( 2.23 ) of Theorem 2.17 are expressed in the following form: $\lim_{t\to-\infty}\frac{U(t,x)}{e(t)\varphi^{\lambda}(x)}=\lim_{t\to-\infty}\frac{V(t,x)}{e(t)\psi^{\lambda}(x)}=\alpha\quad\hbox{uniformly in}\ \ x\in\mathbb{R},$ (4.3) where $e(t)=e^{\lambda_{c}t}$ and $\lambda=\lambda_{c}$ if $c>c^{*}_{R}$ , while $e(t)=-te^{\lambda^{*}t}$ and $\lambda=\lambda^{*}$ if $c=c^{*}_{R}$ . Let us give a brief summary of the structure of the proof.
Although the system ( 4.2 ) is neither parabolic nor uniformly elliptic, the strong maximum principle still holds for ( 4.2 ), because the original equation is uniformly parabolic. Therefore, we can establish the sweeping principle (Proposition 4.1 ), which applies to a family of subsolutions $\big(\underline{U}_{\mu},\underline{V}_{\mu}\big)$ and supersolutions $\big(\overline{U}_{\mu},\overline{V}_{\mu}\big)$ , to show that $\big(\underline{U}_{0},\underline{V}_{0}\big)\leq\big(\overline{U}_{0},\overline{V}_{0}\big)$ implies $\big(\underline{U}_{1},\underline{V}_{1}\big)\leq\big(\overline{U}_{1},\overline{V}_{1}\big)$ .
Then, we use this result to derive an analogue of the sliding method (Proposition 4.2 ).
These two Propositions,
along with the Harnack inequality derived in Lemma 4.3 , constitute our main tools to establish the rate of decay along the leading edges of traveling waves. Then, we start analyzing the exponential behavior of the leading edges of traveling waves. Proposition 4.4 is devoted to a rough exponential lower bound, while Proposition 4.5 establishes a rough exponential upper bound. The proofs are quite similar and rely on the same idea. For the lower bound, suppose by contradiction that there exists a sequence of points $(t_{n},x_{n})$ with $t_{n}\to-\infty$ such that $\big(U(t_{n}),V(t_{n})\big)$ converge to 0 faster than expected, i.e. $\min\big(U(t_{n},x_{n}),V(t_{n},x_{n})\big)\leq C_{n}e^{\lambda t_{n}},$ where $\lambda$ is the smallest positive solution of the equation $\lambda c=k(\lambda)$ (here we explain the argument for super-critical speeds,
which is simpler). By using a supersolution of the type $\big(\overline{U}(t,x),\overline{V}(t,x)\big):=e^{\lambda t}\big(\varphi^{\lambda}(x),\psi^{\lambda}(x)\big)$ and the Harnack inequality, we can use the sliding method on the interval $[t_{n},0]$ to show that $\min\big(U(0,x_{n}),V(0,x_{n})\big)\leq\tilde{C}_{n},$ where $\tilde{C}_{n}$ converges to $0$ as $n\to+\infty$ . Taking the limit, this is obviously a contradiction,
since $\big(U(0,x),V(0,x)\big)$ are uniformly positive because of the periodicity.
We proceed similarly for the upper bound. The technical details are slightly more involved for the critical case $c=c^{*}_{R}$ . To summarize, we use a supersolution to derive a rough lower bound, and a subsolution to derive a rough upper bound, which may sound slightly unusual. As we shall see below, for a more refined lower bound, we use a family of subsolutions. In Lemma 4.6 , we prove the stronger property that $\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\min\left(\frac{U(t,x)}{e^{\lambda t}\varphi^{\lambda}(x)},\frac{V(t,x)}{e^{\lambda t}\psi^{\lambda}(x)}\right)$ converges to a positive limit,
say $\alpha$ , as $t\to-\infty$ .
This is done by a family of subsolutions and the sweeping method (Proposition 4.1 ). To apply the sweeping method, we have to show that the starting member of the family of subsolutions lies below the traveling wave, which is guaranteed by the rough lower bound in Proposition 4.4 . Again, the method is the same for the critical speed, but the computations are more involved.
Note that, by shifting the time, we may assume without loss of generality that $\alpha=1$ in the above limit. Finally we prove ( 4.3 ) with $\alpha=1$ by showing that $\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\max\left(\frac{U(t,x)}{e^{\lambda t}\varphi^{\lambda}(x)},\frac{V(t,x)}{e^{\lambda t}\psi^{\lambda}(x)}\right)\to 1\quad\hbox{as}\ \ t\to-\infty.$ This is done by a contradiction argument and the strong maximum principle. The above strategy is, for the most part, similar to [ 17 ] . However, there are some important differences.
Among other things, since the traveling wave does not necessarily lie in the cooperative region, we do not know if the traveling wave is increasing in $t$ entirely, which is in marked contrast with the scalar case.
Another difficulty, which is also seen in the construction of the traveling wave in section 3 , is that the two components of the subsolutions $\big(\underline{U}(t,x),\underline{V}(t,x)\big)$ do not necessarily become negative at the same position, therefore, we have to pay attention to the behavior of $\big(\underline{U}(t,x),\underline{V}(t,x)\big)$ even where they are negative. We now proceed to the proof.
We start with a comparison principle. Proposition 4.1 (The sweeping method) . Let $\sigma$ be a $C^{1}$ , positive and $L$ -periodic function,
and $\big(\overline{U}_{\mu}(t,x),\overline{V}_{\mu}(t,x)\big)$ and $\big(\underline{U}_{\mu}(t,x),\underline{V}_{\mu}(t,x)\big)$ be continuous with respect to $\mu\in[0,1]$ , $L$ -periodic in $x$ and satisfy respectively $\begin{split}&c\overline{U}_{t}-\sigma(x)\big(\overline{U}_{tt}-2\overline{U}_{tx}+\overline{U}_{xx}\big)+\sigma_{x}(x)\big(\overline{U}_{t}-\overline{U}_{x}\big)\geq g_{u}(x,\overline{U},\overline{V}),\\
&c\overline{V}_{t}-\sigma(x)\big(\overline{V}_{tt}-2\overline{V}_{tx}+\overline{V}_{xx}\big)+\sigma_{x}(x)\big(\overline{V}_{t}-\overline{V}_{x}\big)\geq g_{v}(x,\overline{U},\overline{V}),\end{split}$ (4.4) and $\begin{split}&c\underline{U}_{t}-\sigma(x)\big(\underline{U}_{tt}-2\underline{U}_{tx}+\underline{U}_{xx}\big)+\sigma_{x}(x)\big(\underline{U}_{t}-\underline{U}_{x}\big)\leq g_{u}(x,\underline{U},\underline{V}),\\
&c\underline{V}_{t}-\sigma(x)\big(\underline{V}_{tt}-2\underline{V}_{tx}+\underline{V}_{xx}\big)+\sigma_{x}(x)\big(\underline{V}_{t}-\underline{V}_{x}\big)\leq g_{v}(x,\underline{U},\underline{V}),\end{split}$ (4.5) in the classical sense, for all $\mu\in[0,1]$ , $t\in[T_{1},T_{2}]$ and $x\in\mathbb{R}$ , where $g=(g_{u},g_{v})$ is a cooperative nonlinearity that is also locally Lipschitz in $(U,V)$ and $L$ -periodic in $x$ . Suppose that the following three statements hold true. $\underline{U}_{\mu}(T_{1},x)<\overline{U}_{\mu}(T_{1},x),\quad\underline{V}_{\mu}(T_{1},x)<\overline{V}_{\mu}(T_{1},x),\,^{\forall}x\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }\,^{\forall}\mu\in[0,1),$ (4.6a) $\underline{U}_{\mu}(T_{2},x)<\overline{U}_{\mu}(T_{2},x),\quad\underline{V}_{\mu}(T_{2},x)<\overline{V}_{\mu}(T_{2},x),\,^{\forall}x\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }\,^{\forall}\mu\in[0,1),$ (4.6b) $\underline{U}_{0}(t,x)\leq\overline{U}_{0}(t,x),\quad\underline{V}_{0}(t,x)\leq\overline{V}_{0}(t,x),\,^{\forall}(t,x)\in[T_{1},T_{2}]\times\mathbb{R}.$ (4.6c) Then, $\underline{U}_{1}(t,x)\leq\overline{U}_{1}(t,x)\text{ and }\underline{V}_{1}(t,x)\leq\overline{V}_{1}(t,x),\text{ for all }(t,x)\in[T_{1},T_{2}]\times\mathbb{R}.$ (4.7) If $\overline{U}_{\mu}>0$ and $\overline{V}_{\mu}>0$ for all $\mu\in[0,1)$ ,
then the inequalities ( 4.5 ) need to be satisfied only on the set of $(\mu,t,x)$ where either $\underline{U}_{\mu}(t,x)>0$ or $\underline{V}_{\mu}(t,x)>0$ . Proof. Let us define $\mu^{*}:=\sup\{\mu\geq 0\>:\>\underline{U}_{\mu}(t,x)\leq\overline{U}_{\mu}(t,x)\text{ and }\underline{V}_{\mu}(t,x)\leq\overline{V}_{\mu}(t,x),\,^{\forall}(t,x)\in[T_{1},T_{2}]\}.$ (4.8) Our goal is to show that $\mu^{*}=1$ , which would establish ( 4.7 ). Assume by contradiction that $\mu^{*}<1$ . Then there exists a sequence of points $(\mu_{n},t_{n},x_{n})\in(\mu^{*},1]\times[T_{1},T_{2}]\times\mathbb{R}$ with $\mu_{n}\to\mu^{*}$ as $n\to+\infty$ , such that $\text{ either }\underline{U}_{\mu_{n}}(t_{n},x_{n})>\overline{U}_{\mu_{n}}(t_{n},x_{n})\text{ or }\underline{V}_{\mu_{n}}(t_{n},x_{n})>\overline{V}_{\mu_{n}}(t_{n},x_{n}),\qquad\,^{\forall}n\in\mathbb{N}.$ (4.9) We will assume the former, namely that $\underline{U}_{\mu_{n}}(t_{n},x_{n})>\overline{U}_{\mu_{n}}(t_{n},x_{n})$ ; the argument is similar if $\underline{V}_{\mu_{n}}(t_{n},x_{n})>\overline{V}_{\mu_{n}}(t_{n},x_{n})$ . By using the compactness of $(t,x)\in[T_{1},T_{2}]\times[0,L]$ , we may assume that $t_{n}\to t^{*}\in[T_{1},T_{2}]$ and $x_{n}\to x^{*}\in[0,L]$ . Using ( 4.8 ) we deduce that $\underline{U}_{\mu^{*}}(t^{*},x^{*})=\overline{U}_{\mu^{*}}(t^{*},x^{*})\text{ and }\underline{V}_{\mu^{*}}(t^{*},x^{*})\leq\overline{V}_{\mu^{*}}(t^{*},x^{*}).$ (4.10) Next, we deduce from our assumption ( 4.6a ) that $t^{*}>T_{1}$ and we deduce from ( 4.6b ) that $t^{*}<T_{2}$ . We define the functions $\overline{u}({t},x):=\overline{U}_{\mu^{*}}(ct-x,x)$ , $\overline{v}({t},x):=\overline{V}_{\mu^{*}}(ct-x,x)$ , $\underline{u}({t},x):=\underline{U}_{\mu^{*}}(ct-x,x)$ , $\underline{v}({t},x):=\underline{V}_{\mu^{*}}(ct-x,x)$ in the strip $\frac{T_{1}+{x}}{c}\leq{t}\leq\frac{T_{2}+{x}}{c}$ (and $x\in\mathbb{R}$ ). Then $(\overline{u},\overline{v})$ and $(\underline{u},\underline{v})$ satisfy $\displaystyle\overline{u}_{t}-\big(\sigma(x)\overline{u}_{x}\big)_{x}$ $\displaystyle\geq g_{u}(x,\overline{u},\overline{v}),$ (4.11) $\displaystyle\overline{v}_{t}-\big(\sigma(x)\overline{v}_{x}\big)_{x}$ $\displaystyle\geq g_{v}(x,\overline{u},\overline{v}),$ and $\displaystyle\underline{u}_{t}-\big(\sigma(x)\underline{u}_{x}\big)_{x}$ $\displaystyle\leq g_{u}(x,\underline{u},\underline{v}),$ (4.12) $\displaystyle\underline{v}_{t}-\big(\sigma(x)\underline{v}_{x}\big)_{x}$ $\displaystyle\leq g_{v}(x,\underline{u},\underline{v}).$ It follows from ( 4.10 ) that $\overline{u}(\tilde{t}^{*}+\tau^{*}/c,x^{*})=\underline{u}(\tilde{t}^{*},x^{*})$ and $\overline{v}(\tilde{t}^{*}+\tau^{*}/c,x^{*})\leq\underline{v}(\tilde{t}^{*},x^{*})$ , where $\tilde{t}^{*}:=\frac{t^{*}+x^{*}}{c}$ ; and the point $(\tilde{t}^{*},x^{*})$ is interior to the strip $\frac{T_{1}+{x}}{c}<{t}<\frac{T_{2}-\tau^{*}+{x}}{c}$ . It follows from the strong maximum principle for cooperative parabolic systems (see e.g. [ 24 , Chap. 3 Theorem 13 p.190] ) that $\overline{u}(t/c,x)\equiv\underline{u}(t,x),\text{ for all }(t,x)\text{ such that }\frac{T_{1}+{x}}{c}<{t}<\frac{T_{2}+{x}}{c}.$ (4.13) Coming back to $\overline{U}_{\mu^{*}}$ and $\underline{U}_{\mu^{*}}$ and using the periodicity in $x$ ,
one gets $\overline{U}_{\mu^{*}}(t,\cdot)\equiv\underline{U}_{\mu^{*}}(t,\cdot)$ for $t\in[T_{1},t^{*}]$ , which contradicts ( 4.6a ). We have shown that $\mu^{*}=1$ , which proves ( 4.7 ). Moreover, if we assume that $\overline{U}_{\mu}>0$ and $\overline{V}_{\mu}>0$ for all $\mu\in[0,1)$ , then it is clear that the same argument as above applies, if we simply assume that $(\underline{U}_{\mu},\underline{V}_{\mu})$ satisfies the inequalities ( 4.5 ) in the region where either $\underline{U}_{\mu}(t,x)>0$ or $\underline{V}_{\mu}(t,x)>0$ . This proves the last part of Proposition 4.1 . The proof is complete.
∎ A direct consequence of Proposition 4.1 is the following result, known as the “sliding method”. Proposition 4.2 (The sliding method) . Let Assumption 1 hold true, and $\big(\overline{U}(t,x),\overline{V}(t,x)\big)$ and $\big(\underline{U}(t,x),\underline{V}(t,x)\big)$ be $L$ -periodic in $x$ and satisfy
respectively ( 4.4 ) and ( 4.5 )
in the classical sense, for all $t\in[T_{1},T_{2}]$ and $x\in\mathbb{R}$ , where $g=(g_{u},g_{v})$ is cooperative nonlinearity that is also locally Lipschitz in $(U,V)$ and $L$ -periodic in $x$ . Suppose that: 1. Either $\underline{U}(T_{1},x)<\overline{U}(t,x),\quad\underline{V}(T_{1},x)<\overline{V}(t,x),\,^{\forall}(t,x)\in(T_{1},T_{2}]\times\mathbb{R},$ (4.14a) and $\overline{U}(T_{2},x)>\underline{U}(t,x),\quad\overline{V}(T_{2},x)>\underline{V}(t,x),\,^{\forall}(t,x)\in[T_{1},T_{2})\times\mathbb{R};$ (4.14b) 2. Or, $\underline{U}(T_{2},x)<\overline{U}(t,x),\quad\underline{V}(T_{2},x)<\overline{V}(t,x),\,^{\forall}(t,x)\in[T_{1},T_{2})\times\mathbb{R},$ (4.15a) and $\overline{U}(T_{1},x)>\underline{U}(t,x),\quad\overline{V}(T_{1},x)>\underline{V}(t,x),\,^{\forall}(t,x)\in(T_{1},T_{2}]\times\mathbb{R};$ (4.15b) Then $\overline{U}(t,x)\geq\underline{U}(t,x)\text{ and }\overline{V}(t,x)\geq\underline{V}(t,x),\text{ for all }(t,x)\in[T_{1},T_{2}]\times\mathbb{R}.$ (4.16) Proof. To prove assertion $1$ , we apply the sweeping method (Proposition 4.1 ) to the family of sub- and supersolutions defined by $\big(\underline{U}_{\mu}(t,x),\underline{V}_{\mu}(t,x)\big)=\big(\underline{U}(t,x),\underline{V}(t,x)\big)$ and $\big(\overline{U}_{\mu}(t,x),\overline{V}_{\mu}(t,x)\big)=\big(\overline{U}(t+(1-\mu)(T_{2}-T_{1}),x),\overline{V}(t+(1-\mu)(T_{2}-T_{1}),x)\big)$ . The proof of assertion $2$ is similar to that of assertion $1$ , by using the family $\big(\underline{U}_{\mu}(t,x),\underline{V}_{\mu}(t,x)\big)=\big(\underline{U}(t+(1-\mu)(T_{2}-T_{1}),x),\underline{V}(t+(1-\mu)(T_{2}-T_{1}),x)\big)$ and $\big(\overline{U}_{\mu}(t,x),\overline{V}_{\mu}(t,x)\big)=\big(\overline{U}(t,x),\overline{V}(t,x)\big)$ .
∎ Lemma 4.3 (Harnack-type estimate) . Let Assumption 1 hold true and let $c\geq c^{*}_{R}$ be fixed. There exists a constant $H>0$ such that for any classical solution $\big(U(t,x),V(t,x)\big)$ of ( 4.2 ) with $(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$ , one has $\min\big(U(t,x),V(t,x)\big)\geq H^{-1}\max\big(U(t,y),V(t,y)\big)$ (4.17) for any $t\in\mathbb{R}$ and all $(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$ . Proof. We come back to the original variables by setting $u(t,x)=U(ct-x,x)$ and $v(t,x)=V(ct-x,x)$ ; then $(u,v)$ is an entire solution of the parabolic system ( 1.1 ). In order to obtain ( 4.17 ), it is sufficient to establish that $\underset{x\in[-2L,L]}{\inf_{s\in[t/c,(t+L)/c]}}\min\big(u(s,x),v(s,x)\big)\geq H^{-1}\underset{x\in[-2L,L]}{\sup_{s\in[(t-2L)/c,(t-L)/c]}}\max\big(u(s,x),v(s,x)\big).$ (4.18) The Harnack inequality established by Földes and Poláčik [ 9 , Theorem 3.9] provides a constant $\kappa$ depending only on $\theta$ such that for any $\tau\in\mathbb{R}$ , $\underset{x\in[-2L,L]}{\inf_{s\in[\tau+7/2\theta,\tau+4\theta]}}\min\big(u(s,x),v(s,x)\big)\geq\kappa\underset{x\in[-2L,L]}{\sup_{s\in[\tau+\theta,\tau+2\theta]}}\max\big(u(s,x),v(s,x)\big).$ (4.19) Selecting $\theta=L/c$ and $\tau=(t-3L)/c$ we obtain ( 4.18 ) with $H=\frac{1}{\kappa}$ and therefore ( 4.17 ).
∎ We can now obtain a lower bound of the leading edge of the traveling wave. Proposition 4.4 (Lower rough bound of the leading edge) . Let Assumption 1 hold true and let $c\geq c^{*}_{R}$ be fixed. Let $\big(U(t,x),V(t,x)\big)$ be a classical, nonnegative and nontrivial solution of ( 4.2 ). Then, 1. if $c>c^{*}_{R}$ , there is a constant $C>0$ and $T\in\mathbb{R}$ such that $U(t,x)\geq Ce^{\lambda_{c}t}\varphi^{\lambda_{c}}(x)\text{ and }V(t,x)\geq Ce^{\lambda_{c}t}\psi^{\lambda_{c}}(x),\qquad\,^{\forall}t\leq T,\,^{\forall}x\in\mathbb{R},$ (4.20) where $\lambda_{c}>0$ is the smallest positive solution of $\lambda_{c}c=k(\lambda_{c})$ ; 2. if $c=c^{*}_{R}$ , there is a constant $C>0$ and $T\in\mathbb{R}$ such that $U(t,x)\geq C|t|e^{\lambda^{*}t}\varphi^{\lambda^{*}}(x)\text{ and }V(t,x)\geq C|t|e^{\lambda^{*}t}\psi^{\lambda^{*}}(x),\qquad\,^{\forall}t\leq T,\,^{\forall}x\in\mathbb{R},$ (4.21) where $\lambda^{*}>0$ is the smallest positive solution of $\lambda^{*}c=k(\lambda^{*})$ . Proof. Let us prove ( 4.20 ) first. Assume by contradiction that ( 4.20 ) does not hold, then there exist sequences $t_{n}\to-\infty$ , $x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}$ and $C_{n}\to 0$ such that $U(t_{n},x_{n})\leq C_{n}e^{\lambda_{c}t_{n}}\varphi^{\lambda_{c}}(x_{n})\text{ or }V(t_{n},x_{n})\leq C_{n}e^{\lambda_{c}t_{n}}\psi^{\lambda_{c}}(x_{n}).$ By the Harnack inequality ( 4.17 ) in Lemma 4.3 we deduce $U(t_{n},x)<\widetilde{C}_{n}e^{\lambda_{c}t_{n}}\varphi^{\lambda_{c}}(x)\text{ and }V(t_{n},x)<\widetilde{C}_{n}e^{\lambda_{c}t_{n}}\psi^{\lambda_{c}}(x),\qquad\,^{\forall}x\in\mathbb{R},$ (4.22) where $\widetilde{C}_{n}:=2H\frac{\max(\varphi^{\lambda_{c}},\psi^{\lambda_{c}})}{\min(\varphi^{\lambda_{c}},\psi^{\lambda_{c}})}C_{n}$ . It follows from elementary computations that the vector function $\big(\overline{U}(t,x),\overline{V}(t,x)\big):=\widetilde{C}_{n}e^{\lambda_{c}t}\big(\varphi^{\lambda_{c}}(x),\psi^{\lambda_{c}}(x)\big)$ satisfies ( 4.4 ) with $g_{u}(x,u,v)=u\big(r_{u}(x)-\mu_{u}(x)\big)+\mu_{v}(x)v$ and $g_{v}(x,u,v)=v\big(r_{v}(x)-\mu_{v}(x)\big)+\mu_{u}(x)u$ (there is even equality), and $\big(U(t,x),V(t,x)\big)$ satisfies ( 4.5 ) with the same $g$ , which is cooperative. Moreover, $\big(U(t,x),V(t,x)\big)$ is globally bounded by a constant $K>0$ and there exists $T^{\prime}\geq 0$ such that $\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\min\big(\overline{U}(T^{\prime},x),\overline{V}(T^{\prime},x)\big)>K$ . Thus we may apply assertion $1$ in Proposition 4.2 to find $U(t,x)\leq\widetilde{C}_{n}e^{\lambda_{c}t}\varphi^{\lambda_{c}}(x)\text{ and }V(t,x)\leq\widetilde{C}_{n}e^{\lambda_{c}t}\psi^{\lambda_{c}}(x),\qquad\,^{\forall}t\in[t_{n},T^{\prime}],\,^{\forall}x\in\mathbb{R}.$ Since $t_{n}\to-\infty$ and $T^{\prime}\geq 0$ , we obtain in particular $U(0,x)\leq\widetilde{C}_{n}\varphi^{\lambda_{c}}(x)\text{ and }V(t,x)\leq\widetilde{C}_{n}\psi^{\lambda_{c}}(x),\qquad\,^{\forall}t\in[t_{n},T^{\prime}],\,^{\forall}x\in\mathbb{R}.$ (4.23) Letting $n\to\infty$ and recalling $\widetilde{C}_{n}\to 0$ , we obtain $U(0,x)=0\text{ and }V(0,x)=0,\qquad\,^{\forall}x\in\mathbb{R}.$ This is obviously a contradiction, which proves ( 4.20 ). Next we prove ( 4.21 ). We follow the same steps. Assume by contradiction that ( 4.21 ) does not hold, then there exist sequences $t_{n}\to-\infty$ , $x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}$ and $C_{n}\to 0$ such that $U(t_{n},x_{n})\leq C_{n}|t_{n}|e^{\lambda^{*}t_{n}}\varphi^{\lambda^{*}}(x_{n})\text{ or }V(t_{n},x_{n})\leq C_{n}|t_{n}|e^{\lambda^{*}t_{n}}\psi^{\lambda^{*}}(x_{n}).$ Up to increasing $C_{n}$ , we will assume that $C_{n}t_{n}\to-\infty$ while keeping $C_{n}\to 0$ (for instance, we define $\tilde{C}_{n}:=\max(C_{n},1/\sqrt{-t_{n}})$ and drop the tilde).
Next, recall the upper barrier $\big(\overline{u}^{*}(t,x),\overline{v}^{*}(t,x)\big)$ defined in ( 3.16 ).
Setting $\big(\overline{U}^{*}(t,x),\overline{V}^{*}(t,x)\big):=\big(\overline{u}^{*}(\frac{t+x}{c},x),\overline{v}^{*}(\frac{t+x}{c},x)\big)$ this upper barrier in the new variables
satisfies the following: $\begin{split}&\overline{U}^{*}(t,x)=(-t+\alpha)e^{\lambda^{*}t}\varphi^{\lambda^{*}}(x)-e^{\lambda^{*}t}\partial_{\lambda}\varphi^{\lambda^{*}}(x),\\
&\overline{V}^{*}(t,x)=(-t+\alpha)e^{\lambda^{*}t}\psi^{\lambda^{*}}(x)-e^{\lambda^{*}t}\partial_{\lambda}\psi^{\lambda^{*}}(x).\end{split}$ (4.24) We assume that $\alpha>0$ is sufficiently large, so that (1) $\inf\min\big(\overline{U}^{*}(-1/\lambda^{*},x),\overline{V}^{*}(-1/\lambda^{*},x)\big)>K$ (the value $t=-1/\lambda_{c}$ is chosen as the maximum of the function $t\mapsto|t|e^{\lambda^{*}t}$ for $t\in(-\infty,0]$ ); and (2) $\alpha\geq\sup_{x}\max\left(\frac{\partial_{\lambda}\varphi^{\lambda^{*}}(x)}{\varphi^{\lambda^{*}}(x)},\frac{\partial_{\lambda}\psi^{\lambda^{*}}(x)}{\psi^{\lambda^{*}}(x)}\right)$ , which implies that $t\mapsto\big(\overline{U}^{*}(t,x),\overline{V}^{*}(t,x)\big)$ is decreasing on $(-\infty,-1/\lambda^{*}]$ and strictly positive on this interval. More precisely, $\varphi(z)=C_{1}^{+}e^{X_{1}^{+}z}+C_{1}^{-}e^{X_{1}^{-}z},\text{ where }X_{1}^{\pm}=\lambda\pm\sqrt{k+\frac{\mu}{\varepsilon\delta}-1},$ and the constants $C_{1}^{+},C_{1}^{-}$ satisfy $C_{1}^{+}+C_{1}^{-}=\varphi(0)$ and $C_{1}^{+}X_{1}^{+}+C_{1}^{-}X_{1}^{-}=\varphi^{\prime}(0).$ Consequently, $\varphi(z)=C_{1}^{+}\left(1+X_{1}^{+}z+O\left((X_{1}^{+}z)^{2}\right)\right)+C_{1}^{-}\left(1+X_{1}^{-}z+O\left((X_{1}^{-}z)^{2}\right)\right)=\varphi(0)+z\varphi^{\prime}(0)+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right)$ for all $0\leq z\leq\varepsilon$ .
In particular, $\varphi(\varepsilon)=\varphi(0)+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right).$ (6.5) Integrating the first equation in ( 6.4 ) over $[0,\varepsilon]$ and using the above estimate, we obtain $\varphi^{\prime}(\varepsilon)=\varphi^{\prime}(0)+2\lambda\left(\varphi(\varepsilon)-\varphi(0)\right)+\frac{\mu}{\delta}\varphi(0)+O(\varepsilon)=\varphi^{\prime}(0)+\frac{\mu}{\delta}\varphi(0)+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right).$ (6.6) Next we note that the function $\Psi(z):=\varphi(z)+\psi(z)$ satisfies the following: $\Psi^{\prime\prime}-2\lambda\Psi^{\prime}+(\lambda^{2}+r-k)\Psi=(r-1)\varphi.$ Since the coefficients of the above equation are independent of $\varepsilon,\delta$ , we have $\Psi(\varepsilon)=\Psi(0)+O(\varepsilon),\quad\Psi^{\prime}(\varepsilon)=\Psi^{\prime}(0)+O(\varepsilon).$ Combining this with ( 6.5 ) and ( 6.6 ), we obtain $\psi(\varepsilon)=\psi(0)+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right),\quad\psi^{\prime}(\varepsilon)=\psi^{\prime}(0)-\frac{\mu}{\delta}\varphi(0)+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right).$ (6.7) Analysis on the interval $[\varepsilon,\delta-\varepsilon]$ On this interval, the solution $(\varphi,\psi)$ of ( EPλ ) simply satisfies the following equations: $\begin{cases}\varphi^{\prime\prime}-2\lambda\varphi^{\prime}+(\lambda^{2}+1)\varphi=k\varphi,\\[2.0pt]
\psi^{\prime\prime}-2\lambda\psi^{\prime}+(\lambda^{2}+r)\psi=k\psi,\end{cases}$ (6.8) along with the initial conditions $\varphi(\varepsilon)=\varphi(0)+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right),\ \ \varphi^{\prime}(\varepsilon)=\varphi^{\prime}(0)+\frac{\mu}{\delta}\varphi(0)+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right),$ $\psi(\varepsilon)=\psi(0)+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right),\ \ \psi^{\prime}(\varepsilon)=\psi^{\prime}(0)-\frac{\mu}{\delta}\varphi(0)+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right).$ Therefore $\begin{split}\varphi(\delta-\varepsilon)&=\varphi(\varepsilon)+(\delta-2\varepsilon)\varphi^{\prime}(\varepsilon)+\frac{1}{2}(\delta-2\varepsilon)^{2}\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\varepsilon)+\cdots\\
&=\varphi(0)+(\delta-2\varepsilon)\left(\varphi^{\prime}(0)+\frac{\mu}{\delta}\varphi(0)\right)+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right)+O(\delta^{2})\\[2.0pt]
&=(1+\mu)\varphi(0)+O(\delta)+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right),\end{split}$ $\begin{split}\varphi^{\prime}(\delta-\varepsilon)&=\varphi^{\prime}(\varepsilon)+(\delta-2\varepsilon)\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\varepsilon)+\cdots\\
&=\varphi^{\prime}(\varepsilon)+(\delta-2\varepsilon)\left(2\lambda\varphi^{\prime}(\varepsilon)-(\lambda^{2}+1-k)\varphi(\varepsilon)\right)+\cdots\\[2.0pt]
&=\varphi^{\prime}(0)+\frac{\mu}{\delta}\varphi(0)+2\lambda\mu\varphi(0)+O(\delta)+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right).\end{split}$ Similarly, we have $\begin{split}\psi(\delta-\varepsilon)&=\psi(0)-\mu\varphi(0)+O(\delta)+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right),\\
\psi^{\prime}(\delta-\varepsilon)&=\psi^{\prime}(0)-\frac{\mu}{\delta}\varphi(0)-2\lambda\mu\varphi(0)+O(\delta)+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right).\end{split}$ Combining these, and arguing as in the derivation of ( 6.5 ), ( 6.6 ) and ( 6.7 ), we obtain $\begin{cases}\,\varphi(\delta)=(1+\mu)\varphi(0)+O(\delta)+O\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right),\\[4.0pt]
\,\varphi^{\prime}(\delta)=\varphi^{\prime}(0)+\dfrac{\mu}{\delta}\left(\varphi(0)-\psi(\delta)\right)+2\lambda\mu\varphi(0)+O(\delta)+O\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right),\\[6.0pt]
\,\psi(\delta)=\psi(0)-\mu\varphi(0)+O(\delta)+O\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right),\\[6.0pt]
\,\psi^{\prime}(\delta)=\psi^{\prime}(0)-\dfrac{\mu}{\delta}\left(\varphi(0)-\psi(\delta)\right)-2\lambda\mu\varphi(0)+O(\delta)+O\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right).\end{cases}$ (6.9) In what follows we assume $\varepsilon=O(\delta^{2})$ , so the term $O(\delta)+O\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta}\right)$ is simply expressed as $O(\delta)$ . Let us normalize $(\varphi,\psi)$ so that $\varphi(0)=1\ \left(\,=\varphi(L)\,\right).$ (6.10) The first line of ( 6.9 ) then implies $\varphi(\delta)=1+\mu+O(\delta).$ (6.11) Note that $\varphi$ satisfies the first line of ( 6.8 ) on $\delta<z<L$ , which we can rewrite as $\left(e^{-\lambda z}\varphi\right)^{\prime\prime}=(k-1)\left(e^{-\lambda z}\varphi\right)\quad(\delta<z<L).$ (6.12) As $k$ satisfies the inequality ( 6.2 ), we have $k-1>0$ .
Therefore $e^{-\lambda z}\varphi$ is convex on $[\delta,L]$ , hence its maximum is attained either at $z=\delta$ or at $z=L$ . This and ( 6.10 ), ( 6.11 ) imply $0<\varphi(z)\leq(1+\mu)e^{\lambda L}+O(\delta)\quad(z\in[\delta,L]).$ (6.13) Hence $\varphi|_{[\delta,L]}$ is uniformly bounded as $\varepsilon,\delta\to 0$ .
Integrating the first line of ( 6.8 ), we get $\varphi^{\prime}(L)-\varphi^{\prime}(\delta)=2\lambda(\varphi(L)-\varphi(\delta))+(k-\lambda^{2}-1)\int_{\delta}^{L}\varphi(z)\mathrm{d}z.$ The right-hand side of the above identity is uniformly bounded as $\varepsilon,\delta\to 0$ . This and the second line of ( 6.9 ),
together with $\varphi^{\prime}(L)=\varphi^{\prime}(0)$ , implies $\varphi(0)-\psi(\delta)=O(\delta)$ , thus $\psi(\delta)=1+O(\delta).$ (6.14) Combining this and the third line of ( 6.9 ), and recalling $\varepsilon=O(\delta^{2})$ , we get $\psi(0)=1+\mu+O(\delta).$ (6.15) Note also that, by summing up the second and the fourth lines of ( 6.9 ), we obtain $\varphi^{\prime}(\delta)+\psi^{\prime}(\delta)=\varphi^{\prime}(0)+\psi^{\prime}(0)+O(\delta).$ (6.16) 6.2 Analysis of the limit problem Uniform derivative estimates Let us study the limit problem of ( EPλ ) with ( 2.36 ) as $\varepsilon,\delta\to 0$ . As before, we impose $\varepsilon=O(\delta^{2}).$ For each $\varepsilon,\delta>0$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$ , let $k(z;\lambda,\varepsilon,\delta)$ and $(\varphi(z;\lambda,\varepsilon,\delta),\psi(z;\lambda,\varepsilon,\delta))$ denote, respectively, the principal eigenvalue and the principal eigenvector of ( EPλ ) with ( 2.36 ) under the normalization condition $\varphi(0;\lambda,\varepsilon,\delta)=1$ as before (see ( 6.10 )).
Let us first show that $\varphi,\varphi^{\prime},\psi,\psi^{\prime}$ are uniformly bounded on $[\delta,L]$ as $\varepsilon,\delta\to 0$ . The uniform boundedness of $\varphi$ is already shown in ( 6.13 ). In order to prove the uniform boundedness of $\varphi^{\prime}$ , we prepare the following lemma. Lemma 6.1 . Let $\Phi$ be a $C^{2}$ function on an interval $[a,b]$ satisfying the equation $\Phi^{\prime\prime}(z)=F(z)\quad(a<z<b).$ (6.17) Then the following estimates hold: $\begin{split}&|\Phi(z)|\leq\max\left(|\Phi(a)|,|\Phi(b)|\right)+\frac{(b-z)(z-a)}{b-a}\int_{a}^{b}|\Phi(\xi)|\mathrm{d}\xi,\\
&|\Phi^{\prime}(z)|\leq\frac{\left|\Phi(b)-\Phi(a)\right|}{b-a}+\int_{a}^{b}|\Phi(\xi)|\mathrm{d}\xi.\end{split}$ (6.18) Proof. The solution of the equation ( 6.17 ) is given in the following form $\Phi(z)=\frac{(b-z)\Phi(a)+(z-a)\Phi(b)}{b-a}+\int_{a}^{b}G(z,\xi)F(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi.$ where $G(z,\xi)$ denotes the Green’s function on the interval $[a,b]$ , namely $G(z,\xi)=\begin{cases}\,-\dfrac{(\xi-a)(b-z)}{b-a}&(\hbox{if}\ z\geq\xi)\\[6.0pt]
\,-\dfrac{(z-a)(b-\xi)}{b-a}&(\hbox{if}\ z<\xi)\end{cases}$ The estimates ( 6.18 ) follow easily from the above expression, so we omit the details.
∎ Now let us prove the uniform boundedness of $\varphi^{\prime}$ . Since $e^{-\lambda z}\varphi(z)$ satisfies the equation ( 6.12 ), we see from the above lemma that $e^{-\lambda z}\varphi(z)$ and $\left(e^{-\lambda z}\varphi(z)\right)^{\prime}$ are uniformly bounded. Consequently $\varphi^{\prime}$ is uniformly bounded on $[0,\delta]$ as $\varepsilon,\delta\to 0$ . Next we prove the uniform boundedness of $\psi$ , $\psi^{\prime}$ .
Note that $\psi$ satisfies the second line of ( 6.8 ) on $\delta<z<L$ , which we can rewrite as $\left(e^{-\lambda z}\psi\right)^{\prime\prime}=(k-r)\left(e^{-\lambda z}\psi\right)\quad(\delta<z<L).$ (6.19) If $k\geq r$ , then $\left(e^{-\lambda z}\psi\right)$ is convex. This, together with ( 6.14 ) and ( 6.15 ), implies that $\psi$ is uniformly bounded. Arguing as we did for $\varphi$ , we obtain the uniform boundedness of $\psi^{\prime}$ . Next, if $k<r$ , by summing up the two equations in ( EPλ ) and integrating over $[0,L]$ , we obtain $\begin{split}&(k-\lambda^{2}-1)\int_{0}^{L}\varphi(z)\mathrm{d}z=(\lambda^{2}+r-k)\int_{0}^{L}\psi(z)\mathrm{d}z.\\
&\ \ \hbox{Hence}\ \ \ (r-\lambda^{2}-1)\int_{0}^{L}\varphi(z)\mathrm{d}z>\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{L}\psi(z)\mathrm{d}z.\end{split}$ Since the left-hand side of the above inequality is uniformly bounded as $\varepsilon,\delta\to 0$ , so is the right-hand side. In particular, $\int_{\delta}^{L}\psi(z)\mathrm{d}z$ is uniformly bounded as $\varepsilon,\delta\to 0$ . Combining this with ( 6.14 ), ( 6.15 ), and applying Lemma 6.1 , we see that $\psi,\psi^{\prime}$ are uniformly bounded on $[\delta,L]$ as $\varepsilon.\delta\to 0$ . Formulation of the limit problem Now we let $\varepsilon,\delta\to 0$ while keeping $\varepsilon=O(\delta^{2})$ . The interval $[0,\delta]$ shrinks to the point $z=0$ .
Since $\varphi,\varphi^{\prime},\psi,\psi^{\prime}$ are uniformly bounded as $\varepsilon,\delta\to 0$ , we see, by the bootstrap argument for ( 6.8 ) that $\varphi^{\prime\prime},\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime},\psi^{\prime\prime},\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}$ are also uniformly bounded on $[\delta,L]$ as $\varepsilon,\delta\to 0$ . Note also that, by ( 6.2 ), the eigenvalue $k(\lambda;\varepsilon,\delta)$ varies within the range $\lambda^{2}+1<k(\lambda;\varepsilon,\delta)<\lambda^{2}+r.$ Consequently, for any sequence $\varepsilon_{n},\delta_{n}\to 0$ with $\varepsilon_{n}=O(\delta_{n}^{2})$ , we can extract a subsequence $(\varepsilon_{n_{j}},\delta_{n_{j}})$ such that $(\varphi(z,\lambda,\varepsilon_{n_{j}},\delta_{n_{j}}),\psi(z,\lambda,\varepsilon_{n_{j}},\delta_{n_{j}}))$ converges to some function $(\varphi_{0}(z;\lambda),\psi_{0}(z;\lambda))$ on $(0,L]$ as $j\to\infty$ in the $C^{2}$ sense and $k(\lambda,,\varepsilon_{n_{j}},\delta_{n_{j}})$ to some constant $k_{0}$ as $j\to\infty$ .
Needless to say, since $(\varphi(z,\lambda,\varepsilon_{n_{j}},\delta_{n_{j}}),\psi(z,\lambda,\varepsilon_{n_{j}},\delta_{n_{j}}))$ are $L$ -periodic, so is the limit $(\varphi_{0},\psi_{0})$ , but this function has discontinuity at $z=0$ .
In view of ( 6.10 ), ( 6.11 ), ( 6.15 ), ( 6.14 ) and ( 6.16 ), we see that $(\varphi_{0},\psi_{0},k_{0})$ is a solution of the following limit problem: $\displaystyle\begin{cases}\,\varphi^{\prime\prime}-2\lambda\varphi^{\prime}+(\lambda^{2}+1)\varphi=k\varphi\ \ \ (0<z<L),\\[2.0pt]
\,\varphi(+0)=1+\mu,\ \ \varphi(L-0)=1,\\[2.0pt]
\,\varphi(z)>0\ \ \ (0<z<L),\end{cases}$ (6.20a) $\displaystyle\begin{cases}\,\psi^{\prime\prime}-2\lambda\psi^{\prime}+(\lambda^{2}+r)\psi=k\psi\ \ \ (0<z<L),\\[2.0pt]
\psi(+0)=1,\ \ \psi(L-0)=1+\mu,\\[2.0pt]
\,\psi(z)>0\ \ \ (0<z<L),\end{cases}$ (6.20b) $\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(+0)+\psi^{\prime}(+0)=\varphi^{\prime}(L-0)+\psi^{\prime}(L-0).$ (6.20c) Note that ( 6.20a ) and ( 6.20b ) are separate boundary value problems for $\varphi$ and $\psi$ , and they are coupled only through the condition ( 6.20c ), which plays a crucial role.
More precisely, for each $k$ in a certain range, one can solve ( 6.20a ) and ( 6.20b ) separately, but the resulting pair $(\varphi,\psi)$ satisfies the condition ( 6.20c ) only for
a special value of $k$ . In this sense, ( 6.20 ) can be regarded as an eigenvalue problem with $k$ being the eigenvalue. The following theorem holds: Theorem 6.2 (The limit eigenproblem) . For each $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$ , there is a unique value of $k$ , denoted by $k_{0}(\lambda)$ , for which ( 6.20 ) has a solution $(\varphi,\psi)$ .
Furthermore, the principal eigenvalue $k(\lambda,\varepsilon,\delta)$ of ( EPλ ) with ( 2.36 ) converges to $k_{0}(\lambda)$ as $\varepsilon,\delta\to 0$ locally uniformly in $\lambda$ , provided that $\varepsilon=O(\delta^{2})$ . Before proving the above theorem, let us make some preliminary analysis.
By summing up the equations in ( 6.20a ) and ( 6.20b ) and integrating over $0<z<L$ and using ( 6.20c ), we obtain $(k_{0}-\lambda^{2}-1)\int_{0}^{L}\varphi_{0}(z)\mathrm{d}z=(\lambda^{2}+r-k_{0})\int_{0}^{L}\psi_{0}(z)\mathrm{d}z.$ Since the two integrals are both positive and since $r>1$ , we have $\lambda^{2}+1<k_{0}<\lambda^{2}+r\quad({}^{\forall}\lambda\in\mathbb{R}).$ (6.21) Thus we can retrieve the inequality ( 6.2 ) directly from the limit problem ( 6.20 ). Now we fix $\lambda$ arbitrarily, and let $k$ be a positive constant that is not necessarily the eigenvalue of ( 6.20 ). The equations ( 6.20a ), ( 6.20b ) can be rewritten as follows: $\left(e^{-\lambda z}\varphi\right)^{\prime\prime}=(k-1)\left(e^{-\lambda z}\varphi\right),\ \ \ \left(e^{-\lambda z}\psi\right)^{\prime\prime}=(k-r)\left(e^{-\lambda z}\psi\right)\quad(0<z<L).$ Therefore, in order for ( 6.20a ) and ( 6.20b ) to have positive solutions, both $k-1$ and $k-r$ have to be larger than the principal eigenvalue $\nu$ of the following problem, namely $-\pi^{2}/L^{2}$ : $\Phi^{\prime\prime}=\nu\hskip 1.0pt\Phi\ (0<z<L),\quad\ \Phi(0)=\Phi(L)=0.$ Hence $k>r-\frac{\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}.$ (6.22) Conversely, for any $k$ satisfying ( 6.22 ), the boundary value problems ( 6.20a ), ( 6.20b ) have unique solutions, which we denote by $\big(\varphi(z;k),\psi(z;k)\big)$ . Define $f(k)=\varphi^{\prime}(L-0;k)-\varphi^{\prime}(+0;k),\quad g(k)=\psi^{\prime}(L-0;k)-\psi^{\prime}(+0,k)$ and $F(k)=f(k)+g(k)=\varphi^{\prime}(L-0;k)+\psi^{\prime}(L-0;k)-\varphi^{\prime}(+0;k)-\psi^{\prime}(+0,k).$ (6.23) Then ( 6.20c ) holds if and only if $F(k)=0$ . Proof of Theorem 6.2 . Fix $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$ arbitrarily.
Since we already know the existence of a solution of ( 6.20 ) as a limit of the sequence $(\varphi(z,\lambda,\varepsilon_{n_{j}},\delta_{n_{j}}),\psi(z,\lambda,\varepsilon_{n_{j}},\delta_{n_{j}}))$ and $k(\lambda,\varepsilon_{n_{j}},\delta_{n_{j}})$ , let us prove the uniqueness of the solution. It suffices to show that $F(k)$ is strictly monotone in $k$ . Choose arbitrary constants $\tilde{k}>k$ satisfying ( 6.22 ) and let $\varphi(z):=\varphi(z;k),\ \ \widetilde{\varphi}(z):=\varphi(z,\tilde{k}),\ \ \psi(z):=\psi(z;k),\ \ \widetilde{\psi}(z):=\psi(z;\tilde{k}).$ Then $\varphi^{\prime\prime}-2\lambda\varphi^{\prime}+(\lambda^{2}+1)\varphi=k\varphi,\quad\widetilde{\varphi}^{\prime\prime}-2\lambda\widetilde{\varphi}^{\prime}+(\lambda^{2}+1)\widetilde{\varphi}=\tilde{k}\widetilde{\varphi}\geq k\widetilde{\varphi}.$ Therefore $\widetilde{\varphi}$ is a subsolution of the equation satisfied by $\varphi$ , and $\widetilde{\varphi}(+0)=\varphi(+0)$ , $\widetilde{\varphi}(L-0)=\varphi(L-0)$ .
The function $w=\widetilde{\varphi}-\varphi$ then satisfies $\left(e^{-\lambda z}w\right)^{\prime\prime}\geq(k-1)\left(e^{-\lambda z}w\right)\ \ (0<z<L),\quad w(+0)=w(L-0)=0.$ Since $k-1>-\pi^{2}/L^{2}$ by ( 6.22 ), we can apply the strong maximum principle to show that $w(z)<0\;(0<z<L)$ and that $w^{\prime}(+0)<0$ , $w^{\prime}(L-0)>0$ . In other words, $\widetilde{\varphi}(z)<\varphi(z)\ \ (0<z<L),\quad\widetilde{\varphi}^{\prime}(+0)<\varphi^{\prime}(+0),\quad\widetilde{\varphi}^{\prime}(L-0)>\varphi^{\prime}(L-0).$ Hence $f(\tilde{k})-f(k)=\widetilde{\varphi}^{\prime}(L-0)-\widetilde{\varphi}^{\prime}(+0)-\left(\varphi^{\prime}(L-0)-\varphi^{\prime}(+0)\right)>0.$ This implies that $f(k)$ is strictly monotone increasing.
Similarly, $g(k)$ is strictly monotone increasing, therefore $F(k)$ is strictly monotone increasing in the range $r-\pi^{2}/L^{2}<k<\infty$ .
This proves the uniqueness of the solution of ( 6.20 ) for each given $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$ . Since any sequence of solutions $(\varphi(z,\lambda,\varepsilon_{n_{j}},\delta_{n_{j}}),\psi(z,\lambda,\varepsilon_{n_{j}},\delta_{n_{j}})),k(\lambda,\varepsilon_{n_{j}},\delta_{n_{j}})$ of ( EPλ ) has a subsequence that converges to a solution of ( 6.20 ) and since the solution of ( 6.20 ) is unique, we see that the solution $(\varphi(z;\lambda,\varepsilon,\delta),\psi(z;\lambda,\varepsilon,\delta)\big)$ of ( EPλ ) with ( 2.36 ) converges to the unique solution of the problem ( 6.20 ) as $\varepsilon,\delta\to 0$ without taking a subsequence, and that $k(\lambda,\varepsilon,\delta)\to k_{0}(\lambda)$ .
Furthermore, since $k(\lambda,\varepsilon,\delta)$ is convex in $\lambda$ , the convergence $k(\lambda,\varepsilon,\delta)\to k_{0}(\lambda)\;(\varepsilon,\delta\to 0)$ is locally uniform in $\lambda$ ,
since a pointwise convergence of a sequence of convex functions is uniform on bounded sets ( [ 25 , Theorem 10.8] ). This completes the proof of Theorem 6.2 .
∎ Comparison in the limit problem Lemma 6.3 . Let $k_{0}(\lambda)$ be as in Theorem 6.2 . Then $k_{0}(\lambda)<k_{0}(-\lambda)\quad\hbox{for all}\ \ \lambda>0.$ (6.24) Proof. For each $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$ and $k$ satisfying ( 6.22 ), let $\varphi(z;k,\lambda),\psi(z;k,\lambda)$ denote the solution of ( 6.20a ), ( 6.20b ), and define $f(k;\lambda)=\varphi^{\prime}(L-0;k,\lambda)-\varphi^{\prime}(+0;k,\lambda),\quad g(k;\lambda)=\psi^{\prime}(L-0;k,\lambda)-\psi^{\prime}(+0;k,\lambda),$ $F(k;\lambda)=f(k;\lambda)+g(k;\lambda).$ As we have seen above, $F(k;\lambda)$ is strictly increasing in $k$ , and $F(k;\lambda)=0$ if and only if $k=k_{0}(\lambda)$ .
In order to make a finer estimate of $k_{0}(\lambda)$ , we compute $f(k;\lambda)$ and $g(k;\lambda)$ explicitly. We start with $f(k;\lambda)$ . The characteristic equation for ( 6.20a ) is given by $X^{2}-2\lambda X+\lambda^{2}+1-k=0.$ In view of ( 6.21 ), it suffices to consider $k$ in the range $\lambda^{2}+1<k<\lambda^{2}+r$ . In particular, we may assume $k>1$ , hence the solution of the above characteristic equation is given by $X^{\pm}=\lambda\pm\sqrt{k-1}.$ Thus the solution of ( 6.20a ) is written in the form $\varphi(z;k,\lambda)=C^{+}e^{X^{+}z}+C^{-}e^{X^{-}z},$ with $C^{+},C^{-}$ being determined by the boundary conditions $\varphi(+0)=1+\mu$ , $\varphi(L-0)=1$ . A simple calculation then shows the following, where $\alpha=\sqrt{k-1}$ : $f(k;\lambda)=-\mu\lambda+\frac{\alpha}{e^{\alpha L}-e^{-\alpha L}}\left((\mu+2)\left(e^{\alpha L}+e^{-\alpha L}\right)-2\left((1+\mu)e^{\lambda L}+e^{-\lambda L}\right)\right).$ (6.25) Next we consider $g(k;\lambda)$ . The solution of ( 6.20b ) can be expressed by using the roots of $Y^{2}-2\lambda Y+\lambda^{2}+r-k=0$ , but the expression differs depending on the sign of $k-r$ . (a) The case $k>r$ : In this case, the roots of the characteristic equation are real, so $g(k;\lambda)$ can be computed in the same manner as above, and we have the following, where $\beta=\sqrt{k-r}$ : $g(k;\lambda)=\mu\lambda+\frac{\beta}{e^{\beta L}-e^{-\beta L}}\left((\mu+2)\left(e^{\beta L}+e^{-\beta L}\right)-2\left(e^{\lambda L}+(1+\mu)e^{-\lambda L}\right)\right).$ (6.26) (b) The case $k=r$ : This case is the limit of the above case as $k\to r$ (or $\beta\to 0$ ), thus $g(k;\lambda)=\mu\lambda+\frac{1}{L}\left(\mu+2-\left(e^{\lambda L}+(1+\mu)e^{-\lambda L}\right)\right).$ (6.27) (c) The case $k<r$ : In this case, we also need use ( 6.22 ) which is a necessary condition for the existence of a positive solution of ( 6.20b ). The solution $\psi$ is expressed by a linear combination of $e^{\lambda z}\cos\omega z$ and $e^{\lambda z}\sin\omega z$ , where $\omega=\sqrt{r-k}$ , and we get $g(k;\lambda)=\lambda\mu+\frac{\omega}{\sin\omega L}\left((\mu+2)\cos\omega L-\left(e^{\lambda L}+(1+\mu)e^{-\lambda L}\right)\right).$ (6.28) Note that the expression of $g(k;\lambda)$ in ( 6.28 ) can also be obtained by replacing $\beta$ in ( 6.26 ) by $i\omega$ . So these two expressions are identical. Now we assume $\lambda>0$ , and compare $F(k;\lambda):=f(k;\lambda)+g(k;\lambda)$ and $F(k;-\lambda):=f(k;-\lambda)+g(k;-\lambda)$ . In the case where $k>r$ , ( 6.25 ) and ( 6.27 ) give $\begin{split}F(k,\lambda)&=\frac{\alpha}{e^{\alpha L}-e^{-\alpha L}}\left((\mu+2)\left(e^{\alpha L}+e^{-\alpha L}\right)-2\left((1+\mu)e^{\lambda L}+e^{-\lambda L}\right)\right)\\
&\hskip 20.0pt+\frac{\beta}{e^{\beta L}-e^{-\beta L}}\left((\mu+2)\left(e^{\beta L}+e^{-\beta L}\right)-2\left(e^{\lambda L}+(1+\mu)e^{-\lambda L}\right)\right),\end{split}$ $\begin{split}F(k,-\lambda)&=\frac{\alpha}{e^{\alpha L}-e^{-\alpha L}}\left((\mu+2)\left(e^{\alpha L}+e^{-\alpha L}\right)-2\left((1+\mu)e^{-\lambda L}+e^{\lambda L}\right)\right)\\
&\hskip 20.0pt+\frac{\beta}{e^{\beta L}-e^{-\beta L}}\left((\mu+2)\left(e^{\beta L}+e^{-\beta L}\right)-2\left(e^{-\lambda L}+(1+\mu)e^{\lambda L}\right)\right).\end{split}$ Consequently, $F(k;\lambda)-F(k;-\lambda)=2\mu\left(e^{\lambda L}-e^{-\lambda L}\right)\left(\frac{\beta}{e^{\beta L}-e^{-\beta L}}-\frac{\alpha}{e^{\alpha L}-e^{-\alpha L}}\right).$ Since $\lambda>0$ , $\alpha>\beta$ , and since $y/(e^{yL}-e^{-yL})$ is monotone decreasing in $y$ , we have $F(k;\lambda)>F(k;-\lambda)\quad\hbox{for}\ \lambda>0$ (6.29) if $k>r$ . The same inequality also holds for the range $r\geq k>r-\pi^{2}/L^{2}$ , as one can easily see by using ( 6.27 ) and ( 6.28 ). The details are omitted. Recall that both $F(k;\lambda)$ and $F(k;-\lambda)$ are strictly increasing in $k$ , and that $F(k_{0}(\lambda),\lambda)=F(k_{0}(-\lambda),-\lambda)=0$ .
In view of this and ( 6.29 ), we obtain ( 6.24 ). The lemma is proved.
∎ We are now in a position to prove Theorem 2.22 . Proof of Theorem 2.22 . Recall that we have, by ( 6.1 ), $c^{*}_{R}(\varepsilon,\delta)=\min_{\lambda>0}\frac{k(\lambda,\varepsilon,\delta)}{\lambda},\quad c^{*}_{L}(\varepsilon,\delta)=\min_{\lambda>0}\frac{k(-\lambda,\varepsilon,\delta)}{\lambda},$ (6.30) where $k(\lambda,\varepsilon,\delta)$ denotes the principal eigenvalue of ( EPλ ) with ( 2.36 ).
Furthermore, as shown in ( 6.3 ), the minima in ( 6.30 ) are attained by $\lambda$ that lies in the following range: $\sqrt{r}-\sqrt{r-1}\leq\lambda\leq\sqrt{r}+\sqrt{r-1}.$ By Theorem 6.2 , the convergence $k(\lambda,\varepsilon,\delta)\to k_{0}(\lambda)\;(\varepsilon,\delta\to 0)$ is locally uniform in $\lambda$ .
This, together with ( 6.24 ), implies that $k(\lambda,\varepsilon,\delta)<k(-\lambda,\varepsilon,\delta)\quad\hbox{for}\ \,\lambda\in[\sqrt{r}-\sqrt{r-1},\sqrt{r}+\sqrt{r-1}]$ for all sufficiently small $\varepsilon,\delta$ with $\varepsilon=O(\delta^{2})$ , hence ( 2.37 ).
This establishes Theorem 2.22 .
