Schrödinger-Poisson system in the two-dimensional whole space Satoshi Masaki Address: Division of Mathematics Graduate School of Information Sciences Tohoku University Sendai 980-8579, Japan Email address: masaki@ims.is.tohoku.ac.jp Abstract. We consider the Schrödinger-Poisson system in the two-dimensional whole space.
A new formula of solutions to the Poisson equation is used.
Although the potential term solving the Poisson equation may grow at the spatial infinity,
we show the unique existence of a time-local solution for data in the Sobolev spaces
by an analysis of a quantum hydrodynamical
system via a modified Madelung transform.
This method has been used to justify the WKB approximation of solutions
to several classes of nonlinear Schrödinger equation in the semiclassical limit. 1. Introduction In this article, we study the following Schrödinger-Poisson system (SP) $\left\{\begin{aligned} &i{\partial}_{t}u+\frac{1}{2}\Delta u=\lambda Pu,\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+2},\\
&-\Delta P=|u|^{2},\\
&\nabla P\in L^{\infty},\quad\nabla P\to 0\text{ as }|x|\to\infty,\quad P(0)=0,\\
&u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)\end{aligned}\right.$ or its semiclassical version ( $\text{SP}^{\varepsilon}$ ) $\left\{\begin{aligned} &i\varepsilon{\partial}_{t}u^{\varepsilon}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\Delta u^{\varepsilon}=\lambda P^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon},\quad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{1+2},\\
&-\Delta P^{\varepsilon}=|u^{\varepsilon}|^{2},\\
&\nabla P^{\varepsilon}\in L^{\infty},\quad\nabla P^{\varepsilon}\to 0\text{ as }|x|\to\infty,\quad P^{\varepsilon}(0)=0,\\
&u^{\varepsilon}(0,x)=u_{0}^{\varepsilon}(x),\end{aligned}\right.$ where $\varepsilon$ is a positive parameter corresponding to the Planck constant
and $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ is a given physical constant.
In this article, we assume that the initial data $u_{0}$ and $u_{0}^{\varepsilon}$ belong to the Soblev space $H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ with $s>2$ ,
and show that ( SP ) and ( SP
                  ε ) have a unique
time local solution in $C((-T,T);H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))$ and that, for the solution $u^{\varepsilon}$ of ( SP
                  ε ), the following WKB type
approximation is valid in certain topology: (1.1) $u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)=e^{i\frac{\phi(t,x)}{\varepsilon}}(a_{0}+\varepsilon a_{1}+\cdots+\varepsilon^{N}a_{N}+o(\varepsilon^{N})).$ The Schrödinger-Poisson system is one of
the typical example of a nonlinear Schrödinger equation with a nonlocal nonlinearity,
and there are much literature on this system
if space dimension is larger than or equal to three
(see [ 8 ] and references therein). However, in contrast with higher dimensional cases,
the two-dimensional case is less studied.
One of the reason may be that an appropriate meaning of a solution
to the Poisson equation is not so clear.
We briefly recall the treatment of the solution in previous results.
For a given function $b(x)\geqslant 0$ on $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ , called background, let us replace
the Poisson equation in ( SP ) with (1.2) $-\Delta P=|u|^{2}-b.$ One of the most natural solution may be $P_{1}:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[\frac{1}{|\xi|^{2}}\mathcal{F}(|u|^{2}-b)\right],$ where $\mathcal{F}$ denotes the Fourier transform.
To admit this solution, the following
“neutrality condition” plays a very important role: (1.3) $2\pi\mathcal{F}(|u(t)|^{2}-b)(0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(|u(x,t)|^{2}-b(x))dx\equiv 0.$ To handle the strong singularity of $|\xi|^{-2}\mathcal{F}(|u|^{2}-b)$ at the origin,
this mean-zero assumption is almost essential.
Indeed, in [ 4 , 14 , 21 ] ,
( 1.3 ) and
several integrability conditions on $\mathcal{F}(|u|^{2}-b)$ are assumed to prove that $P_{1}$ belongs to some Lebesgue space. One of the main point in this article is to remove the neutrality condition.
To make this point clear,
we restrict our attention to the zero-background case $b\equiv 0$ throughout this article.
Notice that all nontrivial solutions are excluded
when we assume the neutrality condition with $b\equiv 0$ ,
and so that the zero-background case is out of the framework of the previous results.
In this article, the Poisson equation $-\Delta P=|u|^{2}$ is posed with
the following conditions: $\nabla P\in L^{\infty},\quad\nabla P\to 0\text{ as }|x|\to\infty,\quad\text{and}\quad P(0)=0.$ Under these conditions, the solution $P$ is given by (1.4) $P(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\log\frac{|x-y|}{|y|}\right)|u(y)|^{2}dy$ and unique for a class of $|u|^{2}$ (see Theorem A.1 ).
It might seem to be more natural to use the Newtonian potential $P_{2}(x):=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\log|x|*|u|^{2}\right)(x),$ as the solution of the Poisson equation.
However, $P$ given in ( 1.4 ) makes sense merely if $|u|^{2}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ for some $p\in(1,2)$ ,
and $P_{2}$ requires an additional assumption $\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\log|y|)|u(y)|^{2}dy<\infty$ to make sense (see Proposition A.4 ).
Namely, $P$ is well-defined under a weaker assumption.
This is the reason why we consider ( 1.4 ).
It will turn out that the behavior of $P$ at the spatial infinity is not so good;
in general, $|P(x)|=O(\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\log|x|)$ as $|x|\to\infty$ .
It is worth mentioning that $\nabla P$ never belongs to $L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ for all $u\not\equiv 0$ no matter how fast $u$ decays,
say even if $u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ .
We discuss the Poisson equation in the two-dimensional whole space
more precisely in Appendix A . In what follows, we consider ( 1.4 ) as the solution of the Poisson equation.
With ( 1.4 ), the system ( SP ) and
( SP
                  ε ) are rewritten as ( $\text{SP}^{\prime}$ ) $\left\{\begin{aligned} &i{\partial}_{t}u+\frac{1}{2}\Delta u=\lambda Pu,\\
&P=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\log\frac{|x-y|}{|y|}\right)|u(y)|^{2}dy,\\
&u(0,x)=u_{0}(x)\end{aligned}\right.$ and ( $\text{SP}^{\varepsilon\prime}$ ) $\left\{\begin{aligned} &i\varepsilon{\partial}_{t}u^{\varepsilon}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\Delta u^{\varepsilon}=\lambda P^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon},\\
&P^{\varepsilon}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\log\frac{|x-y|}{|y|}\right)|u^{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}dy,\\
&u^{\varepsilon}(0,x)=u_{0}^{\varepsilon}(x),\end{aligned}\right.$ respectively.
The difficulty for solving ( SP
                  ′ ) (and ( SP

lies in the growth $|P(x)|=O(\log|x|)$ at the spatial infinity.
For example, the Duhamel term of the corresponding integral equation
does not necessarily belong to any Lebesgue space because of this growth,
and so we cannot apply directly the usual perturbation argument to the integral equation. Thus, we apply another method:
We look for the solution of the form (1.5) $u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)=a^{\varepsilon}(t,x)e^{i\frac{\phi^{\varepsilon}(t,x)}{\varepsilon}},$ where the “amplitude part” $a^{\varepsilon}$ is complex-valued and
the “phase part” $\phi^{\varepsilon}$ is real-valued.
We are considering only ( SP
                
                  
                    ε
                    ′ )
because ( SP
                  ′ ) corresponds to the special case $\varepsilon=1$ .
Plugging ( 1.5 ) to ( SP

&{\partial}_{t}\phi^{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\lambda P^{\varepsilon}=0,\\

Notice that if $(a^{\varepsilon},\phi^{\varepsilon})$ solves ( 1.6 ),
then $u^{\varepsilon}=a^{\varepsilon}e^{i\phi^{\varepsilon}/\varepsilon}$ is an exact solution of ( SP

This decomposition, called a modified Madelung transform,
is first introduced in [ 13 ] to justify the WKB approximation ( 1.1 ) of solutions
to a class of nonlinear Schrödinger equations,
and extended to several types of nonlinear Schrödinger equations in [ 2 , 3 , 16 ] (see also [ 6 , 9 , 11 , 12 ] ).
For the WKB approximation of the Schrödinger-Poisson system for other dimensions,
we refer the reader to [ 17 ] (one dimension) and [ 1 , 7 , 18 ] (three dimensions and higher).
In [ 14 , 21 ] , the same limit is treated in the two-dimensional case
by Wigner measures. Before stating our result precisely, we make some definitions and notation. $L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ ( $p\in[1,\infty]$ ) and $H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ ( $s\geqslant 0$ ) denote the usual Lebesgue
and Sobolev space, respectively.
We say $f\in H^{s}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ if any restriction of $f$ on a bounded domain
belongs to $H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ . $X^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ is the Zhidkov space defined by $\displaystyle X^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2}):=\{f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2});\nabla f\in H^{s-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\},$ $\displaystyle\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{X^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}:=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\left\lVert\nabla f\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}$ for $s>1$ . This space is introduced in [ 22 ] (see also [ 10 ] ).
We sometimes write $L^{p}=L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ , $H^{s}=H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ , and $X^{s}=X^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ ,
for short. 1.1. Main result 1 We first state our result on the unique existence of a local solution to ( SP
                  ′ ). Theorem 1.1 . Let $u_{0}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ with $s>2$ .
Then, there exist an existence time $T=T(\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{s-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})})$ and
a time-local solution $u\in C((-T,T);H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\cap C^{1}((-T,T);H^{s-2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))$ to ( SP
                  ′ ).
Moreover, this solution is unique in this space and the
data-to-solution mapping $u_{0}\mapsto u$ is continuous from $H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ to $C((-T,T);H^{s-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))$ .
Furthermore, the mass is conserved. Remark 1.2 . The existence part of Theorem 1.1 holds if $u_{0}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ ( $s>1$ ). Remark 1.3 . For the solution $u$ given in Theorem 1.1 , ${\partial}_{t}u$ does not necessarily decays at the spatial infinity.
This is due to the lack of spatial decay of $P$ .
Therefore, in general, $u\in C^{1}((-T,T);H^{s-2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))$ is not replaced by $u\in C^{1}((-T,T);H^{s-2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))$ .
Nevertheless, the solution is continuous as a $H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ -valued function. Remark 1.4 . The uniqueness of the solution is new in the sense not only that the result itself is new
but also that we use a new argument.
In our proof, we derive the uniqueness of ( SP
                  ′ )
from that of the system ( 1.6 ) via ( 1.5 )
by showing that every solution of ( SP
                  ′ ) is of the form ( 1.5 ),
at least for small time. Remark 1.5 . The solution given by Theorem 1.1 does not necessarily have a finite energy. The energy $E[u](t)=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\nabla u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{4\pi}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\log|x-y|)|u(t,x)|^{2}|u(t,y)|^{2}dxdy$ is finite and conserved
as long as $W(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\log|y|)|u(s,y)|^{2}dyds$ is finite.
Indeed, if $W(t)<\infty$ then $\widetilde{u}(t)=u(t)e^{i\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}W(t)}$ solves (1.7) $i{\partial}_{t}\widetilde{u}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta\widetilde{u}=-\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\widetilde{u}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\log|x-y|)|\widetilde{u}(y)|^{2}dy$ and $E[\widetilde{u}](t)$ is conserved.
Then, one sees that $E[{u}]=E[\widetilde{u}]$ is also conserved.

Theorem 1.7 . Let Assumption 1.6 be satisfied for a positive integer $N$ .
Then, there exist an existence time $T$ independent of $\varepsilon$ and
a unique time-local solution $u^{\varepsilon}\in C([0,T);H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\cap C^{1}((0,T);H^{s-2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))$ to ( SP
                  ′ ).
Moreover, there exist $\beta_{j}\in C([0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))$ ( $0\leqslant j<N$ ) and $\phi_{0}\in C([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})$ such that $u^{\varepsilon}=e^{i\frac{\phi_{0}}{\varepsilon}}(\beta_{0}+\varepsilon\beta_{1}+\cdots+\varepsilon^{N-1}\beta_{N-1}+o(\varepsilon^{N-1}))\quad\text{in }L^{\infty}([0,T);L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))$ for $0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1$ . then the same result as in Theorem 1.7 holds without the neutrality
condition $\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(|u^{\varepsilon}|^{2}-b)dx=0$ . The rest of this article is organized as follows:

Preliminary result In this section, we establish an existence result on the system ( 1.6 ) $\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\partial}_{t}a^{\varepsilon}+\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla a^{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}a^{\varepsilon}\Delta\phi^{\varepsilon}=i\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Delta a^{\varepsilon},\\
&{\partial}_{t}\phi^{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\lambda P^{\varepsilon}=0,\\
&P^{\varepsilon}(t,x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\log\frac{|x-y|}{|y|}\right)|a^{\varepsilon}(t,y)|^{2}dy,\\
&a^{\varepsilon}(0,x)=A^{\varepsilon}(x),\quad\phi^{\varepsilon}(0,x)=\Phi(x).\end{aligned}\right.$ Assumption 2.1 . We assume the following for some $s>2$ : • $A^{\varepsilon}\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ and $\left\lVert A^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}$ is uniformly bounded. • $\Phi\in C^{1}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})$ with $\nabla\Phi\in X^{s+1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ for some $p\in(2,\infty)$ . Theorem 2.2 . Let Assumption 2.1 be satisfied.
Then, there exist $T>0$ independent of $\varepsilon$ and a unique solution $\displaystyle a^{\varepsilon}\in C([0,T);H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))\cap C^{1}((0,T);H^{s-2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})),$ $\displaystyle\phi^{\varepsilon}\in C^{1}([0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})$ of ( 1.6 ).
Moreover, $\left\lVert a^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}$ and $\left\lVert\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{X^{s+1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}$ are uniformly bounded,
and the data-to-solution mapping $(A^{\varepsilon},\nabla\Phi)\mapsto(a^{\varepsilon},\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon})$ is continuous from $H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\times(X^{s+1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))$ to $C([0,T);H^{s-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\times(X^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})))$ .
The mass $\left\lVert a^{\varepsilon}(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}$ is conserved and
it holds that $\limsup_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{|\phi^{\varepsilon}(t,x)-\Phi(x)|}{\log|x|}\leqslant\frac{t|\lambda|}{2\pi}\left\lVert A^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.$ Furthermore, if $s>3$ and $A_{0}:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}A^{\varepsilon}$ exists in $H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ then
the following properties hold: • $(a_{0},\phi_{0}):=(a^{\varepsilon},\phi^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon=0}$ exists in the same class
and solves (2.1) $\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\partial}_{t}a_{0}+\nabla\phi_{0}\cdot\nabla a_{0}+\frac{1}{2}a_{0}\Delta\phi_{0}=0,\\
&{\partial}_{t}\phi_{0}+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi_{0}|^{2}+\lambda P_{0}=0,\\
&a_{0}(0,x)=A_{0}(x),\quad\phi_{0}(0,x)=\Phi(x),\end{aligned}\right.$ where $P_{0}$ is the Poisson term defined from $a_{0}$ by ( 1.4 ),
and $(a^{\varepsilon},\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon})$ converges to $(a_{0},\nabla\phi_{0})$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$ in the $C([0,T];H^{s-2}\times(X^{s-2}\cap L^{2+}))$ topology. • For any bounded domain $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}$ , it holds that $\left\lVert\phi^{\varepsilon}-\phi_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\Omega)}\to 0$ as $\varepsilon\to 0$ . Proof. Put $v^{\varepsilon}:=\nabla\phi^{\varepsilon}$ and consider (2.2) $\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\partial}_{t}a^{\varepsilon}+v^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla a^{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}a^{\varepsilon}\nabla\cdot v^{\varepsilon}=i\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Delta a^{\varepsilon},\quad a^{\varepsilon}(0,x)=A_{0}^{\varepsilon}(x);\\

because this part is easier than the following uniqueness part
and is essentially the same as in [ 18 ] .
Then, the standard argument shows $(a^{\varepsilon},v^{\varepsilon})\in C([0,T);H^{s}\times X^{s+1}\cap L^{p})$ exists (see [ 1 , 7 ] ).
It follows from the first line of ( 2.2 ) that $a^{\varepsilon}\in C^{1}((0,T);H^{s-2})$ .
Since $E(0)$ is bounded uniformly in $\varepsilon$ ,
we see that the solution is also uniformly bounded.

the first line of ( 2.2 ) by a standard argument. Now, we proceed to the proof of the uniqueness of ( 2.2 ).

However, ${\partial}_{1}w\to 0$ as $|x|\to\infty$ and so ${\partial}_{1}w\equiv 0$ .
Similarly, ${\partial}_{2}w\equiv 0$ .
Therefore, $w$ is a constant and so $w(x)\equiv w(0)=0$ ,
which shows $P_{1}\equiv P_{2}$ .
∎ A.1. A solution given by the Newtonian potential We can also give a rigorous meaning of the Newtonian potential (A.14) $\widetilde{P}(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\log|x-y|)f(y)dy$ as a solution of the Poisson equation.
Notice that $-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x|$ is the Newtonian kernel in two dimensions and
so that $\widetilde{P}$ is a two-dimensional version of ( A.5 ). Proposition A.4 . Let $f\in L^{p_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ for some $p_{0}\in(1,2)$ and let $\widetilde{P}$ be as
in ( A.14 ).
If $\widetilde{P}(x)$ is finite at some $x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ ,
then it is finite for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ and,
moreover, $\widetilde{P}(x)=P(x)+\widetilde{P}(0)$ ,
where $P$ is the solution of ( A.1 ) with ( A.2 )–( A.3 )
given by Theorem A.1 . Notice that the proposition implies the following: • If $\widetilde{P}(x)$ diverges at some $x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ then
it necessarily diverges for all $x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ under the same assumption on $f$ . • The difference between $P$ and $\widetilde{P}$ is merely
a constant $\widetilde{P}(0)$ .
However, when we consider $\widetilde{P}$ , we need an additional assumption on $f$ only for saying that this constant is finite. • If $f\in L^{p_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ is so that $\widetilde{P}(0)$ is finite,
then $\widetilde{P}$ is a weak solution of ( A.1 ) with
the condition $P(0)=\widetilde{P}(0)$ and $|\nabla P|\to 0$ as $|x|\to\infty$ . The proof of this proposition is obvious: It suffices to mention that, for any $f\in L^{p_{0}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})$ ( $p_{0}\in(1,2)$ ) and $x_{1}$ , $x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$ , $-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\log\frac{|x_{1}-y|}{|x_{2}-y|}\right)f(y)dy$ is finite.
