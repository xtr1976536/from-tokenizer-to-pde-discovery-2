We provide a rigorous mathematical framework to establish the limit of a nonlocal model of cell-cell adhesion system to a local model. When the parameter of the nonlocality goes to 0, the system tends to a Cahn-Hilliard system with degenerate mobility and cross interaction forces. Our analysis relies on a priori estimates and compactness properties. Key words and phrases: Degenerate Cahn-Hilliard equation; Nonlocal Cahn-Hilliard
equation; Aggregation-Diffusion; Singular limit 1991 Mathematics Subject Classification 35B40, 35D30, 35K25, 35K55 1. Introduction We consider the nonlocal system of Cahn-Hilliard equation with degenerate mobility derived in [ 35 ] $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}$ $\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\nabla\left(\kappa B[\rho]+\alpha B[\eta]-\gamma\rho-\beta\eta\right)\right),$ $\displaystyle\text{in}\quad(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d},$ (1.1) $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}$ $\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\nabla\left(\alpha B[\rho]+B[\eta]-\beta\rho-\eta\right)\right),$ $\displaystyle\text{in}\quad(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}.$ (1.2) equipped with an initial datum $(\rho_{0},\eta_{0})\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}$ where $\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$ is the space of probability measure on the flat torus of dimension $d$ . Here $\kappa,\alpha,\gamma>0$ , $\beta\in\mathbb{R}$ while $B$ is the nonlocal operator $B=B_{\varepsilon}$ defined with $B_{\varepsilon}[u_{\varepsilon}](x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}(u_{\varepsilon}(x)-\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast u_{\varepsilon}(x))=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)(u_{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}(x-y))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y$ (1.3) for $\varepsilon$ small enough and $\omega_{\varepsilon}$ is the usual radial mollification kernel $\omega_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d}}\omega(\frac{x}{\varepsilon})$ with $\omega$ compactly supported in the unit ball satisfying $\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=1,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\,\omega(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=0,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y_{i}y_{j}\omega\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\delta_{i,j}\frac{2}{d}.$ (1.4) Our target is to prove that as $\varepsilon\to 0$ , the constructed solutions of $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}$ $\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\nabla\left(\kappa B_{\varepsilon}[\rho]+\alpha B_{\varepsilon}[\eta]-\gamma\rho-\beta\eta\right)\right),$ $\displaystyle\text{in}\quad(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d},$ (1.5) $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}$ $\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\nabla\left(\alpha B_{\varepsilon}[\rho]+B_{\varepsilon}[\eta]-\beta\rho-\eta\right)\right),$ $\displaystyle\text{in}\quad(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}$ (1.6) tend to the weak solution of the local system of degenerate Cahn-Hilliard equations $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}$ $\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\nabla\left(-\kappa\Delta\rho-\alpha\Delta\eta-\gamma\rho-\beta\eta\right)\right),$ (1.7) $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}$ $\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\nabla\left(-\alpha\Delta\rho-\Delta\eta-\beta\rho-\eta\right)\right).$ (1.8) Here, $\kappa>0$ and $\gamma>0$ represent the relative self-adhesion strength of $\rho$ with respect to $\eta$ ; while $\alpha>0$ and $\beta\in\mathbb{R}$ give the relative strength of the cross-attraction forces. We also denote by $\displaystyle\mu_{\rho,\varepsilon}$ $\displaystyle=\kappa B_{\varepsilon}[\rho]+\alpha B_{\varepsilon}[\eta]-\gamma\rho-\beta\eta\,,$ $\displaystyle\mu_{\eta,\varepsilon}$ $\displaystyle=\alpha B_{\varepsilon}[\rho]+B_{\varepsilon}[\eta]-\beta\rho-\eta\,,$ the chemical potentials (from the Cahn-Hilliard terminology) related to the nonlocal system.
The nonlocal system is associated with the following formal energy/entropy structure $\begin{split}E_{\varepsilon}&[\rho,\eta]:=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\kappa|\rho(x)-\rho(x-y)|^{2}+|\eta(x)-\eta(x-y)|^{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\\
&+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\rho(x)-\rho(x-y))(\eta(x)-\eta(x-y))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\gamma}{2}\rho^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}+\beta\rho\eta\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}$ (1.9) $\Phi[\rho,\eta]:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho(\log(\rho)-1)+\eta(\log(\eta)-1)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.$ (1.10) Their dissipation is formally controlled by the identities $E_{\varepsilon}[\rho,\eta](t)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\,|\nabla\mu_{\rho,\varepsilon}|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\eta\,|\nabla\mu_{\eta,\varepsilon}|^{2}\leq E_{\varepsilon}[\rho_{0},\eta_{0}],$ (1.11) $\begin{split}\Phi[\rho,\eta](t)+\mathcal{D}\Phi[\rho,\eta](t)\leq\Phi[\rho_{0},\eta_{0}].\end{split}$ (1.12) where $\mathcal{D}\Phi[\rho,\eta](t)$ is the dissipation of the entropy defined as $\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\Phi[\rho,\eta](t)=$ $\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\kappa|\nabla\rho(x)-\nabla\rho(x-y)|^{2}+|\nabla\eta(x)-\nabla\eta(x-y)|^{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s$ $\displaystyle+\alpha\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\nabla\rho(x)-\nabla\rho(x-y))\cdot(\nabla\eta(x)-\nabla\eta(x-y))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s$ $\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\gamma|\nabla\rho|^{2}+|\nabla\eta|^{2}+2\beta\nabla\rho\cdot\nabla\eta\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\,.$ It turns out (see Proposition 2.1 ) that for $\kappa>\alpha^{2}$ , $\kappa>0$ they provide strong compactness in space of $\rho_{\varepsilon}$ , $\eta_{\varepsilon}$ , $\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}$ , $\nabla\eta_{\varepsilon}$ . Our first result states that we can construct solutions to ( 1.5 )-( 1.6 ) satisfying additional uniform estimates which will be relevant in the sequel. Theorem 1.1 (Existence of solutions for the nonlocal system) . Suppose that $\kappa>0$ , $\kappa>\alpha^{2}$ . Let $\varepsilon_{0}=\min(\varepsilon_{0}^{A},\varepsilon_{0}^{B})$ be given by Proposition 2.1 and Lemma 3.2 . Let $u_{0}=(\rho_{0},\eta_{0})\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}$ be an initial datum with finite energy and entropy $E_{\varepsilon}(u^{0}),\Phi(u^{0})\leq C$ defined in  ( 1.9 )-( 1.10 ) where $C$ is independent of $\varepsilon$ . Let $\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}$ . Then, there exists a global weak solution $u_{\varepsilon}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}$ of ( 1.5 )-( 1.6 ) as defined in Definition 3.1 . Moreover, it satisfies $\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)}{\varepsilon^{2}}(|\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)-\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)|^{2}+|\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)-\eta_{\varepsilon}(x-y)|^{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq C,$ (1.13) $\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)}{\varepsilon^{2}}(|\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)-\nabla\rho_{\varepsilon}(x-y)|^{2}+|\nabla\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)-\nabla\eta_{\varepsilon}(x-y)|^{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq C,$ (1.14) for a constant $C$ that depends on parameters and the initial condition $u^{0}$ but not on $\varepsilon$ . The proof of this result follows the argument of [ 29 ] . We decided to include the proof to demonstrate estimates ( 1.13 )–( 1.14 ) which are essential for our main result which reads as follows. Theorem 1.2 (Convergence of nonlocal to local Cahn-Hilliard equation on the torus) . Suppose that $\kappa>0$ , $\kappa>\alpha^{2}$ . Let $u_{0}\geq 0$ be an initial datum with finite energy and entropy $E_{\varepsilon}(u^{0}),\Phi(u^{0})\leq C$ defined in  ( 1.9 ) and ( 1.10 ) where $C$ is independent of $\varepsilon$ . Let $\{u_{\varepsilon}\}$ be a sequence of solutions of the degenerate nonlocal Cahn-Hilliard equation ( 1.5 )-( 1.6 ) as defined in Definition 3.1 . Then, up to a subsequence (not relabelled), $u_{\varepsilon}\to u\mbox{ in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\,,$ where $u$ is a weak solution of the degenerate Cahn-Hilliard system ( 1.7 )-( 1.8 ) as in Definition 4.1 . We conclude with a short discussion of applied techniques and considered problems. Existence of weak solutions. The strategy to prove the existence of weak solutions is based on the gradient flow structure of the equation in the Wasserstein space. We use the JKO scheme first introduced in [ 41 ] by Jordan, Kinderlehrer and Otto in the context of the Fokker-Planck equation. The main idea is to use an implicit time discretization of the associated variational problems. The sequence created minimizes movements. When the time step goes to 0, the sequence converges to the associated gradient flow. This strategy has been used for instance in [ 43 , 42 ] in the context of Cahn-Hilliard equation and in [ 29 ] in the context of cross-diffusion systems with nonlocal interaction. The proof of Theorem 1.1 is in fact closely related to the one in [ 29 ] and we follow to some extent their proof. The difference is the control of the positiveness of the energy and the transport of uniform bounds independently of $\varepsilon$ needed in the last section. Also, our settings are periodic in space.
The proof uses two main components: • The "gradient flow" structure of the scheme which provides classical discrete energy estimates and Hölder continuity in time and allow narrow convergences of the scheme. • The weak convergences being not enough, we use the flow interchange lemma [ 44 , 43 ] . The idea is that we usually obtain better estimates with the entropy in the Cahn-Hilliard equation. This entropy generates a heat-flow. Then the flow interchange lemma allows to exchange the dissipation of one functional along the gradient flow of another one and thus improve the regularity of the scheme. Nevertheless, since we assume that $\kappa>\alpha^{2}$ and $\kappa>0$ , the system is strongly parabolic with respect to $(\rho,\eta)$ . Therefore, to prove existence, one could approximate ( 1.5 )–( 1.6 ) by $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}$ $\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(T_{\delta}(\rho)\nabla\left(\kappa B_{\varepsilon}[\rho]+\alpha B_{\varepsilon}[\eta]-\gamma\rho-\beta\eta\right)\right),$ $\displaystyle\text{in}\quad(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d},$ (1.15) $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}$ $\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(T_{\delta}(\eta)\nabla\left(\alpha B_{\varepsilon}[\rho]+B_{\varepsilon}[\eta]-\beta\rho-\eta\right)\right),$ $\displaystyle\text{in}\quad(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d},$ (1.16) where $T_{\delta}$ is a function such that $\delta\leq T_{\delta}\leq\frac{1}{\delta}$ and $T_{\delta}(\varrho)\to\varrho$ as $\delta\to 0$ . Then, one sends $\delta\to 0$ and obtains solutions to ( 1.5 )–( 1.6 ). This method is a standard way of proving existence of solutions to Cahn-Hilliard equation with degenerate mobility [ 28 , 33 , 31 , 47 ] . Of course, one has to prove estistence to ( 1.15 )–( 1.16 ) by virtue of fixed point method and Schauder’s estimates for parabolic equations which is very technical. Therefore, we preferred to apply gradient flow techniques which are natural for our problem. Passage to the limit $\varepsilon\to 0$ and nonlocal compactness results. We use the strategy developed by the second and third author in [ 32 ] for the single Cahn-Hilliard equation. The main tool is the compactness result due to Bourgain-Brezis-Mironescu [ 11 ] and Ponce [ 48 ] which reads as follows: Proposition 1.3 . Let $\{f_{\varepsilon}\}$ be a sequence bounded in $L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$ . Suppose that $\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{|f_{\varepsilon}(x)-f_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}\omega_{\varepsilon}(|x-y|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq C$ (1.17) for some constant $C$ . Then, $\{f_{\varepsilon}\}$ is strongly compact in $L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$ and the limit $f\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$ . Proposition 1.3 is crucial because the difference quotient appearing in ( 1.17 ) can be controlled by the energy and the dissipation of the entropy of Cahn-Hilliard equation which yields compactness. The novelty in this paper comes from the treating of cross-interactions terms. It turns out that we can control possibly negative terms appearing in the energy and the dissipation of entropy by a simple interpolation inequality, see Lemma 2.2 and Proposition 2.1 . Aggregation-diffusion systems. System ( 1.5 )-( 1.6 ) is an example of aggregation-diffusion system [ 18 ] which are attracting a lot of mathematical attention nowadays [ 51 , 21 , 6 , 22 , 34 ] . To motivate, let us first start with an aggregation equation $\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho u)=0,\qquad u=-\nabla W\ast\rho,$ (1.18) where $W:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}$ is a symmetric interaction potential. Over the last years, equation ( 1.18 ) was applied in the context of biological aggregation [ 10 , 52 , 46 ] , materials science [ 38 ] , granular media [ 24 , 7 ] and it attracted also a lot of mathematical interest, particularly for non-smooth potentials. We only refer to [ 9 ] for the $L^{p}$ theory, to [ 19 ] for the theory in spaces of measures and to [ 8 ] for blow-up conditions. Equation ( 1.18 ) can be derived from the particle system via mean-field limit [ 40 , 39 ] : one considers $N$ particles having positions $\{X_{i}\}_{i=1,...,N}$ satisfying system of ODEs: $X_{i}^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\nabla W(X_{i}-X_{j});$ then, under appropriate assumptions on $W$ , empirical measure $\rho^{N}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{i}(t)}$ converges (in the weak ∗ topology of measures) to a solution of ( 1.18 ), see [ 15 , Theorem 3.1] for the proof and [ 16 ] for some extensions. To obtain the aggregation-diffusion equation from ( 1.18 ), one can consider interaction potentials of the form $W_{\nu}=W+2\nu\delta_{0}$ modeling two effects: repulsion of strength $\nu$ and non-local attraction. Informally, we obtain $\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=\nu\,\Delta\rho^{2}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\,\nabla W\ast\rho),$ (1.19) but this can be made rigorous by approximating the Dirac mass with a sequence of mollifiers, see [ 17 , 12 ] with $m=2$ . Of course, ( 1.19 ) can be extended to include more general diffusion term $\Delta\rho^{m}$ instead of $\Delta\rho^{2}$ as well as an advection term, see [ 20 ] . One can also consider system of particles $\{X_{i}\}_{i=1,...,N}$ , $\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1,...,N}$ representing two populations. Then, repeating the derivation explained above, one arrives at a system of equations of the form ( 1.1 )–( 1.2 ) which can be used to model cell-cell adhesion [ 2 , 23 , 25 ] to reproduce the Steinberg cell-sorting phenomena. Structure preserving numerical schemes have been derived for equations and systems of aggregation-diffusion type [ 14 , 3 , 4 ] as well as Cahn-Hilliard equations and systems [ 5 , 35 ] recovering the cell sorting mechanism. Gradient flows in the periodic setting. Due to the presence of a non-local operator $B_{\varepsilon}$ in ( 1.5 )–( 1.6 ), we develop our theory on the $d$ -dimensional torus $\mathbb{T}^{d}$ which makes non-local terms easy to be defined. As already explained, solutions to ( 1.5 )–( 1.6 ) will be constructed via JKO scheme. In what follows, we briefly review the theory of optimal transport on $\mathbb{T}^{d}$ comparing to the usual case of $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ or bounded domain $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}$ . We refer the Reader to [ 50 , Section 1.3.2] . First, we need to define a metric and the natural choice is $d(x,y)=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|x-y+k|,\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{T}^{d},$ where $|\cdot|$ is the Euclidean distance. Then, the Wasserstein distance ${W}_{2}$ is defined as ${W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}}d(x,y)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi(x,y),\qquad\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),$ where $\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ is the set of couplings between $\mu$ and $\nu$ . This already implies that, say in dimension $d=1$ , the optimal transport maps are not necessarily monotone on the torus. Nevertheless, the optimal map always exists if at least one of the measures is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebegue measure. Moreover, the optimal map is given by the gradient of some function which is differentiable a.e. We refer to [ 27 ] for the first proof of this fact and to [ 50 , Theorem 1.25] for a modern presentation. We also refer to the general result of McCann [ 45 ] who proved existence of the optimal map on the general manifold, including the case of torus. To conclude, let us mention that optimal transport was used to study several PDEs on the torus via JKO scheme, in particular fractional porous medium equation [ 36 ] , continuity equation with nonlocal velocity in 1D [ 37 ] , systems of continuity equations with nonlinear diffusion and nonlocal drifts [ 13 ] and certain fourth-order equation in one dimension [ 26 ] . 2. Energy and entropy for the non-local system As already mentioned, system ( 1.5 )–( 1.6 ) has the energy/entropy structure which can be used to obtain compactness estimates. In order to do so, one has to assure nonnegativity of the quantities of interest. In our case, we focus on the energy $E_{\varepsilon}$ and the dissipation of entropy $\mathcal{D}\Phi$ . Proposition 2.1 . Suppose that $\kappa>0$ , $\kappa>\alpha^{2}$ . Then, there exists $\varepsilon_{0}^{A}>0$ depending on $\kappa$ , $\alpha$ , $\beta$ , $\gamma$ with the following property: for all $\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}^{A})$ and $\rho,\eta\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$ , up to a constant, the energy defined by ( 1.9 ) and the dissipation of the entropy defined in ( 1.12 ) are nonnegative and provide the estimates on the quantities $\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)}{\varepsilon^{2}}(|\rho(x)-\rho(x-y)|^{2}+|\eta(x)-\eta(x-y)|^{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq C+E_{\varepsilon}[\rho,\eta],$ $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)}{\varepsilon^{2}}(|\nabla\rho(x)-\nabla\rho(x-y)|^{2}+|\nabla\eta(x)-\nabla\eta(x-y)|^{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq C+\mathcal{D}\Phi[\rho,\eta](t),$ where $C$ depends on $\kappa$ , $\alpha$ , $\beta$ and $\gamma$ . The main tool to establish nonnegativity (up to a constant) is the following non-local Poincare inequality with parameter which allows to handle negative terms. Lemma 2.2 (Poincare inequality with a parameter) . For all $\delta>0$ there exists $C(\delta)$ and $\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(\delta)$ such that for all $\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}$ and all $f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$ we have $\|f\|_{2}^{2}\leq\delta\,\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}}\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)\frac{|f(x)-f(x-y)|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y+C(\delta)\|f\|_{1}^{2},$ (2.1) $\|\nabla f\|_{2}^{2}\leq\delta\,\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}^{d}}\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)\frac{|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(x-y)|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y+C(\delta)\|f\|_{1}^{2}.$ (2.2) Proof. We prove ( 2.2 ) as ( 2.1 ) is in fact easier. Aiming at a contradiction, suppose that there exists $\delta>0$ with the following property: there exists sequence $\{\varepsilon_{n}\}$ with $0<\varepsilon_{n}<\frac{1}{n}$ and sequence $\{f_{n}\}$ such that $\|\nabla f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}>\delta\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla f_{n}(x)-\nabla f_{n}(y)|^{2}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}\omega_{\varepsilon_{n}}(|x-y|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y+n\,\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.$ As $\|\nabla f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}>0$ , we may define $g_{n}:=\frac{f_{n}}{\|\nabla f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}}$ . Note that $\|\nabla g_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=1$ and $1>\gamma\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{|\nabla g_{n}(x)-\nabla g_{n}(y)|^{2}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{2}}\omega_{\varepsilon_{n}}(|x-y|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y+n\,\|g_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}.$ By Poincare inequality with average, $\{g_{n}\}$ is bounded in $L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$ and so in $H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$ . Moreover, the first term gives compactness of the gradients (because $\{g_{n}\}$ is bounded in $H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$ so that, together with Rellich-Kondrachov, there exists function $g$ such that $g_{n}\to g$ in $H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$ (after passing to a subsequence). But then $g=0$ because $n\,\|g_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}<1$ . This is however contradiction with $\|\nabla g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\nabla g_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=1$ .

∎ 3. Existence of weak solutions to the nonlocal problem To prove the existence of weak solutions for our system, we want to apply the JKO scheme, see [ 41 ] . As $\varepsilon$ is fixed in this Section, we write $(\rho,\eta)$ for the solution, instead of $(\rho_{\varepsilon},\eta_{\varepsilon})$ . Definition 3.1 (Weak solutions) . We say that $u=(\rho(\cdot),\eta(\cdot)):[0,+\infty)\to\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}$ is a weak solution of ( 1.5 )-( 1.6 ) with initial condition $(\rho_{0},\eta_{0})$ satisfying ( 3.4 ) if $\rho,\eta\in C([0,T];\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))$ , $\rho,\eta\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))$ for all $T>0$ , and if for all $\varphi,\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})$ we have $\begin{split}-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\partial_{t}\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t&-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{0}\varphi(0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=-\kappa\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\,\nabla B_{\varepsilon}[\rho]\cdot\nabla\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&-\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho\,\nabla B_{\varepsilon}[\eta]\cdot\nabla\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho(\gamma\nabla\rho-\beta\nabla\eta)\cdot\nabla\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}$ $\begin{split}-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\eta\partial_{t}\phi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t&-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\eta_{0}\phi(0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\eta\,\nabla B_{\varepsilon}[\eta]\cdot\nabla\phi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&-\alpha\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\eta\nabla B_{\varepsilon}[\rho]\cdot\nabla\phi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\eta(\nabla\eta-\beta\nabla\rho)\cdot\nabla\phi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}$ We first rewrite system of equations ( 1.5 )-( 1.6 ) in the following form $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}$ $\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\nabla\left(\tilde{\kappa}\rho-(\tilde{\kappa}+\gamma)\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\rho+\tilde{\alpha}\eta-(\tilde{\alpha}+\beta)\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\eta\right)\right),$ $\displaystyle\text{in}\quad(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d},$ (3.1) $\displaystyle\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial t}$ $\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\nabla\left(\tilde{\alpha}\rho-(\tilde{\alpha}+\beta)\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\rho+\tilde{c}\eta-(\tilde{c}+1)\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\eta\right)\right),$ $\displaystyle\text{in}\quad(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}.$ (3.2) where $\tilde{\kappa}=\frac{\kappa}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\gamma$ , $\tilde{\alpha}=\frac{\alpha}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\beta$ , $\tilde{c}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}-1$ . Using this notation, the energy can be written as $E_{\varepsilon}[\rho,\eta]=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\tilde{\kappa}}{2}\rho^{2}+\frac{\tilde{c}}{2}\eta^{2}+\tilde{\alpha}{\rho\eta}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\frac{\tilde{\kappa}+\gamma}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\rho\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho-\frac{\tilde{c}+1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\eta\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\eta-(\tilde{\alpha}+\beta)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\omega_{\varepsilon}\ast\eta\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho$ (3.3) for $\rho,\eta\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$ . We note that when we use $\rho$ and $\eta$ in expressions of the form $\rho^{2},\eta^{2},\rho\eta$ , we mean in fact their Radon-Nikodym derivatives with respect to the Lebesgue measures. If they exist and belong to $L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$ , the energy above makes sense. Otherwise, we consider that these quantities are $+\infty$ . We denote the Wasserstein distance for vectors $u=(u_{1},u_{2})$ and $v=(v_{1},v_{2})$ as $\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(u,v)=W_{2}^{2}(u_{1},v_{1})+W_{2}^{2}(u_{2},v_{2}),$ for all $u,v\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$ and where $W_{2}$ is the usual Wasserstein distance of order $2$ . For the initial condition, we assume that $(\rho_{0},\eta_{0})\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}$ are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure and satisfy $E_{\varepsilon}[\rho_{0},\eta_{0}]\leq C,\quad\Phi[\rho_{0},\eta_{0}]\leq C,$ (3.4) where $C$ is independent of $\varepsilon$ . In what follows it will be necessary to know when the function $(\rho,\eta)\mapsto\frac{\tilde{\kappa}}{2}\rho^{2}+\frac{\tilde{c}}{2}\eta^{2}+\tilde{\alpha}{\rho\eta}$ . Fortunately, when $\kappa>0$ , $\kappa>\alpha^{2}$ , this is always the case for sufficiently small $\varepsilon$ . Lemma 3.2 . Suppose that $\kappa>0$ , $\kappa>\alpha^{2}$ . Then, there exists $\varepsilon_{0}^{B}>0$ depending on $\kappa$ , $\alpha$ , $\gamma$ , $\beta$ such that for all $\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}^{B})$ the function $(\rho,\eta)\mapsto\frac{\tilde{\kappa}}{2}\rho^{2}+\frac{\tilde{c}}{2}\eta^{2}+\tilde{\alpha}{\rho\eta}$ is convex. Proof. We note that the Hessian matrix reads $\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\kappa}&\tilde{\alpha}\\
\tilde{\alpha}&\tilde{c}\end{pmatrix}$ so by the Sylvester’s criterion, the desired convexity is equivalent with $\tilde{\kappa}>0,\tilde{\kappa}\,\tilde{c}-\tilde{\alpha}^{2}>0.$ Concerning the first condition, as $\kappa>0$ and $\tilde{\kappa}=\frac{\kappa}{\varepsilon^{2}}-\gamma$ , we can easily find $\varepsilon$ such that $\tilde{\kappa}>0$ . Concerning the second condition, we observe that the term standing next to the highest order term $\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}}$ equals $\kappa-\alpha^{2}$ and it is positive by assumption. Therefore, the conclusion follows.
∎ 3.1. Construction of weak solutions Let $T\in(0,\infty)$ be a final time of existence and consider the following scheme: given a time step size $\tau>0$ and an initial condition $u_{0}=(\rho_{0},\eta_{0})\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}$ with $E[u_{0}]<+\infty$ we define by induction $u_{\tau}^{0}:=u_{0},\quad u_{\tau}^{n+1}:=\argmin_{u\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}}\left\{\frac{1}{2\tau}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{n},u)+E_{\varepsilon}[u]\right\},$ (3.5) and we set $E_{\varepsilon}[u]=+\infty$ if $u\notin L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}.$ Lemma 3.3 (Existence of minimizers) . Let $\tau>0$ and $u_{0}=(\rho_{0},\eta_{0})\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}$ with $E_{\varepsilon}[u_{0}]<+\infty$ . Then the scheme defined by ( 3.5 ) is well-defined. Moreover, we have the following energy estimate $E_{\varepsilon}[u_{\tau}^{N}]+\frac{1}{2\tau}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(u_{\tau}^{n},u_{\tau}^{n-1})\leq E_{\varepsilon}[u_{0}].$ (3.6) Proof. Step 1: The infimum is bounded. Provided $u_{\tau}^{n}$ is defined, we notice that $u_{\tau}^{n}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}$ satisfy $\frac{1}{2\tau}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{n},u_{\tau}^{n})+E_{\varepsilon}[u_{\tau}^{n}]=E_{\varepsilon}[u_{\tau}^{n}]<+\infty.$ Therefore the infimum is bounded from above. Since $E_{\varepsilon}[u]$ is bounded from below by Proposition 2.1 and $W_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{n},u)$ is nonnegative for all $u$ we also know that the infimum is bounded from below. Step 2: Candidate for a minimizer. We suppose that $u_{\tau}^{n}$ is defined and we want to define $u_{\tau}^{n+1}$ . Let $\{u^{k}\}_{k}$ be a minimizing sequence in $\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}$ for the problem ( 3.5 ). Without loss of generality, we can assume that for $k$ large enough we have $\frac{1}{2\tau}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{n},u^{k})+E_{\varepsilon}[u^{k}]\leq 2\,E_{\varepsilon}[u_{\tau}^{n}],$ (3.7) since the infimum is bounded by $E_{\varepsilon}[u_{\tau}^{n}]$ . In particular $E_{\varepsilon}[u^{k}]\leq C(u_{\tau}^{n})$ with a constant $C$ independent of $k$ . By definition of the energy in ( 3.3 ), this proves that we can extract from $(u^{k})_{k}$ a subsequence (still denoted by $k$ ) which converges weakly to some $u$ in $L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}$ . Now we consider the function $f(\rho,\eta)=\frac{\tilde{\kappa}}{2}\rho^{2}+\frac{\tilde{c}}{2}\eta^{2}+\tilde{\alpha}{\rho\eta}$ which is convex by Lemma 3.2 . It follows by Tonelli theorem that the functional defined by the first integral of ( 3.3 ) is lower semi-continuous with respect to the $L^{2}$ weak convergence. Since the other terms of ( 3.3 ) are defined with convolutions, these terms are continuous with respect to the $L^{2}$ weak convergence. In the end, $u$ minimizes ( 3.5 ). Finally, $u\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}$ as a direct consequence of the weak convergence. Step 3: The energy estimate ( 3.6 ). Estimate ( 3.6 ) is a consequence of an induction of the inequality $E_{\varepsilon}[u_{\tau}^{n+1}]+\frac{1}{2\tau}\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(u_{\tau}^{n+1},u_{\tau}^{n})\leq E_{\varepsilon}[u_{\tau}^{n}],$ by definition of $u_{\tau}^{n}$ .
∎ We have constructed a discrete in time sequence. We want to prove that a time-interpolation of this sequence converges to a solution of ( 3.1 )-( 3.2 ). Let $T>0$ be fixed and $n=\left[\frac{T}{\tau}\right]$ . We define the interpolation $u_{\tau}(t)=(\rho_{\tau}(t),\eta_{\tau}(t))$ by $\rho_{\tau}(t)=\rho_{\tau}^{n},\quad\eta_{\tau}(t)=\eta_{\tau}^{n},\quad t\in((n-1)\tau,n\tau],$ where $(\rho_{\tau}^{n},\eta_{\tau}^{n})=u_{\tau}^{n}$ defined in ( 3.5 ). We prove that this sequence is compact in the following lemma: Lemma 3.4 (Compactness of the time interpolation sequence) . The sequence of curves $\{u_{\tau}\}_{\tau}$ is uniformly bounded in $L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))$ . Moreover, for all $T>0$ , there exists an absolutely continuous curve $u:[0,T]\to\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2}$ such that up to a subsequence, $u_{\tau}(t,\cdot)$ converges to $u(t,\cdot)$ weakly in $L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$ as $\tau\to 0$ for all $t\in[0,T]$ and $u_{\tau}$ converges to $u$ in $C([0,T],\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{2})$ as $\tau\to 0$ for all $T>0$ . From ( 3.6 ) and by definition of $u_{\tau}$ which takes discrete values in time we have that $E_{\varepsilon}[u_{\tau}(t)]+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t-\tau}\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}_{2}(u_{\tau}(s+\tau),u_{\tau}(s))}{\tau}\right)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\leq E_{\varepsilon}[u_{0}].$ (3.9) With Proposition 2.1 , we obtain $\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)}{\varepsilon^{2}}|u_{\tau}(x)-u_{\tau}(x-y)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq C(1+E_{\varepsilon}[u_{0}])$ (3.10) so that by Lemma 2.1 we deduce uniform estimate in $L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))$ . To prove weak compactness for all times $t\in[0,T]$ and Hölder continuity in time for the limiting curve, it is sufficient to prove $\limsup_{\tau\to 0}\mathcal{W}_{2}(u_{\tau}(s),u_{\tau}(t))\leq C\,\sqrt{|t-s|}$ for some constant $C$ and apply [ 1 , Proposition 3.3.1] (with $d$ being $\mathcal{W}_{2}$ distance, $\sigma$ being weak topology on $L^{2}(\Omega)$ , $K$ being the ball in $L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$ such that $u_{\tau}(s)\in K$ for all $s\in[0,T]$ , $\mathcal{C}=\emptyset$ and $\omega(t,s)=\sqrt{t-s}$ ). To this end, we write for $0\leq s<t$ such that $s\in((m-1)\tau,m\tau]$ and $t\in((n-1)\tau,n\tau]$ $\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}(u_{\tau}(s),u_{\tau}(t))$ $\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=m}^{n-1}\mathcal{W}_{2}(u_{\tau}^{i},u_{\tau}^{i+1})\leq\left(\sum_{i=m}^{n-1}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(u_{\tau}^{i},u_{\tau}^{i+1})\right)^{1/2}|n-m|^{1/2}$ $\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2E_{\varepsilon}[u_{0}]}\sqrt{|t-s|+\tau},$ where in the last line we used ( 3.6 ) and $|n-m|<\frac{|t-s|}{\tau}+1$ . This concludes the proof.
∎ 3.2. $H^{1}$ estimates for the JKO scheme via flow interchange lemma The weak convergence of the JKO scheme is not enough to pass to the limit in the definition of weak solutions. We need to obtain better estimates on the solutions. In the Cahn-Hilliard equation, better estimates are derived from considering the functional $\mathcal{U}[\rho,\eta]=\int\rho\log\rho+\eta\log\eta$ . This functional generates the heat flow with respect to the Wasserstein distance $\mathcal{W}_{2}$ . To improve the regularity we, therefore, use the flow interchange lemma which states that the dissipation of one functional along the gradient flow of another functional equals the dissipation of the second functional along the gradient flow of the first one . The main result of this section reads: Proposition 3.5 . Each solution of the JKO scheme ( 3.5 ) satisfies $u_{\tau}^{n}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\quad\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$, $\tau>0$}$ and the time-interpolation $u_{\tau}=(\rho_{\tau},\eta_{\tau})$ satisfies $\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\omega_{\varepsilon}(y)}{\varepsilon^{2}}(|\nabla\rho_{\tau}(t,x)-\nabla\rho_{\tau}(t,x-y)|^{2}+|\nabla\eta_{\tau}(t,x)-\nabla\eta_{\tau}(t,x-y)|^{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq CT,$ for all $T>0$ .
Moreover, for every sequence $\tau_{k}\downarrow 0$ , we can extract a subsequence (still denoted by $\tau_{k}$ ) such that for all $T>0$ , $\displaystyle u_{\tau_{k}}$ $\displaystyle\to u\text{ strongly in $L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})$}$ $\displaystyle u_{\tau_{k}}$ $\displaystyle\rightharpoonup u\text{ weakly in $L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))$}.$ The main tool to prove Proposition 3.5 will be the following lemma. Furthermore, $g$ is lower semicontinuous [ 54 , Chapter 6] . Finally, ( B.1 ) follows from uniform estimates in $L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))$ and estimate ( 3.9 ). Therefore, Theorem B.1 gives us a subsequence (not relabelled) such that $\|u_{n}(t,\cdot)-u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\to 0\mbox{ for a.e. }t\in[0,T].$ As sequence $\{u_{n}\}$ is bounded in $L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))$ , the conclusion follows by dominated convergence theorem.

Since ( 3.5 ) was derived from the Lagrangian point of view, the idea is to investigate its first variation and prove that it is a time-discrete scheme of ( 3.1 ). For this, we introduce a suitable perturbation of $u_{\tau}^{n+1}=(\rho_{\tau}^{n+1},\eta_{\tau}^{n+1})$ as follows: let $\xi$ be a smooth periodic vector field and $T_{\delta}=\Id+\delta\xi$ . It is classical to prove that for $\delta$ small enough, $T_{\delta}$ is a $C^{1}$ diffeomorphism and $\det(\nabla T_{\delta})>0$ . Then, we define $\tilde{u}_{\delta}=(T_{\delta}\#\rho_{\tau}^{n+1},\eta_{\tau}^{n+1}).$ Note carefully that only the first component was perturbed. This will result in the first equation in Definition 3.1 . To obtain the second one, it is sufficient to introduce a similar perturbation on the second component of $u_{\tau}^{n+1}$ . This results in analogous computations as outlined below and we do not repeat them. A version of Lions-Aubin for JKO scheme. We recall here from [ 29 , Theorem 2.1] a version of Lions-Aubin lemma useful for establishing compactness of a sequence of solutions to JKO scheme. For the proof we refer to [ 49 , Theorem 2] . Theorem B.1 . Let $(X,\|\cdot\|_{X})$ be a Banach space. We consider • a lower semi-continuous functional $\mathcal{F}:X\to[0,+\infty]$ with relatively compact sublevels in $X$ , • a pseudo-distance $g:X\times X\to[0,+\infty]$ , that is $g$ is lower semicontinuous and $g(\rho,\eta)=0$ for some $\rho,\eta\in X$ such that $\mathcal{F}(\rho),\mathcal{F}(\eta)<\infty$ implies $\rho=\eta$ . Let $U$ be a set of measurable functions $u:(0,T)\times X$ with $T>0$ fixed. Assume further that $\sup_{u\in U}\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{F}(u(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t<\infty,\qquad\lim_{h\to 0}\sup_{u\in U}g(u(t+h),u(t))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=0.$ (B.1) Then, $U$ contains a sequence $\{u_{n}\}$ converging in measure to some $u\in X$ , i.e. $\forall_{\varepsilon>0}\left|\{t\in[0,T]:\|u_{n}-u\|_{X}>\varepsilon\}\right|\to 0\mbox{ as }n\to\infty.$ In particular, there exists a subsequence (not relabelled) such that $u_{n}(t)\to u(t)\mbox{ in }X\mbox{ for a.e. }t\in[0,T].$ B.2. Nonlocal version of Lions-Aubin lemma The following result was proved in [ 32 , Theorem B.1] based on [ 11 ] and [ 48 ] . In fact, the proof of Theorem B.2 is the proof of Proposition 1.3 integrated in time. Theorem B.2 . Let $d\geq 2$ . Let $\{f_{\varepsilon}\}$ be a sequence bounded in $L^{p}((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})$ . Suppose that there exists a sequence $\{\rho_{\varepsilon}\}$ as above such that $\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{|f_{\varepsilon}(t,x)-f_{\varepsilon}(t,y)|^{p}}{\varepsilon^{p}}\omega_{\varepsilon}(|x-y|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq C$ (B.2) for some constant $C$ . Then, $\{f_{\varepsilon}\}$ is compact in space in $L^{p}((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})$ , i.e. $\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|f_{\varepsilon}\ast\varphi_{\delta}(t,x)-f_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=0$ (B.3) for all families of mollifiers $\{\varphi_{\delta}\}_{0<\delta<1}$ . We prove here: Lemma B.3 . Suppose that $\{f_{\varepsilon}\}$ is a sequence bounded in $L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})$ such that • $\{\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}\}$ is uniformly bounded in $L^{2}(0,T;H^{-k}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))$ for some $k\in\mathbb{N}$ , • $\{f_{\varepsilon}\}$ is compact in space in $L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})$ , i.e. $\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|f_{\varepsilon}\ast\varphi_{\delta}(t,x)-f_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=0$ (B.4) for some family of mollifiers $\{\varphi_{\delta}\}_{0<\delta<1}$ . Then, $\{f_{\varepsilon}\}$ is compact in time in $L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})$ , i.e. $\lim_{h\to 0}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{T-h}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|f_{\varepsilon}(t+h,x)-f_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\to 0\mbox{ as }h\to 0$ (B.5) and so, it is compact in $L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})$ . Proof. Using the mollifiers with $\delta=\delta(h)$ depending on $h$ to be specified later in the way that $\delta(h)\to 0$ as $h\to 0$ , we first split $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-h}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|f_{\varepsilon}(t+h,x)-f_{\varepsilon}(t,x)$ $\displaystyle|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq 4\int_{0}^{T-h}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|f_{\varepsilon}(t,x)-f_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t$ $\displaystyle+4\int_{0}^{T-h}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|f_{\varepsilon}(t+h,x)-f_{\varepsilon}(t+h,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t$ $\displaystyle+4\int_{0}^{T-h}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|f_{\varepsilon}(t+h,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}(x)-f_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.$ When we apply limit $\lim_{h\to 0}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}$ , the first and second term vanish due to ( B.4 ).
It remains to study the third term. For this, suppose first that $f_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)$ is smooth in the time variable. Then, $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-h}$ $\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|f_{\varepsilon}(t+h,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}(x)-f_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T-h}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left|\int_{t}^{t+h}\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}(s,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.$ Now, we can estimate the convolution as follows $\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}(s,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}(s,y)\,\varphi_{\delta}(x-y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq\|\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}(s,\cdot)\|_{H^{-k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\,\|\varphi_{\delta}(x-\cdot)\|_{H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}.$ Using this and applying invariance in space of the $H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})$ norm we obtain $\int_{0}^{T-h}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left|\int_{t}^{t+h}\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}(s,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=h^{2}\,\|\varphi_{\delta}\|_{H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\int_{0}^{T-h}\left|\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}\|\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}(s,\cdot)\|_{H^{-k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.$ Applying Jensen’s inequality we obtain $h^{2}\,\|\varphi_{\delta}\|_{H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\int_{0}^{T-h}\left|\frac{1}{h}\int_{t}^{t+h}\|\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}(s,\cdot)\|_{H^{-k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq Th\,\|\varphi_{\delta}\|_{H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\|\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{-k}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}^{2}.$ Using that $\|\varphi_{\delta}\|_{H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq\frac{C}{\delta^{k+d/2}}$ we finally obtain $\int_{0}^{T-h}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|f_{\varepsilon}(t+h,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}(x)-f_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)\ast\varphi_{\delta}(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq CT\frac{h}{{\delta^{k+d/2}}}\,\|\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{-k}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))}^{2}.$ Now, if $f_{\varepsilon}(t,x)$ is not smooth in time, we extend it with $f_{\varepsilon}(0,x)$ for $t<0$ , $f_{\varepsilon}(T,x)$ for $t>T$ and apply usual regularization to obtain the same estimate. Hence, if we choose $h(\delta)=\delta^{2k+d}$ we conclude $\lim_{h\to 0}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{T-h}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|f_{\varepsilon}(t+h,x)-f_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\leq\theta(h).$ Combined with the compactness in space ( B.5 ) and the Fréchet-Kolmogorov theorem we obtain the compactness of $\{f_{\varepsilon}\}$ in $L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d})$ .
