Examples include aggregation and pattern formation in microbial populations, as well as coordinated cell migration in developmental and pathological processes.
These observations have inspired extensive mathematical studies of chemotaxis models, most notably those based on systems of partial differential equations. Among them, the Keller–Segel model (cf. ( 1.2 ) below), originally introduced by Keller and Segel [ 28 ] to describe the migration of cells guided by chemical signals generated by the cell population itself, has long served as a fundamental model in the study of chemotaxis. Originally motivated by biological observations, the Keller–Segel model has also stimulated extensive mathematical research over the years, leading to a deeper understanding of its structural and dynamical properties (see [ 5 , 12 , 13 , 22 , 23 , 37 , 35 , 36 , 3 ] ).
In this paper, we consider the Cauchy problem for the chemotaxis system: $\begin{cases}u_{t}=\Delta u-\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v)\qquad&\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty),\\
0=\Delta v+w\qquad&\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty),\\
w_{t}=\Delta w+u\qquad&\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty),\\
(u(\cdot,0),w(\cdot,0))=(u_{0}(\cdot),w_{0}(\cdot))\qquad&\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{cases}$ (P) The system ( P ) features an indirect signal production mechanism, where the chemoattractant potential $v$ is determined elliptically from an intermediate variable $w$ , while $w$ itself evolves according to a parabolic equation driven by the cell density $u$ . The system ( P ) can be regarded as a simplified version of the chemotaxis model: $\begin{cases}u_{t}=\Delta u-\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v)&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega\times(0,\infty),\\
\tau_{1}v_{t}=\gamma_{1}\Delta v-\delta_{1}v+w&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega\times(0,\infty),\\
\tau_{2}w_{t}=\gamma_{2}\Delta w-\delta_{2}w+u&\mathrm{in}\ \Omega\times(0,\infty),\end{cases}$ (1.1) where $\Omega$ is either a bounded domain or the whole space and the parameters $\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}$ are nonnegative. The chemotaxis model ( 1.1 ) is a specific instance of a system proposed by [ 31 ] in the context of biological processes related to Alzheimer’s disease. In particular, the model ( 1.1 ) with $\gamma_{2}=0$ has also been used to model the cluster attack behavior of the Mountain Pine Beetle, as proposed in [ 40 ] .
From a mathematical point of view, the chemotaxis model ( 1.1 ) has been studied in various parameter regimes.
In the fully parabolic case, several analytical results are available in the literature (see [ 17 , 18 , 24 , 25 ] , for instance). On the other hand, when $\gamma_{2}=0$ the system reduces to a parabolic–parabolic–ODE (or parabolic–elliptic–ODE) type, for which different aspects of solutions behavior have been investigated in recent years (see [ 30 , 39 , 41 ] , for instance). 1.2 Mass critical phenomena in critical dimensions The system ( P ) can be viewed as a variant of the classical Keller–Segel system: $\begin{cases}u_{t}=\Delta u-\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v),\\
\tau v_{t}=\Delta v+u\end{cases}$ (1.2) in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ , where the parameter $\tau$ is nonnegative. The Keller–Segel system enjoys the scaling invariance: $\displaystyle u_{\lambda}(x,t)=\lambda^{2}u(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t),\quad v_{\lambda}(x,t)=v(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t)\quad\mathrm{for}\ \lambda>0.$ (1.3) In addition, from a biological modeling perspective, the Keller–Segel system is expected to satisfy the mass conservation law $\|u(t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}$ , which follows from the fact that the first equation is written in divergence form. Consequently, the $L^{1}$ -norm of $u$ plays a distinguished role. The scaling leaves the $L^{1}$ -norm invariant if and only if the spatial dimension satisfies $d=2$ , which therefore constitutes the critical dimension. In view of the above observations, the two-dimensional case occupies a distinguished position in the theory of the Keller–Segel system, both in bounded domains and in the whole space. Indeed, in two space dimensions, the long-time behavior of solutions of ( 1.2 ) is known to depend sensitively on the total initial mass. This criticality gives rise to a threshold phenomenon separating global existence from blow-up, which is commonly referred to as the $8\pi$ -problem. More precisely, when the initial cell density $u_{0}$ with radial symmetry is given by a nonnegative measure whose total mass satisfies $\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}}<8\pi$ , the corresponding radial solution remains well-defined for all $t>0$ and does not develop singularities (see [ 36 , 12 , 10 , 33 , 14 ] ). While if $\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}}>8\pi$ , then a finite time blow-up may occur (see [ 37 , 35 , 22 , 23 , 34 , 10 ] ). The systems ( P ) and ( 1.1 ) with $\delta_{1}=\delta_{2}=0$ admit a scaling transformation analogous to that of the Keller–Segel system.
Nevertheless, due to its indirect signal production mechanism, the associated scaling properties lead to a distinct notion of criticality. Indeed these systems are invariant under the natural scaling: for $\lambda>0$ $\displaystyle u_{\lambda}(x,t)=\lambda^{4}u(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t),\quad v_{\lambda}(x,t)=v(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t),\quad w_{\lambda}(x,t)=\lambda^{2}w(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t).$ (1.4) Under this scaling, the critical dimension in the above sense is $d=4$ . Indeed, in [ 17 , 18 ] , the authors revealed several characteristic
features of solutions to ( 1.1 ) in the critical dimension $d=4$ on bounded domains. Here, we restrict ourselves to the case where all parameters are set equal to one. If one has the assumption that the initial data $(u_{0},v_{0},w_{0})$ satisfies $\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}<(8\pi)^{2}$ , then the corresponding solution of ( 1.1 ) exists globally in time and remains uniformly bounded in time, whereas it was shown that there exists the initial data such that $\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\in((8\pi)^{2},\infty)\setminus(8\pi)^{2}\mathbb{N}$ and the corresponding solution blows up. In the whole space $\mathbb{R}^{4}$ , it was proved in [ 24 ] that the solution to ( 1.1 ) exists globally in time whenever $\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{4})}<(8\pi)^{2}$ . Such mass critical phenomena are characteristic of scale critical dimensions and are absent in higher, supercritical settings. This naturally raises the question of what determines the long-time dynamics of solutions to ( P ) and ( 1.1 ), and ( 1.2 ) when the systems are considered in supercritical dimensions. 1.3 Parabolic-elliptic model in higher dimensions $d\geq 3$ In this subsection, we consider the Keller–Segel system ( 1.2 ) with $\tau=0$ corresponding to the parabolic-elliptic case: $\begin{cases}u_{t}=\Delta u-\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v)\qquad&\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty),\\
0=\Delta v+u\qquad&\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)\end{cases}$ (1.5) and discuss the long-time behavior of its solutions in supercritical dimensions.
As noted in previous subsection, a central issue is to identify the mechanisms that control the long-time behavior of solutions. One possible perspective for addressing this issue is provided by the existence of a singular stationary solution, such as the well-known Chandrasekhar steady state solution in [ 15 ] $\displaystyle u_{C}(x)=\dfrac{2(d-2)}{|x|^{2}}.$ Indeed, such singular stationary solution plays a crucial role in the analysis
in supercritical dimensions of the solutions to the simplified Keller–Segel system ( 1.5 ). In this context, two different criteria have been employed
to characterize the role of the singular stationary solution $u_{C}$ in the dynamics of ( 1.5 ). One approach is based on conditions formulated in terms of critical Morrey norms. For instance, according to [ 7 ] ,
if the initial data $u_{0}$ with radially symmetry belongs to the critical Morrey space $M^{\frac{d}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ which are invariant under the scaling argument ( 1.3 ), and satisfies the upper bound $\displaystyle\|u_{0}\|_{M^{\frac{d}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<2\sigma_{d}=\|u_{C}\|_{M^{\frac{d}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},$ where $\sigma_{d}$ is the measure of the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ ,
then corresponding solution of ( 1.5 ) exists globally in time in an appropriate functional class and enjoys the same bound $\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{M^{\frac{d}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<2\sigma_{d}\quad\mathrm{for\ all}\ t>0.$ Moreover, it was also demonstrated in [ 38 ] that for some initial data with $\|u_{0}\|_{M^{\frac{d}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}>2\sigma_{d}$ , the behavior of the corresponding solution to ( 1.5 ) depends on the spatial dimension $d$ .
In dimensions $d\geq 10$ , one can choose such $u_{0}$ so that the corresponding solution of ( 1.5 ) blows up in finite time,
whereas in dimensions $3\leq d\leq 9$ , there exists initial data with the same condition such that the corresponding solution of ( 1.5 ) exists globally in time. Another approach focuses on a direct comparison between the initial data and the singular stationary solution itself. Recently, [ 45 ] established that, whenever the initial data $u_{0}\in BUC(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ satisfies $u_{0}\leq u_{C}$ , ( 1.5 ) admits a unique global classical solution and the solution enjoys the upper bound: $\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{M^{\frac{d}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq 2\sigma_{d}\quad\mathrm{for\ all}\ t>0.$ Furthermore, it was shown in [ 45 ] that, in dimensions $d\geq 10$ , if initial data satisfies the asymptotic condition: $\displaystyle\liminf_{|x|\to\infty}|x|^{2}u_{0}(x)>2(d-2),$ which implies that the decay of $u_{0}$ at infinity is slower than that of the singular stationary solution $u_{C}$ , then the corresponding solution blows up in finite time. Consequently, in supercritical dimensions, the behavior of initial data at spatial infinity is key to the analysis of the long-time dynamics of solutions to ( 1.5 ).
In particular, the singular stationary solution $u_{C}$ appears to act as a critical threshold separating different qualitative behaviors of solutions to ( 1.5 ). 1.4 Statement of the problem The aim of the present paper is to provide a detailed description of the behavior of the solutions to ( P ) in the supercritical case $d\geq 5$ . As noted in the previous subsection, extensive studies on supercritical dimensions have been carried out for the simplified Keller–Segel system ( 1.5 ).
These results crucially rely on the fact that, in the parabolic-elliptic setting, the second equation reduces to a Poisson-type equation. A key feature of the Poisson-type structure is that it effectively reduces the system to a single equation for the cell density through the use of a so-called mass function. In fact, once the mass function is defined in this manner $U(r,t)=r^{-d}\int_{0}^{r}\rho^{d-1}u(\rho,t)d\rho$ , the simplified Keller–Segel system ( 1.5 ) can be transformed into a single equation governing its evolution as follows: $\displaystyle U_{t}=U_{rr}+\dfrac{d+1}{r}U_{r}+rU_{r}U+dU^{2}.$ At the same time,
this formulation allows one to make extensive use of comparison principles (see [ 45 , 38 , 7 ] ).
Moreover, the simplified Keller–Segel system ( 1.5 ) and the resulting formulation above exhibits a superlinear structure,
which plays a crucial role in the analysis of aggregation and blow-up phenomena. By contrast, the system ( P ) remains intrinsically coupled and does not admit a reduction to a single equation nor a superlinear structure. Accordingly, in contrast to the classical setting, the present paper is devoted to clarifying the behavior of the solutions to ( P ) in the supercritical case. Since the analytical framework available for the simplified Keller–Segel system is not applicable to ( P ), alternative approaches are required. In this paper, we place particular emphasis on the stationary problem corresponding to ( P ), which we regard as a key step toward understanding the dynamics of solutions to ( P ). The associated stationary system reads $\begin{cases}0=\Delta u-\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v)\qquad&\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty),\\
0=\Delta v+w\qquad&\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty),\\
0=\Delta w+u\qquad&\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty).\end{cases}$ (1.6) By combining the second and third equations, this system is equivalently reduced to the following problem: $\begin{cases}0=\Delta u-\nabla\cdot(u\nabla v)\qquad&\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty),\\
\Delta^{2}v=u\qquad&\mathrm{in}\ \mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty).\end{cases}$ (1.7) Here focusing on the first equation in ( 1.7 ), one can rewrite it as $\nabla\cdot(u\nabla(\log u-v))=0$ in $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ . Hence, if $u>0$ , the stationary problem formally indicates that $u$ is comparable to $e^{v}$ .
This heuristic relation will be rigorously justified later (see Proposition 6.1 ).
Consequently we consider a radially symmetric solution of the forth-order elliptic problem: $\begin{cases}\Delta^{2}\phi=e^{\phi}\qquad\mathrm{for}\ r\in[0,\mathrm{R}(\alpha,\beta)),\\
\phi(0)=\log\alpha,\ \Delta\phi(0)=\beta,\ \phi^{\prime}(0)=(\Delta\phi)^{\prime}(0)=0,\end{cases}$ (1.8) where $\alpha>0$ and $\beta\in\mathbb{R}$ , and $\mathrm{R}(\alpha,\beta)$ is the maximal interval of existence.
Then it is known from [ 2 , 4 ] that for any $\alpha>0$ there exists a unique $\beta_{0}(\alpha)\in[-4de^{\frac{\alpha}{2}},0)$ such that if $\beta=\beta_{0}(\alpha)$ , then $R(\alpha,\beta)=\infty$ and moreover the corresponding unique radial solution $\phi\in C^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ satisfies the following asymptotic profile: $\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}(\phi(r)+4\log r)=\log 8(d-4)(d-2).$ Furthermore, thanks to the refined analysis developed in [ 21 ] , the asymptotic behavior of the radial solution $\phi$ near infinity is known in much greater detail.
In order to clarify the behavior of solutions to ( P ) in the supercritical case, we make use of the radially symmetric solution $\phi$ to the stationary problem ( 1.8 ) associated with ( P ), characterized by the above asymptotic profile. 1.5 Notation Before stating our main results, we introduce several definitions and remarks
that will be used throughout the paper. Throughout the paper, we work with the homogeneous Morrey spaces $M^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ of measurable functions on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , defined by the norm (see [ 20 , Section 7.9] ): $\displaystyle\|f\|_{M^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{R>0}R^{d(\frac{1}{p}-1)}\int_{|x-y|<R}|f(y)|dy<\infty.$ We also set $M^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ and $M^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ .
In view of the scaling ( 1.4 ), the Morrey spaces $M^{\frac{d}{4}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ and $M^{\frac{d}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ naturally arise as the critical functional framework
for the analysis of ( P ), corresponding to the variables $u$ and $w$ , respectively. Moreover, for $p\in[1,\infty)$ , we define the subspace $\dot{M}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ of $M^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ by $\displaystyle\dot{M}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\{f\in M^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d});\limsup_{R\to 0}R^{d(\frac{1}{p}-1)}\int_{|x-y|<R}|f(y)|dy=0\}$ (see [ 27 , 11 , 42 ] ). This subspace is essential for constructing local-in-time solutions to ( P )
in the critical Morrey spaces $M^{\frac{d}{4}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ and $M^{\frac{d}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ .
Here we basically consider radially symmetric solutions of ( P ) in this paper.
In this case, the norms of the critical Morrey spaces $M^{\frac{d}{4}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ and $M^{\frac{d}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ are equivalent to $\displaystyle\sup_{R>0}R^{4-d}\int_{|y|<R}|f(y)|dy,\quad\sup_{R>0}R^{2-d}\int_{|y|<R}|f(y)|dy,$ respectively (see [ 9 , Proposition 7.1] ).
Accordingly, in the radial setting considered in this paper, we shall adopt the above expressions as the definitions of the norms on $M^{\frac{d}{4}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ and $M^{\frac{d}{2}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ . Moreover, in the radial setting, the Laplacian of a radially symmetric function $f=f(r)$ is given by $\displaystyle\Delta f(r):=f_{rr}(r)+\dfrac{d-1}{r}f_{r}(r).$ As usual, by the Taylor expansion, the value at the origin is defined by $\Delta f(0):=df_{rr}(0)$ . Finally, we note that there are several natural ways to treat the second equation in ( P ), which is of Poisson type. One approach is to assume that $v$ is given by the convolution $\displaystyle v=E_{d}*w,$ where $E_{d}$ denotes the fundamental solution of the Laplacian, given by $\displaystyle E_{d}(x)=\dfrac{1}{(d-2)\sigma_{d}}\dfrac{1}{|x|^{d-2}}\quad\mathrm{for}\ d\geq 3,$ where $\sigma_{d}$ is the measure of the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ . A second approach is to take advantage of radial symmetry and define $v$ by an integral formula: $\displaystyle v(x,t):=-\int_{0}^{|x|}s^{1-d}\int_{0}^{s}\rho^{d-1}w(\rho,t)d\rho ds+C\quad\mathrm{for}\ (x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)$ (1.9) with some constant $C$ . We also denote the radial inverse of the Laplacian $(-\Delta)^{-1}$ by the following integral form $\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{-1}f(x):=-\int_{0}^{|x|}s^{1-d}\int_{0}^{s}\rho^{d-1}f(\rho)d\rho ds\quad\mathrm{for}\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}$ and extend it to $r=0$ by continuity, so that $(-\Delta)^{-1}f(0)=0$ . Throughout this paper, we shall employ these two definitions selectively,
according to the requirements of each theorem. 1.6 Main results Our main results are stated as follows. Theorem 1.1 (Singular stationary solutions) . Let $d\geq 7$ and $\displaystyle u_{C}(x)=\dfrac{8(d-4)(d-2)}{|x|^{4}},\quad w_{C}(x)=\dfrac{4(d-2)}{|x|^{2}},\quad v_{C}(x)=-4\log|x|+C$ for some constant $C$ . Then $(u_{C},v_{C},w_{C})$ is a distributional, stationary solution to the system ( P ). Remark 1.2 . This stands in sharp contrast to the simplified Keller–Segel system ( 1.5 ), whose singular stationary state is generated by the Chandrasekhar steady state singular solution $|x|^{-2}$ . The following theorems yield an essentially complete classification for the behavior of the solutions to ( P ) in supercritical dimensions. Theorem 1.8 . Let $\phi\in C^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ be the radial solution of ( 1.8 ) with $\alpha>0$ and $\beta_{0}(\alpha)$ and assume the radially symmetric nonnegative initial data $(u_{0},w_{0})\in(C(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{2}$ . Remark 1.9 . Remark 1.11 . Theorem 1.10 shows that, for $d\geq 13$ , the values $8(d-2)\sigma_{d}$ and $4\sigma_{d}$ serve as critical thresholds in the corresponding
Morrey spaces, across which the long-time behavior of solutions to ( P ) changes. In this sense, these values are optimal. On the other hand, when $5\leq d\leq 12$ , the situation is different in this respect.
Therefore, it appears highly interesting to investigate the long-time behavior of
solutions in this dimensional range. Remark 1.12 . For the essentially parabolic-parabolic chemotaxis system ( P ), Theorem 1.8 and Theorem 1.10 , which yield a sharp criterion and a complete classification of global existence and finite time blow-up of solutions, have not been previously available. Therefore, our approach and results of the present paper are expected to be of independent interest. 1.7 Strategy and plan of the paper The main strategy and the plan of this paper are summarized as follows.
A central difficulty in the analysis of system ( P ) lies in the fact that,
unlike the classical parabolic-elliptic Keller–Segel system ( 1.5 ), it does not reduce to a single equation nor possess an explicit superlinear structure.
As a consequence, many techniques developed for the Keller–Segel system cannot be applied directly. To overcome this difficulty, we introduce and exploit new comparison principles for the radial version of the original system ( P ), together with suitably defined mass functions ( 2.6 ) and ( 2.7 ) associated with ( P ).
These comparison principles, developed in Section 2 , form a key analytical tool.
More precisely, the comparison principle established for the original system
( P ) allows us to construct local-in-time solutions from the initial data $(u_{0},w_{0})\in(C(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{2}$ .
In contrast, the comparison principle for the associated mass functions serves as a key ingredient in controlling the dynamical behavior of solutions to the system ( 2.8 )
for the mass function. In Section 3 , we establish local-in-time existence of solutions to ( P ) in appropriate function spaces. As a first step, we construct local-in-time solutions with $v=E_{d}*w$ in the scaling-critical Morrey spaces.
Such solutions are obtained by a standard application of the Banach fixed point
theorem (see [ 7 , 11 , 17 , 24 ] ). As a second approach, we construct the solutions using the comparison principle established in Section 2 and radial symmetry.
In this case, the potential $v$ is expressed through a radial integral representation ( 1.9 ). In Section 4 , we present the proofs of Theorem 1.1 and Theorem 1.3 .
In particular, the proof of Theorem 1.3 is inspired by the approach developed in [ 7 ] .
However, since the system under consideration is essentially of
parabolic-parabolic type, consisting of two genuinely coupled equations,
additional care is required to adapt this method to the present setting. In Section 5 , we develop the analytical framework needed to prove convergence of solutions toward the stationary state. However, we cannot rely on arguments based on the parabolic-elliptic structure of the simplified Keller–Segel system ( 1.5 ) (see [ 45 , 38 ] ). Instead, we construct a novel subsolution to the system ( 2.8 ) satisfied by the mass functions that exhibits finite time blow-up. This approach enables us to prove that infinite time blow-up can occur only
at the origin, which in turn leads to convergence of the mass functions
toward the stationary state.
We stress that the novelty of our approach lies not only in the construction
of a new blowing up subsolution itself,
but also in the strategy behind its construction,
which may have further applications to other nonlinear
parabolic systems beyond the classical Keller–Segel system. In Section 6 , we investigate the convergence toward stationary solutions and
their qualitative properties.
We first show that the stationary problem ( 1.6 ) associated with ( P ) coincides with the forth-order elliptic problem ( 1.8 ).
This fact, together with the assumptions of Theorem 1.5 , allows the comparison principles developed earlier to be applied effectively in the proof of Theorem 1.5 (see the next section). Next, we establish convergence of the mass functions toward their stationary states.
After that, starting from the stationary mass functions, we construct the
corresponding stationary solutions to the original system ( P ). Unlike approaches that focus primarily on the stationary mass function (see [ 45 , 38 ] ), we place particular emphasis on the analysis of the asymptotic
behavior of stationary solutions to the original problem ( P ).
This constitutes a new perspective in the study of the system. In Section 7 , we provide the proof of Theorem 1.5 based on the results established
in the preceding sections.
A key point of the argument is that the proof relies on a detailed analysis of
the stationary solutions to the original problem ( P ), rather than on the stationary problem for the mass functions. Finally, in Section 8 , motivated by the results [ 21 , 4 ] showing that the asymptotic behavior of radially symmetric solutions to ( 1.8 ) depends on the
space dimension, we present the proofs of Theorem 1.8 and Theorem 1.10 . 2 Comparison principles In this section, we develop the comparison principles that serve as a fundamental tool in proving the main theorems of this paper. ∎ 5 The existence of blow up points at infinite time via Comparison Principles In this section, we develop the arguments required to establish convergence toward the stationary state.

Hence owing to the monotonicity in $t>0$ for $(M_{\lambda},W_{\lambda})$ , Proposition 6.4 enables us to get the limit function $(M_{\lambda,\infty},W_{\lambda,\infty})\in[C^{2}((0,\infty))\cap L^{\infty}(r_{0},\infty)]^{2}$ for any $r_{0}>0$ satisfying ( 6.6 ) and moreover, $\displaystyle r(M_{\lambda,\infty})_{r}+dM_{\lambda,\infty}\geq 0\quad\mathrm{for}\ r\in(0,\infty).$ (7.12) Gathering the monotonicity in $t>0$ for $(M_{\lambda},W_{\lambda})$ and ( 7.11 ), we see that for all $(r,t)\in(0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)$ $\displaystyle M_{\lambda,0}(r)\leq M_{\lambda}(r,t)\leq M_{\lambda,\infty}(r),\quad W_{\lambda,0}(r)\leq W_{\lambda}(r,t)\leq W_{\lambda,\infty}(r).$ (7.13) As observed in the proof of Proposition 6.1 (see ( 6.4 ), ( 6.5 )), the asymptotic behavior for $(e^{\phi},(-\Delta)\phi)$ allows us to apply L’Hospital’s rule and obtain $\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{4}M_{\lambda,0}(r)$ $\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to\infty}\dfrac{\lambda\int_{0}^{r}\rho^{d-1}e^{\phi(\rho)}d\rho}{r^{d-4}}$ $\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to\infty}\dfrac{\lambda}{d-4}r^{4}e^{\phi(r)}$ $\displaystyle=8(d-2)\lambda>8(d-2).$ (7.14) A similar argument applies to $W_{\lambda,0}$ , leading to $\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{2}W_{\lambda,0}(r)=4\lambda>4.$ (7.15) By combining ( 7.14 ) and ( 7.15 ) with ( 7.13 ), we deduce that $\displaystyle\liminf_{r\to\infty}r^{4}M_{\lambda,\infty}(r)\geq\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{4}M_{\lambda,0}(r)>8(d-2)$ (7.16) as well as $\displaystyle\liminf_{r\to\infty}r^{2}W_{\lambda,\infty}(r)\geq\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{2}W_{\lambda,0}(r)>4.$ (7.17) The asymptotic behavior of $(M_{\lambda,\infty},W_{\lambda,\infty})$ has been derived.
Our next goal is to examine the corresponding asymptotics of the stationary solution $(u_{\lambda,\infty},v_{\lambda,\infty},w_{\lambda,\infty})$ of ( P ).
This can be accomplished by applying Proposition 6.6 , which links the mass functions to the original variables.
At this stage, exploiting ( 7.12 ) and ( 7.17 ) together with the definition of $(u_{\lambda,\infty},v_{\lambda,\infty},w_{\lambda,\infty})$ given in Proposition 6.6 , we arrive at the estimate, valid for sufficiently large $r$ $\displaystyle(r^{4}u_{\lambda,\infty})_{r}$ $\displaystyle=r^{3}(r(M_{\lambda,\infty})_{r}+dM_{\lambda,\infty})(4-r^{2}W_{\lambda,\infty})\leq 0.$ In fact, by setting $\displaystyle\Phi(r)=r(M_{\lambda,\infty})_{r}+dM_{\lambda,\infty},$ the system ( 6.6 ), which is satisfied by $(M_{\lambda,\infty},W_{\lambda,\infty})$ , yields $\displaystyle\Phi_{r}(r)+rW_{\lambda,\infty}(r)\Phi(r)=0\quad\mathrm{for}\ r>0.$ (7.18) Therefore in light of Proposition 6.6 , we obtain for $r>0$ $\displaystyle(r^{4}u_{\lambda,\infty})_{r}$ $\displaystyle=(r^{4}\Phi)_{r}=r^{3}(r\Phi_{r}+4\Phi)=r^{3}\Phi(4-r^{2}W_{\lambda,\infty}).$ As a consequence, since it holds that $(r^{4}u_{\lambda,\infty})_{r}\leq 0$ for sufficiently large $r$ , the quantity $r^{4}u_{\lambda,\infty}$ is nonincreasing for sufficiently large $r$ . Thus the limit $\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{4}u_{\lambda,\infty}(r)$ exists. Here the relationship ( 7.13 ) as well as Proposition 6.6 implies that for $r>0$ $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r}\rho^{d-1}u_{\lambda,\infty}(\rho)d\rho$ $\displaystyle=r^{d}M_{\lambda,\infty}\geq r^{d}M_{\lambda,0}=\lambda\int_{0}^{r}\rho^{d-1}e^{\phi}d\rho.$ Since $e^{\phi}$ exhibits the asymptotic behavior $e^{\phi}=8(d-4)(d-2)r^{-4}+o(1)$ as $r\to\infty$ , the right-hand side diverges to $\infty$ as $r\to\infty$ . Therefore we may apply L’Hospital’s rule to obtain $\displaystyle\liminf_{r\to\infty}r^{4}M_{\lambda,\infty}(r)$ $\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to\infty}\dfrac{\int_{0}^{r}\rho^{d-1}u_{\lambda,\infty}(\rho)d\rho}{r^{d-4}}$ $\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{d-4}r^{4}u_{\lambda,\infty}(r),$ where the last equality follows by the existence of the limit $\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{4}u_{\lambda,\infty}(r)$ .
Furthermore, combining this identity with ( 7.16 ), we deduce that $\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{4}u_{\lambda,\infty}(r)>8(d-4)(d-2).$ (7.19) The definition of $(u_{\lambda,\infty},v_{\lambda,\infty},w_{\lambda,\infty})$ established by Proposition 6.6 and the equality ( 7.18 ) yield that $\displaystyle(u_{\lambda,\infty})_{r}-u_{\lambda,\infty}(v_{\lambda,\infty})_{r}=(r(M_{\lambda,\infty})_{r}+dM_{\lambda,\infty})_{r}-(r(M_{\lambda,\infty})_{r}+dM_{\lambda,\infty})(-rW_{\lambda,\infty})=0.$ Here $u_{\lambda,\infty}$ cannot be identically zero for $r>0$ because of the asymptotic profile ( 7.19 ). This fact provides that $u_{\lambda,\infty}$ is strictly positive for all $r>0$ in the same way as in [ 43 , Lemma 3.1] . Consequently, the identity, together with the positivity of $u_{\lambda,\infty}$ , implies that $\log u_{\lambda,\infty}=v_{\lambda,\infty}+C$ for all $r>0$ and some constant $C$ . Condition ( 7.19 ) corresponds to the case in which the asymptotic behavior ( 7.3 ) in Proposition 7.1 fails to hold.
Therefore, by considering the contrapositive of Proposition 7.1 , we conclude that $\displaystyle\liminf_{r\to\infty}(v_{\lambda,\infty}(r)+4\log r)=-\infty.$ (7.20) Nevertheless, by recalling that for $r>0$ $\displaystyle r^{4}u_{\lambda,\infty}(r)=Cr^{4}e^{v_{\lambda,\infty}(r)}=Ce^{v_{\lambda,\infty}(r)+4\log r},$ the asymptotic behavior ( 7.20 ) for $v_{\lambda,\infty}$ enforces $\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{4}u_{\lambda,\infty}(r)=0$ , which is incompatible with ( 7.19 ). Hence $T_{\lambda}$ must be finite. This necessarily forces the solution $(u,v,w)$ blows up at some time earlier than $T_{\lambda}$ . (iii) Since $\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>1$ , the proof of (ii) extends directly, by Proposition 6.3 .
∎ 8 Proof of Theorem 1.8 and Theorem 1.10 In this section, motivated by the results obtained in [ 2 , 4 ] , we demonstrate that the qualitative behavior of solutions to the system ( P ) changes depending on the spatial dimension. Our approach is to focus first on the radial solution of the fourth-order elliptic problem ( 1.8 ) and to show that the Morrey norms for $(e^{\phi},(-\Delta)\phi)$ differ significantly with the dimension. Lemma 8.1 . Let $d\geq 13$ and $\phi\in C^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ be a radially symmetric solution of ( 1.8 ) with initial conditions $\displaystyle\phi(0)=\log\alpha,\quad\Delta\phi(0)=\beta_{0}(\alpha),\quad\phi^{\prime}(0)=(\Delta\phi)^{\prime}(0)=0,$ where $\alpha>0$ and $\beta_{0}(\alpha)\in[-4d\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}},0)$ . Then it holds that $\displaystyle\sup_{R>0}R^{4-d}\int_{0}^{R}r^{d-1}e^{\phi(r)}dr=8(d-2)$ (8.1) as well as $\displaystyle\sup_{R>0}R^{2-d}\int_{0}^{R}r^{d-1}(-\Delta)\phi(r)dr=4.$ (8.2) Proof.. According to [ 4 , Lemma 12] , when $d\geq 13$ , the regular radial solution $\phi$ of ( 1.8 ) with the prescribed conditions at the origin and a singular radial solution $-4\log r+\log 8(d-4)(d-2)$ of ( 1.8 ) do not intersect each other for $r>0$ , namely we have $\displaystyle\phi(r)<\log\dfrac{8(d-4)(d-2)}{r^{4}}\quad\mathrm{for}\ r\in(0,\infty).$ (8.3) This behavior implies that for $R>0$ $\displaystyle R^{4-d}\int_{0}^{R}r^{d-1}e^{\phi(r)}dr$ $\displaystyle<R^{4-d}\int_{0}^{R}r^{d-1}\dfrac{8(d-4)(d-2)}{r^{4}}dr=8(d-2).$ (8.4) On the other hand, the asymptotic behavior of $\phi$ in light of [ 2 , Theorem 2] and [ 4 , Theorem 4] : $\displaystyle\phi(r)=-4\log r+\log 8(d-4)(d-2)+o(1)\quad\mathrm{as}\ r\to\infty$ immediately implies that $\int_{0}^{R}r^{d-1}e^{\phi(r)}dr\to\infty$ as $R\to\infty$ , allowing the use of L’Hospital’s rule.
Consequently we get $\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}R^{4-d}\int_{0}^{R}r^{d-1}e^{\phi(r)}dr$ $\displaystyle=\lim_{R\to\infty}\dfrac{R^{d-1}e^{\phi(R)}}{(d-4)R^{d-5}}=8(d-2),$ which lead to our desired conclusion ( 8.1 ) together with ( 8.4 ). We next confirm ( 8.2 ). Since the solution $\phi$ solves the fourth-order equation $\Delta^{2}\phi=e^{\phi}$ on $[0,\infty)$ , the result in [ 21 , Theorem 1.1] leads to the following estimate: $\displaystyle-\Delta\phi(r)<\dfrac{4(d-2)}{r^{2}}\quad\mathrm{for\ all}\ r\in(0,\infty).$ Hence we obtain for any $R>0$ $\displaystyle R^{2-d}\int_{0}^{R}r^{d-1}(-\Delta)\phi(r)dr<4(d-2)R^{2-d}\int_{0}^{R}r^{d-3}dr=4.$ Moreover as noted in the proof (see ( 6.5 )) in Proposition 6.1 , the detailed asymptotic expansion of $\phi$ is available thanks to the results in [ 21 ] . This allows us to apply L’Hospital’s rule to deduce $\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}R^{2-d}\int_{0}^{R}r^{d-1}(-\Delta)\phi(r)dr=\lim_{R\to\infty}\dfrac{R^{d-1}(-\Delta)\phi(R)}{(d-2)R^{d-3}}=4,$ which clearly proves ( 8.2 ).
∎ Lemma 8.2 . Let $5\leq d\leq 12$ and let $\phi\in C^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})$ be a radially symmetric solution of ( 1.8 ) with the conditions at the origin $\displaystyle\phi(0)=\log\alpha,\quad\Delta\phi(0)=\beta_{0}(\alpha),\quad\phi^{\prime}(0)=(\Delta\phi)^{\prime}(0)=0,$ where $\alpha>0$ and $\beta_{0}(\alpha)\in[-4d\alpha^{\frac{1}{2}},0)$ . Then there exist $K_{0}>8(d-2)$ and $K_{1}>4$ such that $\displaystyle\sup_{R>0}R^{4-d}\int_{0}^{R}r^{d-1}e^{\phi(r)}dr=K_{0}$ (8.5) and $\displaystyle\sup_{R>0}R^{2-d}\int_{0}^{R}r^{d-1}(-\Delta)\phi(r)dr=K_{1}.$ (8.6) Proof.. As shown in [ 21 , Theorem 1.1] , it turns out that when $5\leq d\leq 12$ , the regular radial solution $\phi$ of ( 1.8 ) subject to the conditions at the origin oscillates around the singular profile $-4\log r+\log 8(d-4)(d-2)$ , and in particular, the two solutions intersect infinitely many times. Moreover thanks to [ 21 , Theorem 3.1] the regular radial solution $\phi$ satisfies the following oscillatory asymptotic expansion as $r\to\infty$ $\displaystyle\phi(r)=-4\log r+\log 8(d-4)(d-2)+C_{0}r^{\tau}\sin(k_{0}\log r+k_{1})+C_{1}r^{\nu}+O(r^{2\tau}),$ (8.7) where $\tau=-\frac{d-4}{2}$ and $\nu<2-d$ , and for some constants $k_{0},k_{1},C_{0},C_{1}$ with $C_{0}\not=0$ .
Our goal is to show that $R^{4-d}\int_{0}^{R}r^{d-1}e^{\phi(r)}dr-8(d-2)$ changes sign infinitely many times as $R\to\infty$ .
Using the asymptotic profile ( 8.7 ), we write $\displaystyle e^{\phi(r)}=\dfrac{8(d-4)(d-2)}{r^{4}}e^{\varepsilon(r)}\quad\mathrm{as}\ r\to\infty,$ where $\varepsilon(r)=C_{0}r^{\tau}\sin(k_{0}\log r+k_{1})+C_{1}r^{\nu}+O(r^{2\tau})$ . Since $\varepsilon\to 0$ as $r\to\infty$ , we may expand $e^{\varepsilon(r)}$ by Taylor’s formula to obtain $\displaystyle e^{\varepsilon(r)}=1+\varepsilon(r)+O(\varepsilon(r)^{2})\quad\mathrm{as}\ r\to\infty.$ Noticing that $\varepsilon(r)=O(r^{\tau})$ due to ( 8.7 ), we deduce $\displaystyle e^{\phi(r)}=\dfrac{8(d-4)(d-2)}{r^{4}}(1+\varepsilon(r)+O(r^{2\tau}))\quad\mathrm{as}\ r\to\infty.$ (8.8) Let $R_{0}>0$ be sufficiently large so that ( 8.7 ) holds for all $r\geq R_{0}$ . Then we have $\displaystyle R^{4-d}\int_{0}^{R}r^{d-1}e^{\phi(r)}dr=R^{4-d}\int_{0}^{R_{0}}r^{d-1}e^{\phi(r)}dr+R^{4-d}\int_{R_{0}}^{R}r^{d-1}e^{\phi(r)}dr.$ (8.9) Since $\phi$ is regular near the origin, the first term contributes only a bounded quantity.
More precisely, there exists a constant $K_{0}>0$ such that $\displaystyle R^{4-d}\int_{0}^{R_{0}}r^{d-1}e^{\phi(r)}dr\leq K_{0}R^{4-d}.$ (8.10) Therefore, our task is reduced to understanding the asymptotics of the second term in ( 8.9 ). Combing ( 8.8 ) with the second term on ( 8.9 ) yields that for $R\geq R_{0}$ $\displaystyle R^{4-d}\int_{R_{0}}^{R}r^{d-1}e^{\phi(r)}dr$ $\displaystyle=8(d-4)(d-2)R^{4-d}\int_{R_{0}}^{R}r^{d-5}dr$ $\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+8(d-4)(d-2)R^{4-d}\int_{R_{0}}^{R}r^{d-5}\varepsilon(r)dr$ $\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+8(d-4)(d-2)R^{4-d}\int_{R_{0}}^{R}r^{d-5}O(r^{2\tau})dr$ $\displaystyle=:I+II+III.$ The term $I$ can be computed explicitly.
Indeed, we have $\displaystyle I=8(d-2)-8(d-2)R_{0}^{d-4}R^{4-d},$ (8.11) which implies that $I=8(d-2)+O(R^{4-d})$ as $R\to\infty$ . With regard to the term $III$ , since $d-5+2\tau=-1$ , we can see that there exists $C>0$ such that $\displaystyle III\leq CR^{4-d}\log R+CR^{4-d}\log R_{0}.$ (8.12) This clearly implies that $III=O(R^{\tau})$ as $R\to\infty$ .
As for the term $II$ , we expand it as $\displaystyle II$ $\displaystyle=8(d-4)(d-2)C_{0}R^{4-d}\int_{R_{0}}^{R}r^{d-5+\tau}\sin(k_{0}\log r+k_{1})dr$ $\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+8(d-4)(d-2)C_{1}R^{4-d}\int_{R_{0}}^{R}r^{d-5+\nu}dr$ $\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+8(d-4)(d-2)R^{4-d}\int_{R_{0}}^{R}r^{d-5}O(r^{2\tau})dr.$ Among these contributions, only the first term requires careful attention.
Indeed, the remaining two parts are of order $O(R^{4-d})+O(R^{\nu})+O(R^{\tau})$ , so we may safely disregard them when analyzing the asymptotic behavior. Hereafter, every constant appearing in the computations will be denoted by the same symbol $C$ . We proceed to observe that $\displaystyle 8(d-4)(d-2)C_{0}R^{4-d}\int_{R_{0}}^{R}r^{d-5+\tau}\sin(k_{0}\log r+k_{1})dr$ $\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=CR^{4-d}\int_{R_{0}}^{R}r^{d-5+\tau}\sin(k_{0}\log r+k_{1})dr$ $\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=CR^{4-d}\int_{\log R_{0}}^{\log R}e^{(d-5+\tau)s}\sin(k_{0}s+k_{1})e^{s}ds$ $\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=CR^{4-d}\int_{\log R_{0}}^{\log R}e^{(d-4+\tau)s}\sin(k_{0}s+k_{1})ds.$ (8.13) By a standard computation, one can check that $\displaystyle\int_{\log R_{0}}^{\log R}e^{(d-4+\tau)s}\sin(k_{0}s+k_{1})ds$ $\displaystyle=CR^{d-4+\tau}\sin(k_{0}\log R+k_{1})$ $\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+CR^{d-4+\tau}\cos(k_{0}\log R+k_{1})+C.$ Hence using the trigonometric addition formulas, we obtain $\displaystyle\int_{\log R_{0}}^{\log R}e^{(d-4+\tau)s}\sin(k_{0}s+k_{1})ds$ $\displaystyle=CR^{d-4+\tau}\sin(k_{0}\log R+\theta)+C$ for some phase shift $\theta$ . Combining ( 8.13 ) with the identity above yields $\displaystyle CR^{4-d}\int_{R_{0}}^{R}r^{d-5+\tau}\sin(k_{0}\log r+k_{1})dr=CR^{\tau}\sin(k_{0}\log R+\theta)+CR^{4-d}.$ (8.14) Substituting ( 8.10 ), ( 8.11 ), ( 8.12 ), and ( 8.14 ) into ( 8.9 ), we arrive at $\displaystyle R^{4-d}\int_{0}^{R}r^{d-1}e^{\phi(r)}dr-8(d-2)=CR^{\tau}\sin(k_{0}\log R+\theta)+O(R^{\tau})\quad\mathrm{as}\ R\to\infty$ for some $\theta$ . Since $\tau<0$ , the above asymptotic expansion immediately implies that $\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}R^{4-d}\int_{0}^{R}r^{d-1}e^{\phi(r)}dr=8(d-2).$ Nevertheless, the oscillatory term $\sin(k_{0}\log R+\theta)$ changes sign infinitely many times as $R\to\infty$ . Thus we obtain the desired conclusion ( 8.5 ). Similarly, differentiating the asymptotic expansion of $\phi$ shows that $\displaystyle-\Delta\phi(r)=4(d-2)r^{-2}+\tilde{C_{0}}r^{\tau-2}\sin(k_{0}\log r+\tilde{\theta})+O(r^{\nu-2})+O(r^{2\tau-2})\quad\mathrm{as}\ r\to\infty$ for some constant $\tilde{C_{0}}\not=0$ and some phase shift $\tilde{\theta}$ . Arguing in exactly the same way as above, but now with $-\Delta\phi$ in place of $e^{\phi}$ , we conclude that $R^{2-d}\int_{0}^{R}r^{d-1}(-\Delta)\phi(r)dr$ oscillates infinitely many times around its limit $4$ . This completes the proof.
