Introduction We consider the system, (1.1a) $\displaystyle\partial_{t}h+\Div(hu)$ $\displaystyle=0\quad$ $\displaystyle\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{n},$ (1.1b) $\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla h-\nabla\Delta h$ $\displaystyle=0\quad$ $\displaystyle\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{n},$ (1.1c) $\displaystyle(h,u)|_{t=0}$ $\displaystyle=(h_{0},u_{0}),$ in dimensions $n=1,2$ . We can view ( 1.1 ) as a model for shallow water equations with surface tension. Here $h>0$ denotes the height, $u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ denotes the velocity of the water wave, the pressure term $\nabla h$ denotes the effect of gravity, and the third order term $-\nabla\Delta h$ arises from surface tension. Physically, ( 1.1a ) and ( 1.1b ) stand for the conservation of mass and momentum, respectively. The shallow water equations ( 1.1 ) were formally derived from the free boundary problem for the incompressible water wave equations by the first author and his collaborators in [ 29 ] , and were also previously known in the review article [ 13 ] and the monograph [ 15 ] . As a shallow water model, the validity of ( 1.1 ) was justified by Bresch and Noble in [ 14 ] in some scales. Remark 1.1 . For $n\geq 3$ , one should interpret ( 1.1 ) as a special case of the Euler–Korteweg system [ 10 , 7 , 11 ] , which models a fluid with quantum effects. See Section 7 for our remark about the irrotation flows in $n$ dimension. 1.1. Main result Our goal is to prove local well-posedness for ( 1.1 ). We note that the cases of $n=1$ and $n=2$ admit fundamentally different structures. Therefore, we will treat them separately. In the case of $n=1$ , system ( 1.1 ) is reduced to the following: (1.2a) $\displaystyle\partial_{t}h+\partial_{x}(hu)$ $\displaystyle=0\quad$ $\displaystyle\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R},$ (1.2b) $\displaystyle\partial_{t}u+u\partial_{x}u+\partial_{x}h-\partial_{x}^{3}h$ $\displaystyle=0\quad$ $\displaystyle\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R},$ (1.2c) $\displaystyle(h,u)|_{t=0}$ $\displaystyle=(h_{0},u_{0}).$ Theorem 1.2 . Consider initial data $(h_{0},u_{0})$ for system ( 1.2 ), with (1.3) $0<\underline{h}:=\inf_{x}h_{0}(x)<1<\overline{h}:=\sup_{x}h_{0}(x)<\infty,$ and (1.4) $h_{0}-1\in H^{3}(\mathbb{R}),\quad u_{0}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R}).$ Then there exists $T\in(0,\infty)$ , depending only on the initial data, such that there exists a unique solution $(h-1,u)\in L^{\infty}([0,T];H^{3}(\mathbb{R}))\times L^{\infty}([0,T];H^{2}(\mathbb{R}))$ to system ( 1.2 ), with (1.5) $\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sum_{0\leq j\leq 2}\|\partial_{x}^{j}(h(t)-1),\partial_{x}^{j}u(t),\partial_{x}^{j+1}h(t)\|_{L^{2}}<C<\infty,$ for some constant $C\in(0,\infty)$ depending only on the initial data. Meanwhile, in the case of $n=2$ , we will show Theorem 1.3 . Consider initial data $(h_{0},u_{0})$ for system ( 1.1 ), with (1.6) $0<\underline{h}:=\inf_{x}h_{0}(x)<1<\overline{h}:=\sup_{x}h_{0}(x)<\infty,$ and with regularity (1.7) $h_{0}-1\in H^{7}(\mathbb{R}),\quad u_{0}\in H^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).$ Then there exists $T\in(0,\infty)$ depending only initial data, such that there exists a unique solution $(h-1,u)\in L^{\infty}([0,T];H^{4}(\mathbb{R}))\times L^{\infty}([0,T];H^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))$ to system ( 1.1 ), with (1.8) $\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\sum_{0\leq j\leq 3}\|\nabla^{j}(h(t)-1),\nabla^{j}u(t),\nabla^{j+1}h(t)\|_{L^{2}}<C<\infty,$ for some constant $C\in(0,\infty)$ depending only on the initial data. To prove Theorems 1.2 and 1.3 , we will make use of the symmetric structure of system ( 1.1 ), which can be seen via the momentum variable $m:=hu.$ Indeed, one can write down the following equivalent system of the shallow water equations using $(h,m)$ : (1.9a) $\displaystyle\partial_{t}h+\Div m$ $\displaystyle=0,$ (1.9b) $\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\partial_{t}m+\frac{m}{h^{2}}\cdot\nabla m+\frac{m}{h^{2}}\Div m+\nabla h-\nabla\Delta h$ $\displaystyle=\frac{(m\cdot\nabla)h}{h^{3}}m,$ (1.9c) $\displaystyle\partial_{t}\nabla h+\nabla\Div m$ $\displaystyle=0.$ To explain the idea, in the case of $n=1$ , i.e., in one spatial dimension, let $U:=(h-1,m,\partial_{x}h)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$ . One can write system ( 1.2 ) (or equivalently, system ( 1.9 )) as (1.10a) $\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\frac{1}{h}&0\\
0&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}\partial_{t}U+\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&\frac{2m}{h^{2}}&0\\
0&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\partial_{x}U+\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&-1\\
0&1&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\partial_{x}^{2}U=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\frac{m^{2}}{h^{3}}\partial_{x}h\\
0\\
\end{pmatrix},$ (1.10b) $\displaystyle U(0,x)=(h_{0}-1,m_{0},\partial_{x}h_{0}).$ One can see that system ( 1.10 ) resembles a symmetric hyperbolic system, with additional second order terms. In particular, the associated linear operator generates a bounded group, and therefore there is no loss of regularity; see Lemma 2.2 , below. Due to this symmetry, the local well-posedness of the generalized system ( 2.5 ) follows easily from the method of vanishing viscosity. We refer to Section 3 for the complete argument. At last, in Section 4 , we show the well-posedness of system ( 1.10 ) (or equivalently, system ( 1.2 )) using an approximating argument. However, in dimension two, the symmetric structure of the shallow water system ( 1.1 ) differs from that in ( 1.10 ). Indeed, only the acoustic component of the flow admits a symmetric structure, while the vorticity component of the flow is transported. See Section 5 for the detailed formulation of $n=2$ , as well as the a priori estimates for the 2d system ( 5.12 ).
In Section 6 we explain why the approximating argument in Section 4 cannot be directly applied to the 2d case without additional regularity assumptions.
Finally, in Section 7 , we remark about a result for irrotational flows in dimensions $\geq 3$ . 1.2. Background and literature on inviscid water waves with gravity and surface tension For inviscid shallow water equations without surface tension, or for viscous shallow water equations with surface tension, we refer the reader to Bresch’s book [ 15 ] , Lannes’ works [ 27 , 28 ] and the review article by Bresch–Desjardins–Métivier [ 13 ] for an extensive overview on the mathematical properties of shallow water models in the absence/presence of boundary. In particular, Bresch and Noble in [ 14 ] rigorously justified the validity of ( 1.1 ) in smooth function spaces. Much more mathematical studies on inviscid water waves are available without the assumption of shallowness. Here we highlight the following works: Wu [ 35 , 36 , 33 , 34 ] , Coutand–Shkoller [ 19 ] , Alazard–Burq–Zuily [ 2 , 4 , 3 ] and Deng–Ionescu–Pausader–Pusateri [ 24 , 25 , 26 , 20 ] managed to show local and global well-posedness of incompressible irrotational water waves with or without surface tension using various methods, whereas Castro–Córdoba–Fefferman–Gancedo–Gómez-Serrano [ 17 , 16 ] investigated the finite-time formation of splash singularities. Finally, we note that Ambrose–Masmoudi [ 5 , 6 ] and Agrawal [ 1 ] investigated the zero surface tension limit problem. We remark here that Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 1.3 corresponds to the $K=1$ case for the Euler–Korteweg system in [ 10 , Theorem 1.1] . The proof in [ 10 ] relies on finding the good variable $w:=\frac{\nabla h}{\sqrt{h}},$ then complexifying the equations and performing rather involved weighted energy estimates. Moreover, the argument in [ 10 ] requires a vanishing viscosity argument using the hyperdissipative term $-\varepsilon\Delta^{2}$ , for which uniform-in- $\varepsilon$ estimates are more difficult to establish. We provide a shorter and more physically intuitive proof based on another good variable ( 5.16 ), which in turn gives more insights to the hidden symmetric structure of ( 1.1 ). In addition, we believe that our approach based on (almost) symmetric systems can be useful for further numerical studies of shallow water waves. See for instance [ 12 , 21 , 8 , 22 , 23 , 30 , 39 , 37 , 38 ] for numerical studies of shallow water models and of the Euler–Korteweg system. 1.3. To be more precise, let $J_{ij},I_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ , $1\leq i,j\leq n$ , be matrices defined by (7.1) $(J_{ij})_{lk}:=-\delta_{li}\delta_{kj}+\delta_{ki}\delta_{lj},\qquad(I_{ij})_{lk}:=\delta_{li}\delta_{kj}+\delta_{ki}\delta_{lj}.$ In analogy to ( 5.6 ), we focus on the term (7.2) $K:=\frac{m}{h^{2}}\cdot\nabla m+\frac{m}{h^{2}}\Div m=\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}\partial_{i}m,$ where (7.3) $(B_{i})_{lk}=\frac{m_{i}}{h^{2}}\delta_{lk}+\frac{m_{l}}{h^{2}}\delta_{ki}.$ Then one can write (7.4) $B_{i}=B_{\mathrm{i,sym}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij},$ where (7.5) $\displaystyle B_{\mathrm{i,sym}}$ $\displaystyle:=\frac{m_{i}}{h^{2}}\mathbb{I}_{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{m_{j}}{2h^{2}}I_{ij},$ $\displaystyle A_{ij}$ $\displaystyle:=\frac{m_{j}}{2h^{2}}J_{ij}.$ Indeed, $\displaystyle(I_{ij}+J_{ij})_{lk}$ $\displaystyle=2\delta_{ki}\delta_{lj},$ by definition, and therefore $\displaystyle(B_{\mathrm{i,sym}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij})_{lk}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{m_{i}}{h^{2}}\delta_{lk}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{m_{j}}{2h^{2}}(I_{ij}+J_{ij})_{lk}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{m_{i}}{h^{2}}\delta_{lk}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{m_{j}}{2h^{2}}2\delta_{ki}\delta_{lj}=\frac{m_{i}}{h^{2}}\delta_{lk}+\frac{m_{l}}{h^{2}}\delta_{ki}\overset{\eqref{def:B-i}}{=}(B_{i})_{lk}.$ Therefore, substituting ( 7.4 ) and ( 7.5 ) into ( 7.2 ) yields (7.6) $\displaystyle K$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{\mathrm{i,sym}}\partial_{i}m+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{m_{j}}{2h^{2}}J_{ij}\partial_{i}m$ $\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{\mathrm{i,sym}}\partial_{i}m+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{m_{j}}{2h^{2}}J_{ij}\underbrace{(\partial_{i}m-\nabla m_{i})}_{=(\nabla_{\mathrm{skw}}m)_{i}}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{m_{j}}{2h^{2}}J_{ij}\nabla m_{i},$ where (7.7) $J_{ij}\nabla\cdot m=-\partial_{j}m_{i}+\partial_{i}m_{j}=(\nabla_{\mathrm{skw}}m)_{ij},$ is the $ij$ -th component of the anti-symmetric gradient.
In particular, ( 7.7 ) implies the duality (7.8) $\int J_{ij}\nabla f\cdot g\,d\vec{x}=-\int f(\nabla_{\mathrm{skw}}g)_{ij}\,d\vec{x},$ in analogy to ( 5.26 ). Now we are ready to write the system ( 1.9 ) into symmetric form in higher dimensions. Let $U:=(h-1,m,\nabla h)=(h-1,m_{1},m_{2},\cdots,m_{n},\partial_{1}h,\partial_{2}h,\cdots,\partial_{n}h)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}$ . Write, for $i,j=1,2,\cdots,n$ , (7.9) $\displaystyle M_{0}$ $\displaystyle:=\diag(1,\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{I}_{n},\mathbb{I}_{n}),$ $\displaystyle M_{i}$ $\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}0&\vec{e}_{i}^{\top}&0\\
\vec{e}_{i}&B_{\mathrm{i,sym}}&0\\
0&0&0\times\mathbb{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix},$ $\displaystyle M_{ij}$ $\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&M_{ij}^{\prime\prime}\\
0&M_{ij}^{\prime}&0\end{pmatrix},$ with (7.10) $(M_{ij}^{\prime})_{lk}:=\delta_{li}\delta_{kj}\ \text{for}\ l,k=1,2,\cdots n,\quad\text{and}\quad M_{ij}^{\prime\prime}=-(M_{ij}^{\prime})^{\top},$ and (7.11) $S:=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\frac{(m\cdot\nabla)h}{h^{3}}m\\
0\end{pmatrix},\qquad S^{\prime\prime}:=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{m_{j}}{2h^{2}}J_{ij}(\nabla_{\mathrm{skw}}m)_{i}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{m_{j}}{2h^{2}}J_{ij}\nabla m_{i}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.$ It is easy to verify that $M_{i}$ , $i=0,1,2,\cdots n$ , are symmetric matrices and $M_{ij}$ , $i,j=1,2,\cdots n$ are constant anti-symmetric matrices. Then the system ( 1.9 ) can be written as (7.12) $\underbrace{M_{0}\partial_{t}U+\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{i}\partial_{i}U+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}M_{ij}\partial_{ij}U=S}_{\text{symmetric part}}\qquad+\underbrace{S^{\prime\prime}}_{\mathclap{\text{non-symmetric part}}}.$ While the symmetric part can be handled as in the one and two dimensional cases, the non-symmetric part requires the estimates of (7.13) $\nabla_{\mathrm{skw}}m=h{\nabla_{\mathrm{skw}}u}+u(\nabla h)^{\top}-\nabla hu^{\top}.$ Meanwhile, from ( 1.1b ), one can calculate, for $\forall\ l,k=1,2,\cdots,n$ , $\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\partial_{k}u_{l}-\partial_{l}u_{k})=-\partial_{k}(u\cdot\nabla u_{l})+\partial_{l}(u\cdot\nabla u_{k})$ $\displaystyle=-u\cdot\nabla(\partial_{k}u_{l}-\partial_{l}u_{k})-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\partial_{k}u_{j}\partial_{j}u_{l}-\partial_{l}u_{j}\partial_{j}u_{k})$ $\displaystyle=-u\cdot\nabla(\partial_{k}u_{l}-\partial_{l}u_{k})-\sum_{j=1}^{n}[(\partial_{k}u_{j}-\partial_{j}u_{k})\partial_{j}u_{l}+(\partial_{j}u_{l}-\partial_{l}u_{j})\partial_{j}u_{k}].$ That is, (7.14) $\partial_{t}\nabla_{\mathrm{skw}}u+u\cdot\nabla\nabla_{\mathrm{skw}}u=-\nabla_{\mathrm{skw}}u(\nabla u)^{\top}-\nabla u\nabla_{\mathrm{skw}}u.$ In particular, if $\nabla_{\mathrm{skw}}u(t=0)=0$ , one can conclude from ( 7.14 ) that, for all $\forall\ t\geq 0$ , (7.15) $\nabla_{\mathrm{skw}}u(t)\equiv 0.$ Thus, from ( 7.13 ) and ( 7.15 ), one can conclude that (7.16) $\nabla_{\mathrm{skw}}m=u(\nabla h)^{\top}-\nabla hu^{\top}\simeq U^{2},$ where no loss of regularity appears.
Thus, repeating the arguments as in Section 5 leads to the following: Proposition 7.1 . For $n\geq 3$ , let (7.17) $\frac{n}{2}+1<s_{n}:=\begin{cases}\frac{n}{2}+\frac{3}{2}&\text{if $n$ is odd},\\
\frac{n}{2}+2&\text{if $n$ is even}.\end{cases}$ Consider initial data $(h_{0}-1,u_{0})\in H^{2s_{n}+1}(\mathbb{R})\times H^{2s_{n}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$ for the system ( 1.1 ) with $\nabla_{\mathrm{skw}}u_{0}=0$ and ( 1.3 ). Then there exists some time $T\in(0,\infty)$ such that there exists a unique local-in-time solution $(h-1,m)\in L^{\infty}([0,T];H^{s_{n}+1}(\mathbb{R}))\times L^{\infty}([0,T];H^{s_{n}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))$ to ( 1.1 ).
