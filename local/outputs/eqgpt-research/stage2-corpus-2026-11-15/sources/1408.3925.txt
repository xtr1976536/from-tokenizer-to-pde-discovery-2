Email: monneau@cermics.enpc.fr Abstract In this paper, we study degenerate parabolic system, which is strongly coupled. We prove general existence result, but the uniqueness remains an open question. Our proof of existence is based on a crucial entropy estimate which both control the gradient of the solution and the non-negativity of the solution. Our system are of porous medium type and our method applies to models in seawater intrusion. 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification: 35K55, 35K65 Keywords: Degenerate parabolic system; entropy estimate; porous medium like systems. 1 Introduction For the sake of simplicity, we will work on the torus $\Omega:=\mathbb{T}^{N}=\left(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\right)^{N}$ , with $N\geq 1$ . Let $\Omega_{T}:=(0,T)\times\Omega$ with $T>0$ . Let an integer $m\geq 1$ . Our purpose is to study a class of degenerate strongly coupled parabolic system of the form $u_{t}^{i}={\rm div}\left(u^{i}\overset{m}{\underset{j=1}{\sum}}A_{ij}\nabla u^{j}\right)\quad\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega_{T},\quad\mbox{ for }i=1,\ldots,m.$ (1.1) with the initial condition $u^{i}(0,x)=u^{i}_{0}(x)\geq 0\quad\quad\mbox{ a.e. in }\Omega,\quad\mbox{ for }i=1,\ldots,m.$ (1.2) In the core of the paper we will assume that $A=(A_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}$ is a real $m\times m$ matrix (not necessarily symmetric) that satisfies the following positivity condition: we assume that there exists $\delta_{0}>0$ , such that we have $\xi^{T}A\,\xi\geq\delta_{0}|\xi|^{2},\quad\mbox{ for all }\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.$ (1.3) This condition can be weaken: see Subsection 4.1 .
Problem ( 1.1 ) appears naturally in the modeling of seawater intrusion (see Subsection 1.2). 1.1 Main results To introduce our main result, we need to define the nonnegative entropy function $\Psi$ : $\displaystyle\Psi(a)-\frac{1}{e}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}a\ln a&\mbox{ for}\quad a>0,\\
0&\mbox{ for}\quad a=0,\\
+\infty&\mbox{ for}\quad a<0,\end{array}\right.$ which is minimal for $\displaystyle a=\frac{1}{e}$ . Theorem 1.1 . (Existence for system ( 1.1 )) Assume that $A$ satisfies ( 1.3 ). For $i=1,\ldots,m$ , let $u_{0}^{i}\geq 0$ in $\Omega$ satisfying $\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(u_{0}^{i})<+\infty,$ (1.8) where $\Psi$ is given in ( 1.1 ). Then there exists a function $u=(u^{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}\in(L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\ \cap\ C([0,T);(W^{1,\infty}(\Omega))^{\prime}))^{m}$ solution in the sense of distributions of ( 1.1 ),( 1.2 ), with $u^{i}\geq 0$ a.e. in $\Omega_{T}$ , for $i=1,\ldots,m$ . Moreover this solution satisfies the following entropy estimate for a.e. $t_{1},t_{2}\in(0,T)$ , with $u^{i}(t_{2})=u^{i}(t_{2},\cdot)$ : $\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(u^{i}(t_{2}))+\delta_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u^{i}|^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(u_{0}^{i}),$ (1.9) where $\Psi$ is given in ( 1.1 ). Here $\left\|A\right\|$ is the matrix norm defined as $\left\|A\right\|=\sup_{\left|\xi\right|=1}\left|A\xi\right|.$ (1.10) Notice that the entropy estimate ( 1.9 ) guarantees that $\nabla u^{i}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))$ , and therefore allows us to define the product $\displaystyle u^{i}\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{ij}\nabla u^{j}$ in ( 1.1 ). When our proofs were obtained, we realized that a similar entropy estimate has been obtained in [ 7 ] and [ 9 ] for a special system different from ours. Remark 1.2 . ( Decreasing energy ) If $A$ is a symmetric matrix then a solution $u$ of system ( 1.1 ) satisfies $\frac{d}{dt}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}A_{ij}u^{i}u^{j}\right)=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\Omega}u^{i}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{ij}\nabla u^{j}\right|^{2}.$ 1.2 Application to seawater intrusion In this subsection, we describe briefly a model of seawater intrusion, which is particular case of our system ( 1.1 ). An aquifer is an underground layer of a porous and permeable rock through which water can move. On the one hand coastal aquifers contain freshwater and on the other hand saltwater from the sea can enter in the ground and replace the freshwater. We refer to [ 4 ] for a general overview on seawater intrusion models. Now let $\nu=1-\epsilon_{0}\in(0,1)$ where $\epsilon_{0}=\frac{\gamma_{s}-\gamma_{f}}{\gamma_{s}}$ with $\gamma_{s}$ and $\gamma_{f}$ are the specific weight of the saltwater and freshwater respectively. Figure 1: Seawater intrusion in coastal aquifer We assume that in the porous medium, the interface between the saltwater and the bedrock is given as $\{z=0\}$ , the interface between the saltwater and the freshwater, which are assumed to be unmiscible, can be written as $\left\{z=g(t,x)\right\}$ , and the interface between the freshwater and the dry soil can be written as $\left\{z=h(t,x)+g(t,x)\right\}$ . Then the evolutions of $h$ and $g$ are given by a coupled nonlinear parabolic system (we refer to see [ 17 ] ) of the form $\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}h_{t}&=&{\rm div}\left\{h\nabla(\nu(h+g))\right\}&\mbox{ in }\Omega_{T},\\
g_{t}&=&{\rm div}\left\{g\nabla(\nu h+g)\right\}&\mbox{ in }\Omega_{T},\\
\end{array}\right.$ This is a particular case of ( 1.1 ), where the $2\times 2$ matrix $A=\begin{pmatrix}\nu&\nu\\
\nu&1\end{pmatrix}$ (1.14) satisfies ( 1.3 ). 1.3 Brief review of the litterature The cross-diffusion systems, in particular the strongly coupled ones (for which the equations are coupled in the highest derivatives terms), are widely presented in different domains such as biology, chemistry, ecology, fluid mechanics and others. They are difficult to treat. Many of the standard results cannot be applied for such problems, such as the maximum principle. Hereafter, we cite several models where our method applies for most of them (see Section 4 for more generalizations on our problem). In [ 28 ] , Shigesada, Kawasaki and Teramoto proposed a two-species SKT model in one-dimensional space which arises in population dynamics. It can be written in a generalized form with m-species as $\displaystyle u_{t}^{i}-\Delta\left[\left(\beta_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{ij}u^{j}\right)u^{i}\right]=\displaystyle\left(a_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{ij}u^{j}\right)u^{i},\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),$ (1.15) where $u^{i}$ , for $i=1,\ldots,m$ , denotes the population density of the i-th species and $\beta_{i}$ , $\alpha_{ij}$ , $a_{i}$ , $b_{ij}$ are nonnegative constants. The existence of a global solution for such problem in arbitrary space dimension is studied in [ 32 ] , where the quadratic form of the diffusion matrix is supposed positive definite. On the other hand, the two-species case was frequently studied, see for instance [ 23 , 16 , 31 , 14 , 29 ] for dimensions $1$ , $2$ , and [ 7 , 26 , 27 , 6 ] for arbitrary dimension and appropriate conditions. Another example of such problems is the electochemistry model studied by Choi, Huan and Lui in [ 8 ] where they consider the general form $u^{i}_{t}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\ell}}\left(a^{ij}_{\ell}(u)\frac{\partial u^{j}}{\partial x_{\ell}}\right),\quad u=(u^{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}\quad\mbox{for}\quad i=1,\ldots,m,$ (1.16) and prove the existence of a weak solution of ( 1.16 ) under assumptions on the matrices $A_{l}(u)=(a_{l}^{ij}(u))_{1\leq i,j\leq m}$ : it is continuous in $u$ , its components are uniformly bounded with respect to $u$ and its symmetric part is definite positive. Their strategy of proof seeks to use Galerkin method to prove the existence of solutions to the linearized system and then to apply Schauder fixed-point theorem. Then they apply the results obtained to an electrochemistry model. A third example of cross-diffusion models is the chemotaxis model introduced in [ 20 ] . The global existence for classical solutions of this model is studied by Hillen and Painter in [ 15 ] where they considered $\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{clll}u_{t}&=&\nabla\cdot(\nabla u-\chi(u,v)\nabla v),&\quad t>0,x\in\Omega\\
v_{t}&=&\mu\Delta v+g(u,v),&\quad t>0,x\in\Omega,\end{array}\right.$ on a $C^{3}$ - differentiable compact Riemannian manifold without boundary, where the function $u$ describes the particle density, $v$ is the density of the external signal, the chemotactic cross-diffusion $\chi$ is assumed to be bounded, and the function $g$ describes production and degradation of the external stimulus. Another kind of chemotaxis model (the angiogenesis system) has been suggested and studied in [ 9 ] : $\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{clll}u_{t}&=&\kappa\Delta u-\nabla\cdot(u\,\chi(v)\nabla v),&\quad t>0,x\in\Omega\\
v_{t}&=&-v^{m}u,&\quad t>0,x\in\Omega,\end{array}\right.$ where $m\geq 1$ and $\kappa$ is a constant. Moreover, Alt and Luckhaus prove the existence in finite time of a solution for the following elliptic-parabolic problem $\partial_{t}b^{i}(u)-\hbox{div}(a^{i}(b(u),\nabla u))=f^{i}(b(u)),\quad\mbox{ in }\Omega\times(0,T),$ (1.19) where $\Omega\subset R^{N}$ is open, bounded, and connected with Lipschitz boundary, $b$ is monotone and continuous gradient and $a$ is continuous and elliptic with some growth condition. This problem can be seen as a standard parabolic equation when $b(z)=z$ . Another problem is the Muskat Problem for Thin Fluid Layers of the form $\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\partial_{t}f&=&(1+R)\partial_{x}(f\partial_{x}f)+R\partial_{x}(f\partial_{x}g),\\
\partial_{t}g&=&R_{\mu}\partial_{x}(g\partial_{x}f)+R_{\mu}\partial_{x}(g\partial_{x}g).\\
\end{array}\right.$ It models, [ 11 ] , the motion of two fluids with different densities and viscosities in a porous meduim in one dimension, where $f$ and $g$ are the thickness of the two fluids and $R$ , $R_{\mu}>0$ depending on the densities and the viscosities of the fluids. The authors in [ 11 , 12 ] studied the classical solutions of such problem. Moreover, weak solutions are established under different assumptions in [ 10 , 24 , 18 , 19 ] . 1.4 Strategy of the proof In ( 1.1 ), the elliptic part of the equation does not have a Lax-Milgram structure. Otherwise, our existence result is mainly based on the entropy estimate ( 1.9 ). It is difficult to get this entropy estimate directly (we do not have enough regularity to do it), so we proceed by approximations. Approximation 1: We discretize in time system ( 1.1 ), with a time step $\Delta t=T/K$ , where $K\in\mathbb{N}^{*}$ . Then for a given $u^{n}=(u^{i,n})_{1\leq i\leq m}\in(H^{1}(\Omega))^{m}$ , we consider the implicit scheme which is an elliptic system: $\displaystyle{\frac{u^{i,n+1}-u^{i,n}}{\Delta t}}={\rm div}\left\{u^{i,n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{ij}\nabla u^{j,n+1}\right\}.$ (1.21) Approximation 2: We regularize the right-hand term of ( 1.21 ).
To do that, we take $\eta>0$ and $0<\epsilon<1<\ell$ , and we choose the following regularization $\displaystyle{\frac{u^{i,n+1}-u^{i,n}}{\Delta t}}={\rm div}\left\{T^{\epsilon,\ell}(u^{i,n+1})\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{ij}\nabla\rho_{\eta}\star\rho_{\eta}\star u^{j,n+1}\right\},$ (1.22) where $T^{\epsilon,\ell}$ is truncation operator defined as $\displaystyle T^{\epsilon,\ell}(a):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}\epsilon&\mbox{ if }a\leq\epsilon,\\
a&\mbox{ if }\epsilon\leq a\leq\ell,\\
\ell&\mbox{ if }a\geq\ell,\end{array}\right.$ and the mollifier $\rho_{\eta}(x)=\eta^{-N}\rho\left(x/\eta\right)$ with $\rho\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N})$ , $\rho\geq 0$ , $\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\rho=1$ and $\rho(-x)=\rho(x)$ . Now, with the convolution by $\rho_{\eta}$ in ( 1.22 ), the term $\nabla\rho_{\eta}\star\rho_{\eta}\star u^{j,n+1}$ behaves like $u^{j,n+1}$ . Note that, considering the $\mathbb{Z}^{N}$ - periodic extension on $\mathbb{R}^{N}$ of $u^{j,n+1}$ , the convolution $\rho_{\eta}\star u^{j,n+1}$ is possible over $\mathbb{R}^{N}$ . Approximation 3: Let $\delta>0$ . We will add a second order term like $\delta\Delta u^{i}$ to equation ( 1.22 ) in order to obtain an elliptic one. More specifically, we consider ${\rm div}\left(\delta T^{\epsilon,\ell}(u^{i})\nabla u^{i}\right)$ instead of $\delta\Delta u^{i}$ , to keep an entropy estimate. Then we freeze the coefficients $u^{i,n+1}$ on the right-hand side to make a linear structure (these coefficients are now called $\delta T^{\epsilon,\ell}(v^{i,n+1})$ ), we obtain the following modified system: $\displaystyle{\frac{u^{i,n+1}-u^{i,n}}{\Delta t}}={\rm div}\left\{T^{\epsilon,\ell}(v^{i,n+1})\left(\overset{m}{\underset{j=1}{\sum}}A_{ij}\nabla\rho_{\eta}\star\rho_{\eta}\star u^{j,n+1}+\delta\nabla u^{i,n+1}\right)\right\}.$ (1.27) We will look for fixed points solutions $v^{i,n+1}=u^{i,n+1}$ of this modified system. Finally, we will recover the expected result dropping one after one all the approximations. 1.5 Organization of the paper In Section 2, we recall some useful tools. In Section 3, we study system ( 1.1 ). By discretizing our problem on time, in Subsection 3.1, we obtain an elliptic problem. We use the Lax-Milgram theorem to show the existence of a unique solution to the linear problem ( 1.27 ). We demonstrate, in Subsection 3.2, the existence of a solution of the nonlinear problem, using the Schaefer’s fixed point theorem. Then we pass to the limit in the following order: $(\Delta t,\epsilon)\rightarrow(0,0)$ in Subsection 3.3, $(\ell,\eta)\rightarrow(\infty,0)$ in Subsection 3.4 and $\delta\rightarrow 0$ in Subsection 3.5.
Generalizations (including more general matrices $A$ or tensors) will be presented in Section 4. We end with an Appendix showing some technical results in Section 5. 2 Preliminary tools Theorem 2.1 . Then we have to take $\rho_{\eta}\star(A_{ij}(x,u)(\nabla\rho_{\eta}\star u^{j}))$ instead of $A_{ij}\nabla(\rho_{\eta}\star\rho_{\eta}\star u^{j})$ in the approximate problem. We can consider a problem $u^{i}_{t}={\rm div}\left(u^{i}\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{ij}(x,u)\nabla u\right)+g^{i}(x,u),\quad\mbox{with }g^{i}\geq 0,$ where the source terms are not too large as $u$ goes to infinity. 4.2.3 Laplace-type equations Moreover, our method applies to models of the form $u^{i}_{t}=\Delta(a_{i}(u)u^{i})\quad\mbox{with }u=(u^{i})_{1\leq i\leq m},$ (4.6) under these assumptions: $\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}&a_{i}(u)\geq 0\quad\mbox{if}\quad u^{j}\geq 0\quad\mbox{for }j=1,\ldots,m,\\
&a_{i}\quad\mbox{is sublinear},\\
&a_{i}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}),\\
&\displaystyle Sym\left(\left(\frac{\partial a_{i}}{\partial u_{j}}\right)_{i,j}\right)\geq\delta_{0}I\quad\mbox{with}\quad\delta_{0}>0,\end{array}\right.$ where $Sym$ denotes the symmetric part of a matrix.
We can consider a particular case of ( 4.6 ) where $\displaystyle a_{i}(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{ij}u^{j}$ . Then problem ( 4.6 ) can be written as $u^{i}_{t}={\rm div}\left\{u^{i}\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{ij}\nabla u^{j}+\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}A_{ij}u_{j}\right)\nabla u^{i}\right\},$ (4.12) which can be also solved under these assumptions: $\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{cll}&A_{ij}\geq 0\quad\mbox{for}\quad i,j=1,\ldots,m,\\
&Sym(A)\geq\delta_{0}I.\end{array}\right.$ 4.2.4 Diffusion matrix We can consider the model $u^{i}_{t}=div\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}B_{ijk}u^{j}\nabla u^{k}\right),\quad\mbox{ for }i=1,\ldots,m.$ (4.14) where $Sym\left((\sum_{j=1}^{m}B_{ijk})_{i,k}\right)\geq\delta_{0}I$ . 5 Appendix: Technical results In this section we will present some technical results that are used in our proofs. Proposition 5.1 . ( Recovering the initial condition ) Let Y be a Banach space with the norm $\left\|.\right\|_{Y}$ . Consider a sequence $(g_{m})_{m}\in C(0,T;Y)$ such
that $(g_{m})_{t}$ is uniformly bounded in $L^{q}(0,T;Y)$ with $1<q\leq\infty$ , and $(g_{m})_{|t=0}\rightarrow g_{0}$ in $Y$ . Then there exists $g\in C(0,T;Y)$ such that $g_{m}\rightarrow g$ in $C(0,T;Y)$ and $g_{|t=0}=g_{0}\qquad\mbox{in }Y.$ Proof. We have that for all $s<t\in(0,T)$ $\displaystyle\|g_{m}(t)-g_{m}(s)\|_{Y}$ $\displaystyle=$ $\displaystyle\left\|\int_{s}^{t}(g_{m})_{\tau}(\tau)\right\|_{Y}$ (5.1) $\displaystyle\leq$ $\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\|(g_{m})_{\tau}(\tau)\|_{Y}\;ds$ $\displaystyle\leq$ $\displaystyle(t-s)^{\frac{q-1}{q}}\;\|(g_{m})_{\tau}(\tau)\|_{L^{q}(0,T;Y)}\leq(t-s)^{\frac{q-1}{q}}C,$ where we have used in the second line Holder’s inequality, and the fact that $(g_{m})_{\tau}$ is uniformly bounded in $L^{q}(0,T;Y)$ . Since ( 5.1 ) implies the equicontinuity of $(g_{m})_{m}$ , by Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, there exists $g\in C(0,T;Y)$ such that $g_{m}\rightarrow g$ in $C(0,T;Y)$ .
Moreover, Taking $s=0$ in ( 5.1 ) we get $\|g_{m}(t)-g_{m}(0)\|_{Y}\leq t^{\frac{q-1}{q}}C.$ (5.2) By passing to the limit in $m$ in ( 5.2 ), we deduce that $\displaystyle\|g(t)-g_{0}\|_{Y}\leq t^{\frac{q-1}{q}}C$ Particularly, for $t=0$ , we have $\displaystyle\|g(0)-g_{0}\|_{Y}=0.$ This implies the result. ∎ Lemma 5.2 . ( Convergence result ) Let $(a_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon}$ a real sequence such that $a_{\epsilon}\rightarrow a_{0}$ as $\epsilon\rightarrow 0$ . Then we have $\Psi_{0,\ell}(a_{0})\leq\liminf_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\Psi_{\epsilon,\ell}(a_{\epsilon}),$ where $\Psi_{\epsilon,\ell}$ and ${\Psi_{0,\ell}}$ are given in ( 3.2 ) and ( 3.3 ) respectively. Proof. Consider the case where $a_{0}=0$ . We suppose that the sequence $(a_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon}\in(-\infty;\frac{1}{e}]$ . Let $(b_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon}\in(-\infty;\frac{1}{e}]$ a sequence that decreases to $0$ as $\epsilon\rightarrow 0$ with $b_{\epsilon}>a_{\epsilon}$ . Since $\Psi_{\epsilon,\ell}$ is decreasing on $(-\infty;\frac{1}{e}]$ we have $\Psi_{\epsilon,\ell}(a_{\epsilon})\geq\Psi_{\epsilon,\ell}(b_{\epsilon})$ . Moreover, using the fact that $\Psi_{\epsilon,\ell}(b_{\epsilon})\rightarrow 0=\Psi_{0,\ell}(0)$ we get the result. Otherwise, when $(a_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon}\in(\frac{1}{e};+\infty)$ the proof is the same as above but with taking $b_{\epsilon}<a_{\epsilon}$ since $\Psi_{\epsilon,\ell}$ is nondecreasing in $(\frac{1}{e};+\infty)$ . For the other cases, $a_{0}<0$ and $a_{0}>0$ , the result is easily obtained. ∎ Acknowledgments R.M. thanks his grant SAAW.
