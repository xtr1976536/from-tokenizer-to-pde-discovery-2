To do this we apply the “fictitious control method” in two cases: general systems with a “non-degenerate” coupling, and a particular case where the coupling is “degenerate”, namely a cubic coupling. In the latter case, our proof requires to find nontrivial trajectories of the control system that go from $\displaystyle 0$ to $\displaystyle 0$ . We build these trajectories by adapting (in $\displaystyle 1$ space dimension) a construction developed by Jean-Michel Coron, Sergio Guerrero and Lionel Rosier for the study of coupled parabolic systems. Keywords. Wave equations, coupled systems, exact internal controllability, fictitious control method, algebraic solvability, return method. 1 Main results and outline of proof 1.1 Control systems Let $\displaystyle T>0$ , and $\displaystyle 0<a<b<L$ . We study the following class of systems: $\left\{\begin{aligned} u_{tt}-\nu_{1}^{2}u_{xx}&=f_{1}(u,v)+h,\ x\in[0,L],\\

In what follows we shall note, for any such $\displaystyle\nu\neq 0$ , $\square_{\nu}:=\partial_{tt}-\nu^{2}\partial_{xx}$ We will also study the following particular system: $\left\{\begin{aligned} \square_{\nu_{1}}u&=h,\ x\in[0,L],\\
\square_{\nu_{2}}v&=u^{3},\ x\in[0,L],\\

v&=0\ \textrm{on}\ \{0,L\}.\end{aligned}\right.$ (1.2) These are systems of coupled semilinear wave equations, with different speeds, which we seek to control with a single control, which takes the form of a source term in the first equation. In both cases, as we will study solutions with $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{k}$ regularity in order to establish a controllability result with two controls, the initial and final conditions $\displaystyle((u_{0},u_{1}),(v_{0},v_{1}),(u_{0}^{f},u_{1}^{f}),(v_{0}^{f},v_{1}^{f}))$ have to satisfy some compatibility conditions. There exists a multivariate polynomial $\displaystyle Q_{n,i}^{f_{i}}$ such that $\left(\frac{d}{dt}\right)^{n}\left(f_{i}(u,v)\right)=Q_{n,i}^{f_{i}}\left(J_{t}^{n}(u,v)\right),\ i=1,2,$ (1.4) where $\displaystyle J_{t}^{n}(u,v)$ denotes the $\displaystyle n$ -jet of time derivatives of $\displaystyle u$ and $\displaystyle v$ , that is $\left(u,v,u_{t},v_{t},\cdots,\partial_{t}^{n}u,\partial_{t}^{n}v\right).$ Now, define by recurrence the following family of operators: $\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{D}_{1}^{(i)}&=\partial_{t}\\

Now, ( 1.6 ) can be written in the corners using only $\displaystyle u_{0},u_{1},u_{0}^{f},u_{1}^{f},v_{0},v_{1},v_{0}^{f},v_{1}^{f}$ , which gives the following compatibility conditions of order $\displaystyle k$ : $\left\{\begin{aligned} P_{n,i}^{f_{i}}\left(J_{x}^{n}(u_{0},v_{0})(0),J_{x}^{n-1}(u_{1},v_{1})(0),(0,\cdots,0)\right)&=0,\\
P_{n,i}^{f_{i}}\left(J_{x}^{n}(u_{0},v_{0})(L),J_{x}^{n-1}(u_{1},v_{1})(L),(0,\cdots,0)\right)&=0,\\

P_{n,i}^{f_{i}}\left(J_{x}^{n}(u_{0}^{f},v_{0}^{f})(L),J_{x}^{n-1}(u_{1}^{f},v_{1}^{f})(L),(0,\cdots,0)\right)&=0,\end{aligned}\right.\quad\forall n\leq k,i=1,2.$ (1.8) The existence and unicity of solutions to these systems can be derived from TaTsien Li’s general results on quasilinear wave equations (see [ LR03 ] or [ Li10 , chapter 5, section 5.2] ). As for second order systems, results of global boundary and internal controllability for the semilinear wave equation are well known, and were first proven by Enrique Zuazua in [ Zua93 ] and [ Zua91 ] . These articles introduced the use of HUM (Hilbert Uniqueness Method) to prove controllability results for semilinear and quasilinear equations. Boundary controllability results for scalar systems with $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{2}$ regularity can be found in [ Li10 , chapter 5] , and can be adapted to coupled systems, and for $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{k}$ regularity. Regarding controllability with a reduced number of controls, results for boundary and internal control of linear wave systems with a reduced number of controls have been proved by Fatiha Alabau-Boussouira ( [ ABL13 ] and [ AB13 ] ) in any space dimension, using energy methods. This was used by Fatiha Alabau Boussouira in [ AB14 ] to prove the existence of insensitizing controls for a single wave equation, as this is linked to the controllability of linear cascade systems in one space dimension, with the same speed in both equations. In the nonlinear case, Louis Tebou followed the same path for semilinear equations in [ Teb11 ] , where he proves the controllability of cascade systems of the form: $\left\{\begin{aligned} \square u+f(u)&=h+\xi,\\
\square v+f^{\prime}(u)v&=0,\\
u=0,v(t,0)&=\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\chi_{\Gamma_{0}}\ \textrm{on}\ \partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.$ (1.15) where $\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}$ is a portion of the boundary, and where $\displaystyle f$ is subject to a growth constraint to have global well-posedness. To prove the controllability of such systems, the author first establishes the controllability of a linear problem, using a form of HUM combined with Carleman estimates. Then, using the Schauder fixed-point theorem, he establishes the controllability of the nonlinear problem. A similar strategy of proof appears in [ CGR10 ] for parabolic systems with cubic coupling. In this case, as for system ( 1.2 ), the linearised system around the equilibrium $\displaystyle(0,0,0)$ is not controllable. A classical tool to handle this problem in finite dimension is the use of iterated Lie brackets, see for example [ Isi95 , chapter 2] , [ NvdS90 , chapter 3] , and [ Cor07 , chapter 3] . However, this tool does not work (see for example [ Cor07 , chapter 5] ) for many partial differential equations, including our control system ( 1.2 ). In that case, a method to handle this situation is the return method. It consists in looking for trajectories going from $\displaystyle 0$ to $\displaystyle 0$ and such that the linearised system around them is controllable (return trajectories). This method has been introduced in [ Cor92 ] for the stabilisation of driftless control systems and in [ Cor96 ] and in [ Cor93 ] for the controllability of the Euler equations of incompressible fluids. Following this method, in [ CGR10 ] the authors build return trajectories, using the structure of the coupling.
Then, using Carleman estimates, they prove the controllability of a family of related parabolic linear systems close to the return trajectory, from which they deduce null-controllability using Kakutani’s fixed-point theorem. In other cases, a phenomenon of loss of derivatives can occur when working with $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{k}$ regularities: this can be handled with an inversion theorem of the Nash-Moser type, with a stronger condition on the linearised system. This was done in the case for quasilinear first order hyperbolic systems, which have been studied in [ ABCO17 ] , using the “fictitious control method”, which we will explain in the following section. More precisely the result that has been obtained concerns systems of the form: $\left\{\begin{aligned} u_{t}+\Lambda_{1}(u,v)(u,v)+f_{1}(u,v)&=h,\\
v_{t}+\Lambda_{2}(u,v)(u,v)+f_{2}(u,v)&=0.\end{aligned}\right.$ with $\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial u}(0,0)\neq 0.$ (1.16) 1.3 The fictitious control method The fictitious control method was introduced in [ Cor92 ] and [ GBPGa05 ] , and successfully used in [ CL14 ] , [ ABCO17 ] and [ CG17 ] . The idea is to first prove a controllability result with two controls (the fictitious controls), then reduce the number of controls, using some sort of fixed-point theorem. In this article, we apply it to second order hyperbolic systems, which present the same problem of loss of derivatives as the systems in [ ABCO17 ] . This loss of derivatives is handled by using Gromov’s notion of algebraic solvability, which allows for differential operators to be inverted in a special way under some condition (infinitesimal inversion) on their derivative. This yields local results around the equilibrium, but we will also work around other trajectories than the stationary trajectory at the equilibrium, in the spirit of the return method, paying close attention to the regularities involved. Indeed, condition ( 1.10 ) from Theorem 1.1 is identical to condition ( 1.16 ), and is crucial to solving the system algebraically (see Proposition 2.2 ). If, as in Theorem 1.2 , it is not satisfied, then, following the spirit of the return method, one can build trajectories of the system along which such a condition is verified, at least on some appropriate spatial domain. Remark 1.1 . In both cases, conditions ( 1.10 ) and ( 1.16 ) appear as a sufficient condition on the coupling for internal controllability with a reduced number of controls. However there is no indication (except in trivial cases like the linearised system above) that this sort of condition is necessary. We can thus sum up our strategy of proof in three steps: 1. When necessary, find smooth trajectories around which Theorem 2.1 can be used. 2. Prove a local controllability result with two controls (fictitious controls) around the return trajectory, using classical boundary control results. 3. Use Theorem 2.1 to reduce the number of controls to one. This article is organised as follows: in section 2, we illustrate Gromov’s ideas on a linear example, and then prove Theorem 1.1 , which is a case where we do not need to find return trajectories. This will allow us to present how Gromov’s ideas can be applied in a nonlinear setting. In section 3 we prove Theorem 1.2 . In this case we need to find return trajectories, and the application of Theorem 2.1 around those trajectories will require a more detailed knowledge of the supports of the return trajectory. Finally section 4 is devoted to possible improvements and further questions on this topic. 2 The non-degenerate case As mentioned in section 1.3 , we build on the method presented in [ ABCO17 ] . One of the main ingredients of this method is the theory of differential operators, and the notion of algebraic solvability, which we briefly present in the subsection below. The use of algebraic solvability in the study of control systems first appears in [ Cor92 ] , where it was used to prove the stabilisability of finite dimensional systems without drift with time-varying feedbacks. It was first used in the context of partial differential equations in [ CL14 ] for the control of the Navier-Stokes equation. But first let us give an informal explanation of our method in the case of a linear system: first we have to rewrite the control problem using differential operators. We note $\displaystyle\mathscr{D}$ the operator associated with the equation of our control problem. Then, the control problem, given initial and final conditions, consists in finding $\displaystyle(u,v)$ with those initial and final conditions, and a control $\displaystyle h$ such that $\mathscr{D}(u,v,h)=0.$ This corresponds to an inversion problem, but with a twist: one has to find an inverse image with the right initial and final conditions. Now, using the solutions to forward- and backward-evolving Cauchy problems corresponding to the initial and final conditions, one can build functions $\displaystyle(u,v)$ with the right initial and final conditions. The nonlinear version of this is done at the beginning of subsection 2.2 . In general, one can do this so that for some $\displaystyle\eta>0$ , $(h_{1},h_{2}):=\mathscr{D}(u,v,0)=0,\forall t\notin[\eta,T-\eta].$ Now suppose $\displaystyle\mathscr{D}$ is invertible. We can make the following computation, the nonlinear version of which is made in subsection 2.2 : $\mathscr{D}\left((u,v,0)+\mathscr{D}^{-1}(-h_{1},-h_{2})\right)=(h_{1},h_{2})-(h_{1},h_{2})=0.$ This seems to yield a solution to the control problem, however we still need to check that the “corrective term” does not change the initial and final conditions. This is where Gromov’s notion of algebraic solvability comes into play: the right property for $\displaystyle\mathscr{D}$ is not to be invertible, but to be algebraically solvable. That is, that the inverse can also be written as a differential operator: $\mathscr{D}^{-1}(-h_{1},-h_{2})=\sum_{r}a_{r}\partial_{r}(-h_{1})+\sum_{r}b_{r}\partial_{r}(-h_{2})$ for some functions $\displaystyle a_{r},b_{r}$ . With this additional property, one can see that, because $\displaystyle-h_{1},-h_{2}$ vanish for $\displaystyle t\notin[\eta,T-\eta]$ , $\mathscr{D}^{-1}(-h_{1},-h_{2})=0,\forall t\notin[\eta,T-\eta].$ Hence, $\displaystyle(u,v,0)+\mathscr{D}^{-1}(-h_{1},-h_{2})$ still has the right initial and final conditions. 2.1 Differential relations and Gromov’s theorem In this section we sum up some basic notions regarding differential operators, and Gromov’s local inversion theorem for differential operators. More details can be found in [ Gro86 ] . In what follows, $\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}$ is the closure of a non-empty open bounded smooth subset of $\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}$ , and $\displaystyle p,q,r\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}$ . We note $\displaystyle n_{r,p}:=2+p\textup{ card}\{(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\ |\ \alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\leq r\}$ . Recall the definition of the $\displaystyle r$ -jet of a function $\displaystyle z\in\mathit{C}^{r}(\mathcal{Q})^{p}$ : $J^{r}z(t,x)=\left((t,x),z(t,x),\cdots,\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}z}{\partial t^{\alpha_{1}}\partial x^{\alpha_{2}}},\cdots,\frac{\partial^{r}z}{\partial t^{\alpha_{1}}\partial x^{\alpha_{2}}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{r,p}},\ \forall(t,x)\in\mathcal{Q}.$ Definition 2.1 . A map $\displaystyle\mathscr{D}:\mathit{C}^{r}(\mathcal{Q})^{p}\rightarrow\mathit{C}^{0}(\mathcal{Q})^{q}$ is a $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{\infty}$ nonlinear differential operator of order $\displaystyle r$ if there exists $\displaystyle F\in\mathit{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{r,p}},\mathbb{R}^{q})$ such that $\mathscr{D}(z)=F(J^{r}z),\ \forall z\in\mathit{C}^{r}(\mathcal{Q})^{p}.$ This clearly implies that $\displaystyle\mathscr{D}$ is $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{\infty}$ (with the usual $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{r},\mathit{C}^{0}$ topologies), and we denote by $\mathscr{L}_{z}:\mathit{C}^{r}(\mathcal{Q})^{p}\rightarrow\mathit{C}^{0}(\mathcal{Q})^{q}$ its Fréchet differential at $\displaystyle z\in\mathit{C}^{r}(\mathcal{Q})^{p}$ . We now define some sort of manifold, over which we can invert these operators: Definition 2.2 . A subset $\displaystyle\mathcal{A}$ of $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{d}(\mathcal{Q})^{p}$ is a differential relation of order $\displaystyle d\in\mathbb{N}$ if there exists $\displaystyle\mathcal{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{d,p}}$ such that $\mathcal{A}=\{z\in\mathit{C}^{d}(\mathcal{Q})^{p}\ |\ \forall(t,x)\in\mathcal{Q},J^{d}z(t,x)\in\mathcal{R}\}.$ It is said to be open if $\displaystyle\mathcal{R}$ is an open subset of $\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n_{d,p}}$ .
For $\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}$ , we note $\mathcal{A}^{k}:=\mathcal{A}\cap\mathit{C}^{k}(\mathcal{Q})^{p}$ For classical local inversion theorems, one needs the differential at one point to be invertible. Here the requirement is somewhat stronger: we need the differential at any point to be invertible, with the extra property that the inverse of each differential is also a linear differential operator. Definition 2.3 . Let $\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\subset\mathit{C}^{d}(\mathcal{Q})^{p}$ be a differential relation of order $\displaystyle d$ , and let $\displaystyle\mathscr{D}$ be a differential operator of order $\displaystyle r$ . We say that $\displaystyle\mathscr{D}$ admits an infinitesimal inversion of order $\displaystyle s\in\mathbb{N}$ over $\displaystyle\mathcal{A}$ if there exists a family of linear differential operators of order $\displaystyle s$ $z\in\mathcal{A},\mathscr{M}_{z}:\mathit{C}^{s}(\mathcal{Q})^{q}\rightarrow\mathit{C}^{0}(\mathcal{Q})^{p},$ such that: 1. For every $\displaystyle g\in\mathit{C}^{s}(\mathcal{Q})^{q}$ , $\displaystyle z\mapsto\mathscr{M}_{z}(g)$ is a differential operator of order d (possibly nonlinear) and it is a $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{\infty}$ -differential operator in $\displaystyle(z,g)$ . 2. (Algebraic solvability) For every $\displaystyle z\in\mathcal{A}^{d+r}$ , $\mathscr{L}_{z}\circ\mathscr{M}_{z}=\textrm{Id}_{\mathit{C}^{r+s}(\mathcal{Q})}.$ We can now state Gromov’s inversion theorem (see [ Gro86 , Section 2.3.2, main theorem] ): Theorem 2.1 (Gromov) . Let $\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\subset\mathit{C}^{d}(\mathcal{Q})^{p}$ be a non-empty open differential relation of order $\displaystyle d$ , and let $\displaystyle\mathscr{D}$ be a differential operator of order $\displaystyle r$ . Assume that $\displaystyle\mathscr{D}$ admits an infinitesimal inversion of order $\displaystyle s$ over $\displaystyle\mathcal{A}$ . (Neighbourhood property) For every $\displaystyle z\in\mathcal{A}^{\sigma_{0}+r+s}$ , $\displaystyle 0\in\mathcal{B}_{z}$ and $\mathcal{B}:=\bigcup_{z\in\mathcal{A}^{\sigma_{0}+r+s}}\{z\}\times\mathcal{B}_{z}$ is an open subset of $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{\sigma_{0}+r+s}(\mathcal{Q})^{p}\times\mathit{C}^{\sigma_{0}+s}(\mathcal{Q})^{q}$ . 2. (Inversion property) $\mathscr{D}\left(\mathscr{D}_{z}^{-1}(g)\right)=\mathscr{D}(z)+g,\ \forall(z,g)\in\mathcal{B}.$ (2.3) 3. (Normalisation property) $\mathscr{D}_{z}^{-1}(0)=z,\ \forall z\in\mathcal{A}^{\sigma_{0}+r+s}.$ (2.4) 4. (Regularity and continuity)
Let $\displaystyle\sigma_{0}\leq\sigma_{1}\leq\eta_{1}$ , then for all $\displaystyle z\in\mathcal{A}^{\eta_{1}+r+s}$ and $\displaystyle g\in\mathcal{B}_{z}^{\sigma_{1}+s}:=\mathcal{B}_{z}\cap\mathit{C}^{\sigma_{1}+s}$ , $\mathscr{D}^{-1}_{z}(g)\in\mathcal{A}^{k},\quad\forall k<\sigma_{1}.$ (2.5) Moreover, $(z,g)\mapsto\mathscr{D}^{-1}_{z}(g)\in\mathit{C}^{0}(\mathcal{A}^{\sigma_{0}+r+s}\times\mathcal{B}_{z}^{\sigma_{1}+s},\mathcal{A}^{k}),\quad\forall k<\sigma_{1}.$ (2.6) Finally, if $\displaystyle\eta_{1}>\sigma_{1}$ , then ( 2.5 ) and ( 2.6 ) hold for $\displaystyle k=\sigma_{1}$ . 5. (Locality) For every $\displaystyle(t,x)\in\mathcal{Q}$ , and for every $\displaystyle(z_{1},g_{1}),(z_{2},g_{2})\in\mathcal{B}$ , if we have $(z_{1},g_{1})(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})=(z_{2},g_{2})(\tilde{t},\tilde{x}),\ \forall(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})\in B((t,x),\nu)\cap\mathcal{Q},$ then, $\mathscr{D}_{z_{1}}^{-1}(g_{1})(t,x)=\mathscr{D}_{z_{2}}^{-1}(g_{2})(t,x).$ Remark 2.1 . The neighbourhood property allows to relate the domains of inversion for each local inversion to each other: local inverses at two “neighbouring” points will be defined on domains that have “neighbouring” sizes. In particular that means the domains of inversions are bound to overlap. The locality property tells us that when this happens (albeit locally), the images of the local inverses agree locally. In the linear case, this corresponds to the fact that when a function vanishes on an open set, its image by any linear differential operator also vanishes on this open set (see the beginning of the section). 2.2 From two controls to one: algebraic solvability As in the linear case, we first build a trajectory $\displaystyle(u,v)$ with the right initial and final conditions, but with $\displaystyle\mathscr{D}(u,v,0)$ potentially non-zero on some restricted domain. To build trajectories that start at $\displaystyle 0$ and return there, the idea is to use the cascade structure of the equation: first we find a $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{\infty}([-1,1]\times[0,1])$ function $\displaystyle\bar{v}$ such that $\displaystyle\square\bar{v}$ is the third power of a $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{\infty}([-1,1]\times[0,1])$ function $\displaystyle\bar{u}$ . By setting the right conditions at the start and end times, this gives us a return trajectory. The corresponding control will then be $\displaystyle\square_{\nu_{1}}\bar{u}$ . Let us recall that $\displaystyle x\mapsto\sqrt[3]{x}$ is $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{\infty}$ on $\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{\ast}$ . So, by composition, the cubic root of a $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{\infty}$ function $\displaystyle f$ is $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{\infty}$ at all the points where $\displaystyle f$ is non-zero. At the points where $\displaystyle f$ vanishes, by Taylor’s formula, a fairly simple sufficient condition for $\displaystyle\sqrt[3]{f}$ to be $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{\infty}$ at those points is for $\displaystyle f$ to vanish, along with its first and second derivatives, while its third derivative is non-zero. Now, to find functions whose image by the wave operator is a third power of a $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{\infty}$ function, we consider the solutions to the corresponding stationary problem, namely functions whose Laplacian is the third power of a $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{\infty}$ function. As is often the case when using a Nash-Noser scheme, these regularities are probably not optimal. However, if we require for example $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{k}$ regularity for the control, $\displaystyle k\geq 2$ , the initial and final data have to be at least one notch smoother. Indeed, note $w:=u_{t}-\nu_{1}u_{x},$ and consider to the following computation, where one requires the control and the trajectories to be $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{k}$ : $\begin{array}[]{rl}\frac{d}{dt}w(t,x-\nu_{1}t)&=w_{t}-\nu_{1}w_{x}\\
&=w_{t}+\nu_{1}w_{x}-2\nu_{1}w_{x}\\
&=h(t,x-\nu_{1}t)-2\nu_{1}(u_{tx}-\nu_{1}u_{xx})\\
&=h(t,x-\nu_{1}t)-2\nu_{1}\frac{d}{dt}u_{x}(t,x-\nu_{1}t),\end{array}$ hence, for a fixed $\displaystyle t>0$ and for characteristics going from $\displaystyle\{0\}\times[0,L]$ to $\displaystyle\{t\}\times[0,L]$ , $\int_{0}^{t}h(s,x-\nu_{1}s)ds=u_{t}(t,x-\nu_{1}t)-u_{1}(x)-\nu_{1}u_{x}(t,x-\nu_{1}t)+\nu_{1}u_{0}^{\prime}(x)-2\nu_{1}(u_{x}(t,x-\nu_{1}t)-u_{0}^{\prime}(x)).$ (4.1) Now the left-hand side of ( 4.1 ) is of class $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{k}$ , so we need to upgrade the regularity of $\displaystyle u_{0}$ to $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{k+1}$ , and that of $\displaystyle u_{1}$ , $\displaystyle x\mapsto u_{x}(t,x)$ and $\displaystyle x\mapsto u_{t}(t,x)$ to $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{k}$ . This shows a partial derivative loss (in the space dimension) between the trajectory and the control, and, taking $\displaystyle t=T$ , a derivative loss between the initial and final data for $\displaystyle u$ , and the control. For linear cascade systems with smooth coefficients, the same procedure can be repeated on the second equation to establish similar losses of derivatives, showing that because $\displaystyle u$ has increased spatial regularity, the initial and final data for $\displaystyle v$ have to be $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{k+2}\times\mathit{C}^{k+1}$ . Note that with two controls, this would not be the case, as each control “absorbs” the loss of derivatives in each equation. Furthermore, it would be interesting to consider other iteration schemes such as the one presented in [ Cor07 ] , section 4.2.1, where one considers the following linear system: $\left\{\begin{aligned} \square u&=f_{1}(0,0)+g_{1}^{v}(a,b)v+g_{1}^{u}(a,0)u+h\\
\square v&=f_{2}(0,0)+g_{2}^{v}(0,b)v+g_{2}^{u}(a,b)u,\end{aligned}\right.$ (4.2) where, for $\displaystyle i\in\{1,2\}$ , $g_{i}^{u}(u,v)=\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{f_{i}(u,v)-f_{i}(0,v)}{u}\ &\textrm{for}\ u\neq 0\\
\partial_{u}f_{i}(0,v)\ &\textrm{for}\ u=0.\end{aligned}\right.$ $g_{i}^{v}(u,v)=\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{f_{i}(u,v)-f_{i}(u,0)}{v}\ &\textrm{for}\ v\neq 0\\
\partial_{v}f_{i}(u,0)\ &\textrm{for}\ v=0.\end{aligned}\right.$ Then, by superposition one can restrict to the study of $\left\{\begin{aligned} \square u&=h\\
\square v&=g_{2}^{u}(a,b)u.\end{aligned}\right.$ (4.3) But ultimately, this only shifts the problem of the $\displaystyle\mathit{C}^{k}$ regularity gap between data and control, although we now have a linear system instead of a semilinear one. On the other hand, it is now well known that in some cases, results that were obtained using the Nash-Moser iteration scheme can also be obtained through more classical iteration schemes, see for example the works of Matthias Gunther ( [ G8̈9 ] , [ G9̈1 ] ). It would be interesting to know if a similar do-over is possible for our result. Finally, it would be interesting to investigate a $\displaystyle H^{k}$ version of this result, using other versions of the Nash-Moser implicit function theorem. 4.2 Other degenerate couplings Our scheme of proof also allows to prove a controllability result for systems of the form $\left\{\begin{aligned} \square_{\nu_{1}}u&=G(u,v)+h,\ G\in\mathit{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\\
\square_{\nu_{2}}v&=u^{3},\\
u_{|\partial\Omega}&=0,\\
v_{|\partial\Omega}&=0.\end{aligned}\right.$ (4.4) Indeed, this simply adds a term in the definition of $\displaystyle\bar{h}$ when we build our return trajectory. However, $\displaystyle\bar{h}$ is no longer supported in $\displaystyle[0,T]\times[a,b]$ . The other steps remain unchanged, as the additional $\displaystyle G$ term does not prevent the differential operator $\displaystyle\mathscr{D}$ from being algebraically solvable. So we get a local internal controllability result with the same time conditions, but no condition on the support of the control. Finally, if $\displaystyle G$ is homogeneous of degree $\displaystyle 1$ , we can use the scaling argument to deduce a global result. In addition to adding a coupling term to the first equation, we can also change the power of the coupling term in the second equation. There are two cases: 1. Even powers As such, our method cannot work for even powers: indeed, $\displaystyle u^{2k}$ has nonnegative values. In particular, by the same convexity argument as in Remark 3.2 , solutions to the stationary problem cannot vanish smoothly in $\displaystyle 1$ . So the perturbative approach would allow us to build smooth return trajectories only if $\displaystyle u$ (and thus $\displaystyle h$ ) is spatially supported in all of $\displaystyle[0,L]$ . Another way of answering this question would be to switch to complex values, as is done in the appendix of [ CGR10 ] for the quadratic case. 2. Odd powers Thanks to Proposition 3.7 , we know that the part that requires the most work is the construction of return trajectories: say the power of the coupling is $\displaystyle 2k+1,\ k\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}$ , in order to control all the derivatives of $\displaystyle v$ up to $\displaystyle 2k+1$ , we would have to look for $\displaystyle v$ in the form $\sum_{i=0}^{2k+1}f_{i}(t)g_{i}\left(\frac{|x-\frac{b+a}{2}|}{\lambda}\right).$ This would call for ever longer computations, and for now there is no indication that there might or might not be new difficulties with these additional terms. 4.3 Boundary controllability In this article we have explored a method to prove internal controllability with one control. However, to our knowledge there is no result for boundary controllability with one control for semilinear systems such as ( 1.1 ). Although boundary controllability is relatively easy to establish for simple equations, or when there are the same number of controls and equations, we cannot use results on the inversion of differential operators to reduce the number of controls.
