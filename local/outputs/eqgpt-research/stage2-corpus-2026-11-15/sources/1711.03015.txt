original system of equations. Although the bacterium B. subtilis is used as a prototype, the method
and results apply in more generality. Introduction When grown in vitro , many species of bacteria exhibit a variety of complex spatial patterns which
depend upon environmental conditions such as the level of nutrients or the hardness of the substrate.
Continuous mean-field systems of partial differential equations for the bacterial density and the nutrient
concentration have been proposed to simulate these patterns with moderate to relative great success (see, for
example, Murray [ 44 ] , the discussion and criticism by Ben-Jacob and collaborators [ 6 , 22 ] , and the references therein). In a recent contribution, Leyva et al. [ 39 ] introduced a system of reaction-diffusion and nutrient taxis system of equations to model the complex
spatio-temporal dynamics exhibited by strains of the bacterium Bacillus subtilis observed in
experiments (cf. [ 46 ] ). The model reads $\begin{aligned} u_{t}&=\nabla\cdot\big(\sigma uv\nabla u\big)-\nabla\cdot\left(\sigma u^{2}v\frac{\chi_{0}K_{d}}{(K_{d}+v)^{2})}\nabla v\right)+\theta kuv,\\
v_{t}&=D_{v}\Delta v-kuv,\end{aligned}\qquad\quad x\in\Omega,\;\;t>0,$ (1.1) for scalar unknowns $u=u(x,t)$ and $v=v(x,t)$ , where $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$ , $n=1,2$ , is a bounded open
domain, with piecewise regular boundary, and $\sigma,\theta,k,K_{d},D_{v},\chi_{0}>0$ are positive
constants. In addition, $u$ and $v$ satisfy no-flux boundary conditions of the form $\begin{aligned} \left(uv\nabla u-u^{2}v\frac{\chi_{0}K_{d}}{(K_{d}+v)^{2})}\nabla v\right)\cdot\hat{\nu}&=0,\\
\nabla v\cdot\hat{\nu}&=0,\end{aligned}\qquad\quad x\in\partial\Omega,\;\;t>0,$ (1.2) where $\hat{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ , $|\hat{\nu}|=1$ , denotes the outer unit normal at each point of $\partial\Omega$ . System ( 1.1 ) is further endowed with initial conditions, $u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\;\;\;\;v(x,0)=v_{0}(x),\qquad x\in\Omega,$ (1.3) where $u_{0}$ and $v_{0}$ are known functions. In this model, $u$ and $v$ represent the bacterial density and the
nutrient concentration, respectively. Thus, $u_{0}$ and $v_{0}$ represent the initial spatial distribution of
bacteria and nutrient. The constant $\sigma$ is related to the hardness of the substrate on the Petri dish
(usually agar or a similar substance), $\theta$ is the conversion rate factor in the signal consumption
mechanism, $K_{d}$ is the receptor-ligand dissociation constant, $k$ is the intrinsic consumption rate, and $D_{v}$ is the diffusion coefficient for the nutrient. Notice that the equation for the bacterial density
contains a chemotactic term that models the response of the bacteria towards changes in the gradient of a
chemical signal (in this case, originated by the nutrient). The constant $\chi_{0}>0$ measures the intensity
of such signal. One of the main features of system ( 1.1 ) is the appearance of a non-linear, degenerate,
cross-diffusion coefficient in the bacterial density equation of the form $D_{u}(u,v)=\sigma uv,$ (1.4) which was originally proposed by Kawasaki et al. [ 32 ] . When grown
on semi-solid agar plates with poor-to-medium nutrient levels, the B. subtilis strain form densely
branched, highly structured stable patterns (cf. Oghiwari et al. [ 46 ] ). Experiments suggest that
the bacterial colony envelope is a boundary layer of cells whose activity is high around the front, but
immotile either inside of the envelope (where the nutrient is almost completely consumed) or in the outermost
region (where the bacterial density is still low). Kawasaki and collaborators modelled this behaviour by
allowing the diffusivity of the bacteria $D_{u}$ to be low when either the nutrient concentration or the
bacterial density are low, and consequently proposed the form of the diffusion coefficient in
( 1.4 ). The original model by Kawasaki et al. is a reaction-diffusion system with no
transport terms due to taxis. The motivation of Leyva and collaborators to extend Kawasaki’s model originated from the theoretical and
experimental work by E. Ben-Jacob and his group [ 5 , 14 , 22 ] , who argued in favour of the
chemotactic response of bacteria as an essential ingredient to understand some of the fundamental features of
the spatio-temporal patterns observed in experiments. Chemotactic terms in Keller-Segel type models [ 34 , 35 ] usually appear in the equation for bacterial density as the divergence of a chemotactic flux , $\boldsymbol{J}_{c}$ , having the generic form, $\boldsymbol{J}_{c}=-\zeta(u,v)\chi(v)\nabla v.$ The function $\chi=\chi(v)$ is known as the chemotactic sensitivity function and it usually depends
on the chemical concentration alone. The function $\zeta=\zeta(u,v)$ was labeled by Ben-Jacob as the bacterial response function . It measures the response of the bacteria to the effective sensed
gradient, namely, $\chi(v)\nabla v$ . Based on experimental observations [ 14 ] , Ben-Jacob and
collaborators [ 5 , 22 ] proposed an empirical rule: the bacterial response function should be
proportional to the product of the bacterial density and its corresponding diffusion
coefficient 1 1 1 notice that when the diffusion coefficient is constant, $D_{u}\equiv 1>0$ (after
normalizations), this rule implies that the bacterial response function should be $\zeta=u$ , and the
chemotactic flux reads $\boldsymbol{J}_{c}=-u\chi(v)\nabla v$ , recovering the standard Keller-Segel model [ 34 , 35 ] . , that is, $|\zeta|\propto uD_{u}.$ (1.5) Based on this suggestion and on the form of the effective diffusion coefficient ( 1.4 ), Leyva et al. [ 39 ] proposed a chemotactic flux of the form $\boldsymbol{J}_{c}=-\sigma u^{2}v\chi(v)\nabla v,$ where the chemotactic sensitivity obeys a Lapidus-Schiller receptor law [ 36 ] , $\chi(v)=\frac{\chi_{0}K_{d}}{(K_{d}+v)^{2}}.$ The result is the taxis term appearing in ( 1.1 ). This new chemotactic term affects the velocity of
the colony envelope as well as the morphology of the patterns, as numerical simulations and asymptotic
calculations actually show (see [ 39 ] ). In addition, the presence of an increasing chemotactic signal
supresses the onset for instabilities [ 10 ] (for a related discussion, see [ 3 ] ). The new model
seems to capture well the complexity of the observed patterns. In Figure 1 , the results of the
numerical computation of a non-dimensionalization of system ( 1.1 ) - ( 1.3 ) (see equations
( 3.21 ) - ( 3.23 ) below) are presented. The simulations were performed in the dense

(figure 1(b) ). The parameter values considered were $\sigma_{0}=4.0$ (soft agar) and $\chi_{0}=2.5$ (color plot online). Density-dependent diffusion coefficients are used in many biological contexts, such as continuous models in
population biology [ 2 , 23 , 45 ] , spatial ecology [ 57 ] , and eukaryotic cell biology [ 55 ] , just to mention a few. From the mathematical viewpoint, when the nonlinearity of the diffusion
coefficient is degenerate (meaning that diffusion approaches zero when the density does also), these
equations or systems of equations are endowed with interesting properties. Among the new mathematical
features one finds that equations with degenerate diffusion might exhibit finite speed of propagation
of initial disturbances [ 21 ] , in contrast with the strictly parabolic case. Another property is the
emergence of traveling waves of “sharp” type (cf. [ 53 , 56 ] ). The cross-diffusion system
( 1.1 ) has been the subject of recent investigations. Among the new findings we mention the global
existence of bounded weak solutions and the absence of finite time blow-up [ 61 ] , even in the case
of several space dimensions [ 60 ] . Thus, density-dependent diffusion systems with appropriately
coupled taxis terms warrant attention from the community working on the analysis of partial differential
equations thanks to their rich mathematical structure. The underlying problem when using macroscopic field equations like ( 1.1 ) is how to justify the
form of the chemotactic flux and/or of the diffusion coefficient which, under a pure phenomenological
approach, are simply postulated based on empiric considerations. Another issue is how to incorporate
microscopic responses of individual cells into the chemotactic sensitivity. There is, however, a stochastic
process that has become standard (and appropriate) for describing the motion of cells, known as
the velocity jump process . Based on experimental observations [ 7 , 8 , 33 ] showing
that individual cells exhibit dynamics based on a run-and-tumble type of motion (see section 2 below), kinetic transport equations of Boltzmann type with jumps in velocity space are common to model
the movement of biological agents and their chemotactic responses (see [ 51 , 1 , 47 ] and the
references therein). Moreover, kinetic equations have been also used to derive macroscopic mean-field
continuous equations after a limiting procedure (cf. [ 27 , 48 , 18 , 28 ] ),
involving outer (or Hilbert) asymptotic expansions which are customary in kinetic theory [ 17 , 24 , 37 ] . The purpose of this work is to take a closer look at the mathematical modelling approach that leads to the
particular form of system ( 1.1 ), by showing that the former can be retrieved as the parabolic
diffusion limit of a velocity-jump process. Under the assumptions of a parabolic scaling together with an
unbiased unperturbed turning kernel and a perturbed turning frequency (which depends on the chemical and
population densities), it is shown that the leading order term in the Hilbert expansion to solutions of the
Boltzmann type transport equation is a solution to the equation for the bacterial density in
( 1.1 ). The key ingredient is to consider that the unperturbed turning frequency does not depend on
the velocity, but does depend on the zero velocity moment (or marginal density) of the agent population as
well as on the chemical concentration. Furthermore, the next order in the expansion of the turning
frequency does depend on the velocity and encodes the bacterial response to the chemotactic signal. In
addition, it is also shown that kinetic no-mass-flux boundary conditions for solutions to the transport
equation lead to the no flux boundary conditions for $u$ in ( 1.2 ). These observations are summarized in
Theorem 3.5 . It is important to mention that this procedure is strictly formal. Yet, the
main purpose of this derivation is to connect the forms of the chemotactic flux
term and of the density dependent cross-diffusion coefficient ( 1.4 ) with microscopic features of
the underlying velocity-jump process such as the bias due to the chemotactic signal and the turning frequency
of individuals. In this fashion, a simple microscopic interpretation of the (otherwise purely
phenomenological) choices for the particular form of the diffusion and advection terms appearing in
( 1.1 ) is presented. Up to our knowledge, this work is the first attempt to derive degenerate
cross-diffusion coefficients and chemotactic sensitivities which are density-dependent and related to each
other, from a velocity jump process. Although the bacterium B. subtilis and system ( 1.1 )
are used as a prototype, the methodology and results apply more generally. The plan of the paper is as follows. In section 2 the master transport equation
governing velocity jump processes is recalled. Section 2.1 contains a description of the
method introduced by Hillen and Othmer [ 27 , 48 ] , and the interior limit under a
parabolic scaling is presented. Particular attention is devoted to boundary conditions. It is shown that, in
the limit, the no-flux boundary conditions in ( 1.2 ) depend on the no normal
mass flux across the boundary nature of the conditions imposed on the solutions to the transport equations,
regardless of their particular form. The central section 3 contains the mathematical
modelling of the perturbed turning kernel and frequency that leads to the explicit form of system
( 1.1 ). Finally, in section 4 the main features of this derivation and some
potential consequences, both at the mathematical and at the biological levels, are discussed. 2. Velocity-jump processes and formal asymptotic expansions Experimental observations show that some species of bacteria alternate two basic modes of motion: a simple
linear motion with constant speed, called a run , followed by a tumble , which is basically a
reorientation of the cell (for experimental evidence of this behaviour see [ 8 , 7 ] for the
species E. coli , and [ 33 ] for B. subtilis ). During a tumble the bacterium’s location
remains essentially constant while it spins around. After a tumble, the cell selects a new direction of
movement. Experiments show that (i) the duration of a tumble is short in comparison to that of a run; (ii)
the distribution of the change in direction between the run preceding a tumble and the following one is the
same from tumble to tumble; and, (iii) the speed of the bacterium during a run does not change from run to
run. To sum up, the frequency of tumbles seems to be a function of time, position and direction, whereas run
speed and distribution of the change in direction due to a tumble appear to be relatively constant. This
run-and-tumble type of bacterial movement can be efficiently described by a Boltzmann-type transport equation
from kinetic theory. This description is called a velocity-jump process, and it is based on the assumption
that generic agents (which can be particles, cells or microorganisms) make instantaneous jumps in velocity
space rather than in physical space. Thus, the motion consists on a sequence of runs separated by
reorientations during which a new velocity is chosen. It is assumed that the changes in velocity are the
result of a Poisson process of intensity $\lambda>0$ (called the turning frequency or turning
rate ), that is, the probability distribution specifying the time between turning events is exponential with
mean $1/\lambda$ (the mean run duration). In such processes, agents follow a given velocity $\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\in V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$ , $n=1,2,3$ , for a finite time.
Here $V$ is the set of allowed velocities (often regarded as a symmetric with respect to the origin, compact
set). A new velocity is chosen according to a function $T:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}$ , where $T(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})$ is the
probability density of turning from velocity $\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}$ to velocity $\boldsymbol{v}$ , given that a reorientation occurs. $T$ is called the turning kernel . Let $p=p(x,t,\boldsymbol{v})$ be the probability density function describing
the population of agents which, at position $x\in\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and at time $t>0$ , travel with
velocity $\boldsymbol{v}\in V$ . Here $\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is an open set. In a velocity jump process, the evolution
of $p$ is governed by the following transport (or forward Kolmogorov) equation (cf. [ 47 ] ), $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p(x,t,\boldsymbol{v})+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{x}p(x,t,\boldsymbol{v})=-\lambda p(x,t,\boldsymbol{v})+\lambda\int_{V}T(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})p(x,t,\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\,d\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}+\mathcal{G},$ (2.1) where $\mathcal{G}$ accounts for the rate of change in $p$ due to reaction. The drift term $\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{x}p$ represents straight runs with velocity $\boldsymbol{v}$ , whereas the absorption term $-\lambda p$ on the
right hand side corresponds to agents leaving the “state” $(x,\boldsymbol{v})$ (position $x$ with velocity $\boldsymbol{v}$ ). The
integral term accounts for the agents jumping into the state $(x,\boldsymbol{v})$ after a reorientation. Since $T:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}_{+}$ is a probability density, we assume it is non-negative and normalized so that $\int_{V}T(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\,d\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}=1,$ for all $\boldsymbol{v}\in V$ . For many purposes, one does not need to determine the distribution $p=p(x,t,\boldsymbol{v})$ but
only some of its first velocity moments, such as the marginal density (or zero moment), $\rho(x,t)=\int_{V}p(x,y,\boldsymbol{v})\,d\boldsymbol{v},$ which is the density of individuals at position $x$ at time $t>0$ , whatever their velocity. Velocity-jump processes have been often used to model the movement of biological individuals, such as the
bacteria E. coli [ 7 ] and B. subtilis [ 42 ] , or even reef fish larvae [ 13 ] . A backward equation similar to ( 2.1 ) was first derived by Stroock [ 58 ] to
describe random motion of bacteria. We employ the transport equation ( 2.1 ) as the master equation to
describe the motion of biological agents, as proposed by Othmer et al. [ 47 ] . Our goal is to
derive an approximate, continuous, mean-field limit system that encodes an external bias imposed on the
motion due to a chemical signal. For that purpose we follow the general method by Hillen and Othmer [ 27 , 48 ] , which is based on regular perturbation (Hilbert) expansions on solutions to equation
( 2.1 ). 2.1. The method of Hillen and Othmer Let $\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}$ be an open domain. The set of admissible velocities $V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is assumed
to
be symmetric with respect to the origin (that is, $\boldsymbol{v}\in V\Rightarrow-\boldsymbol{v}\in V$ ) and compact. Let us
assume that $\lambda>0$ is constant in $\boldsymbol{v}$ . Let $\mathcal{K}:=\{f\in L^{2}(V)\,:\,f\geq 0\}$ be
the cone of non-negative functions in $L^{2}(V)$ . Then, for fixed $(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,+\infty)$ we
regard ${\mathcal{T}}p(\boldsymbol{v}):=\int_{V}T(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})p(x,t,\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\,d\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime},$ (2.2) as an integral operator in $L^{2}(V)$ . Hillen and Othmer [ 27 ] impose the following structural
assumptions on $T$ . $T(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\geq 0$ for all $(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\in V\times V$ ; $\int_{V}T(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\,d\boldsymbol{v}=1$ , for all $\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\in V$ , and $\int_{V}\int_{V}T(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{v}\,d\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}<+\infty$ . (T 1 ) There exist functions $\eta_{0},\phi,\psi$ in $\mathcal{K}$ satisfying $\eta_{0}\not\equiv 0$ , $\phi,\psi\neq 0$ a.e., such that $\eta_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})\phi(\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\leq T(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\leq\eta_{0}(\boldsymbol{v})\psi(\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}),$ for all $(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\in V\times V$ . (T 2 ) $\|\mathcal{T}\|_{\langle 1\rangle^{\perp}\to\langle 1\rangle^{\perp}}<1$ , where $\langle 1\rangle^{\perp}\subset L^{2}(V)$ is the orthogonal complement of constants in $L^{2}(V)$ . (T 3 ) $\int_{V}T(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\,d\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}=1$ , for all $\boldsymbol{v}\in V$ . (T 4 ) For every given $\lambda>0$ , constant in $\boldsymbol{v}\in V$ , the turning operator , ${\mathcal{L}}:L^{2}(V)\to L^{2}(V)$ , is defined as ${\mathcal{L}}p(\boldsymbol{v}):=-\lambda p(\boldsymbol{v})+\lambda{\mathcal{T}}p(\boldsymbol{v})=-\lambda p(\boldsymbol{v})+\lambda\int_{V}T(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})p(\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\,d\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}.$ (2.3) The following theorem summarizes the most important properties of the turning operator that we shall need. Theorem 2.1 (Hillen and Othmer [ 27 , 48 ] ) . Under assumptions ( T1 ) - ( T2 ) the following properties hold: (a) $\mu=0$ is a simple eigenvalue of ${\mathcal{L}}:L^{2}(V)\to L^{2}(V)$ with eigenfunction $f(\boldsymbol{v})\equiv 1$ . (b) For all $g\in\langle 1\rangle^{\perp}$ , $\langle g,{\mathcal{L}}g\rangle_{L^{2}}=\int_{V}g{\mathcal{L}}g\,d\boldsymbol{v}\leq-\mu_{2}\|g\|_{L^{2}(V)}^{2},$ where $\mu_{2}=\lambda(1-\|\mathcal{T}\|_{\langle 1\rangle^{\perp}\to\langle 1\rangle^{\perp}})$ . (c) All non-zero eigenvalues $\mu$ of ${\mathcal{L}}$ satisfy the estimate $-2\lambda<\mathrm{Re}\,\mu\leq-\mu_{2}<0$ , and to within scalar multiples there is no other positive eigenfunction. (d) $\|{\mathcal{L}}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\leq 2\lambda$ . (e) ${\mathcal{L}}$ restricted to $\langle 1\rangle^{\perp}\subset L^{2}(V)$ has a linear pseudo-inverse ${\mathcal{F}}$ with
norm $\|{\mathcal{F}}\|_{\langle 1\rangle^{\perp}\to\langle 1\rangle^{\perp}}\leq\frac{1}{\mu_{2}}.$ Let us now consider a velocity-jump processes (for simplicity in the course of this section, in the absence
of reaction terms or external forces) governed by the equation $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p(x,t,\boldsymbol{v})+\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{x}p(x,t,\boldsymbol{v})=-\lambda p(x,t,\boldsymbol{v})+\int_{V}\lambda T(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})p(x,t,\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\,d\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime},$ (2.4) where the turning rate $\lambda$ may as well depend on the velocity $\boldsymbol{v}\in V$ and on other external
signals, represented by $S=S(x,t)$ (for example, the density function of a chemo-attractant or a
chemo-repellent substance). $\lambda$ may also depend on $(x,t)$ via other state variables such as the
marginal density $\rho=\rho(x,t)$ . As Hillen and Othmer point out, the dependence of $\lambda$ is not only
on $S$ pointwise (on the value of $S$ at $(x,t)$ ), but also on $\nabla S$ , or nonlocally on $S$ as well.
Thus, following [ 48 ] , we write $\lambda=\lambda(\boldsymbol{v},\hat{S})$ to indicate this behaviour. According to custom in the study of transport equations, we consider a scaling limit that allows to
approximate the description of the motion by a simpler model, such as a diffusion- or drift-dominated
partial differential equation. Here we adopt a parabolic (or diffusion) scaling with its corresponding
Hilbert expansion and limiting equation (cf. [ 27 , 26 , 28 ] ). The parabolic scaling takes the form $\tau=\epsilon^{2}t,\quad\xi=\epsilon x,$ (2.5) under the fundamental assumption that $\tau,\,\xi\,=O(1),\quad\text{as}\;\;\epsilon\to 0^{+}.$ (2.6) Remark 2.2 . In the description of the motion of bacteria, for instance, these quantities can be determined
experimentally, leading to the justification of the use of this scaling in certain regimes of the physical
parameters under consideration. For example, the bacterium E. coli on a Petri dish displays a
characteristic speed of $s\approx 10$ $\mu$ m/sec. and a mean run time of $1/\lambda\approx 1$ sec. (cf. [ 49 ] ). Under similar experimental conditions, strains of B. subtilis show characteristic
speeds of $s\approx 1$ $\mu$ m/sec. (cf. [ 29 ] ) with average run time of $1/\lambda\approx 1$ sec.
(cf. [ 30 ] ). Choosing a typical experimental length scale of $L=1$ mm. and a time scale of $T=10^{4}$ sec., we have that $s=O(\epsilon^{-1})$ and $\lambda=O(\epsilon^{-2})$ , with $\epsilon=10^{-2}$ for E.
coli and $\epsilon=10^{-1}$ for B. subtilis . Adopting the scaling ( 2.5 ) we have that, in both
cases, the assumption ( 2.6 ) holds for a small, non-dimensional parameter $\epsilon>0$ . Rewriting equation ( 2.4 ) in the new space and time coordinates, $(\xi,\tau)$ , we obtain $\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}p(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v})+\epsilon\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}p(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v})=-\lambda p(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v})+\int_{V}\lambda T(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})p(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\,d\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime},$ (2.7) where $(\xi,\tau)\in\widetilde{\Omega}\times(0,+\infty)$ , $\widetilde{\Omega}:=\epsilon\Omega=\{\epsilon x\,:\,x\in\Omega\}$ , $\boldsymbol{v}\in V$ . We are interested in the case where the velocity-jump process is influenced by the presence of an external
chemical field, whose concentration in the scaled variables will be denoted as $S=S(\xi,\tau)$ . Following [ 48 ] we propose a first order perturbation of a certain turning frequency $\lambda_{0}$ independent of $\boldsymbol{v}$ , together with an unperturbed turning kernel $T_{0}$ that depends only on $(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\in V\times V$ : $\displaystyle T$ $\displaystyle=T_{0}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}),$ (2.8) $\displaystyle\lambda(\boldsymbol{v},\hat{S})$ $\displaystyle=\lambda_{0}(\xi,\tau,\hat{S})+\epsilon\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{v},\hat{S}).$ Here the turning kernel is unperturbed from a normalized turning kernel $T_{0}:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}_{+}$ which is
assumed to satisfy assumptions ( T1 ) - ( T4 ), whereas $\lambda_{0}$ may depend on $(\xi,\tau)$ via
the marginal density $\rho$ and the chemical concentration $S$ . The important fact is that $\lambda_{0}$ does
not depend on $\boldsymbol{v}$ (it is constant with respect to the operator acting on $L^{2}(V)$ for each
fixed $(\xi,\tau)$ ). The perturbation $\lambda_{1}$ is assumed to be bounded. Thus, we denote ${\mathcal{L}}_{0}p(\boldsymbol{v}):=-\lambda_{0}p(\boldsymbol{v})+\lambda_{0}\int_{V}T_{0}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})p(\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\,d\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime},\qquad\quad{\mathcal{L}}_{0}:L^{2}(V)\to L^{2}(V),$ (2.9) an operator which belongs to the class of operators acting on $L^{2}(V)$ as described in Theorem 2.1 . Under our parabolic scaling, it is assumed that the population density underlies a regular perturbation
expansion of the form $p(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v})=p_{0}(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v})+\epsilon p_{1}(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v})+\epsilon^{2}p_{2}(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v})+O(\epsilon^{3}),$ (2.10) also called an outer or Hilbert expansion in the context of kinetic theory (cf. [ 17 , 24 , 37 ] ). Since the marginal density is independent of $\epsilon>0$ we may assume that $\int_{V}p_{j}(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v})\,d\boldsymbol{v}=0,$ (2.11) for all $j\geq 1$ and all $(\xi,\tau)\in\widetilde{\Omega}\times(0,+\infty)$ , without loss of
generality. 2.2. Interior parabolic limit In this section we derive a mean field equation for the leading order term in the Hilbert expansion
( 2.10 ) which has been reported in [ 48 ] . We include the details of such derivation for
completeness. Let us assume that $(\xi,\tau)\in\widetilde{\Omega}\times(0,+\infty)$ are fixed. Being $\widetilde{\Omega}$ an open set, this means that we do not consider boundary conditions for the moment. Upon
substitution of the expansions ( 2.10 ) and ( 2.8 ) into equation ( 2.7 ) we obtain a
hierarchy of equations by comparing terms of equal order in powers of $\epsilon$ . First, at leading order $O(1)$ we obtain the equation $0=-\lambda_{0}p_{0}(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v})+\lambda_{0}\int_{V}T_{0}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})p_{0}(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\,d\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}={\mathcal{L}}_{0}p_{0}(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v}).$ (2.12) In view of Theorem 2.1 , $p_{0}(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v})$ belongs to the kernel of ${\mathcal{L}}_{0}$ with dimension equal

the

unperturbed from a
normalized turning kernel $T_{0}$ which is a multiple of the identity, $T=T_{0}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{|V|},$ (3.1) corresponding to a uniform reorientation in velocity space. In this case, the turning kernel is unbiased and
it clearly satisfies assumptions ( T1 ) - ( T4 ). We also define the set $V$ of admissible velocities
to be $V:=s\mathbb{S}^{n-1}:=\{s\boldsymbol{v}\,:\,|\boldsymbol{v}|=1,\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\},$ (3.2) for a certain constant speed $s>0$ . This choice is tantamount to a uniform distribution of velocities in
all directions with constant magnitude $s>0$ . Clearly, $V$ is compact and symmetric with respect to the
origin. 3.1. Choice of the perturbed turning rate Regarding the turning frequency, we follow a suggestion by Othmer et al. [ 47 ] . They observed
that dispersal or aggregation can be modelled if one assumes that $\lambda$ depends explicitly on the
marginal density $\rho$ (see also [ 41 ] ). Aggregation effects, for instance, can be described if $\lambda$ is a decreasing function of $\rho$ , in which case the waiting time between jumps increases with the
density. Likewise, $\lambda$ may also depend on the chemical concentration and its gradient. Experiments
show, for instance, that the turning time is low when the concentration of the chemoattractant is low (see,
e.g., [ 42 ] in the case of B. subtilis and oxygen as chemoattractant 2 2 2 we are
unaware of similar experimental results for E. coli , for example, or for any other chemoattractant,
such as peptone (nutrient); still, we conjecture that this assumption is reasonable within certain regimes of
concentration. ). In view of the above observations, we propose the following space- and time-dependent
unperturbed turning rate in ( 2.8 ), $\lambda_{0}(\xi,\tau):=\frac{\mu_{0}}{\rho(\xi,\tau)S(\xi,\tau)},$ where $\mu_{0}>0$ is a constant with the appropriate units: for $\lambda_{0}$ to have frequency units
( $1/[T]$ ), $\mu_{0}$ must have units of square moles over time ( $[M]^{2}/[T]$ ), and it can be regarded as a
turning frequency per square unit mass. To encode the dependence of $\lambda$ on the
gradient of the chemical signal, we follow [ 48 ] and set the $O(\epsilon)$ -perturbation of the
turning rate to be $\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{v},\hat{S}):=\kappa(S)(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}S),$ (3.3) where $\kappa=\kappa(S)$ is a scalar function to be chosen (and interpreted) later. In this fashion, the
turning rate takes the following form, $\lambda(\boldsymbol{v},\hat{S})=\lambda_{0}(\xi,\tau)+\epsilon\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{v},\hat{S})=\frac{\mu_{0}}{\rho(\xi,\tau)S(\xi,\tau)}+\epsilon\kappa(S(\xi,\tau))\,(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}S(\xi,\tau)).$ (3.4) Remark 3.1 . The turning frequency considered here is of Schnitzer type [ 54 ] , $\lambda=\lambda_{0}+\epsilon\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{v})$ , in which the order $O(\epsilon)$ perturbation depends on the velocity, whereas $\lambda_{0}$ does
not. $\lambda_{0}$ and $\lambda_{1}$ , however, may depend on space and time (see also the Discussion section in [ 48 ] ). We assume that this dependence happens through the marginal population density $\rho$ and the
chemical concentration density $S$ . It is to be observed that our choice for such dependence, namely $\lambda_{0}\sim\rho^{-1}S^{-1}$ , is motivated by experimental observations which indicate that the running
time is low when the chemical concentration is low (cf. [ 42 ] ), and that $\lambda$ must be a
decreasing function of $\rho$ in order to model aggregation [ 41 , 47 ] . Moreover, notice that the
dependence of $\lambda$ on space and time occurs at the lowest order in $\epsilon$ , affecting the form of the
resulting chemotactic velocity. If the turning kernel $T=T_{0}$ is defined by ( 3.1 ) then the pseudo-inverse, $\mathcal{F}_{0}\,:\,\langle 1\rangle^{\perp}\subset L^{2}(V)\to L^{2}(V)$ , of the operator $\mathcal{L}_{0}$ defined in ( 2.9 )
reduces to multiplication by $-\lambda_{0}^{-1}$ (see [ 27 ] ). Therefore, the expression for the
diffusion tensor ( 2.21 ) reduces to $\mathbb{D}=-\frac{1}{|V|}\int_{V}\boldsymbol{v}\otimes({\mathcal{F}}_{0}\boldsymbol{v})\,d\boldsymbol{v}=\frac{\lambda_{0}^{-1}}{|V|}\int_{V}\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}\,d\boldsymbol{v}.$ Since $|V|=s^{n-1}|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|=:s^{n-1}\omega_{n}$ and $\int_{V}\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}\,d\boldsymbol{v}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}s^{2}\boldsymbol{\eta}\otimes\boldsymbol{\eta}s^{n-1}\,dS_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=s^{n+1}\frac{\omega_{n}}{n}\mathbb{I}_{n},$ then the (cross) diffusion tensor takes the form $\mathbb{D}=(\lambda_{0}s^{2}/n)\mathbb{I}_{n}$ , that is, $\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{D}(\rho,S)=\left(\frac{s^{2}}{\mu_{0}n}\right)\rho S\,\mathbb{I}_{n}.$ (3.5) In order to compute the chemotactic velocity ( 2.22 ), first we observe that the average bias vanishes, $\displaystyle\overline{\lambda_{1}}(\boldsymbol{v})=\int_{V}\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})T_{0}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\,d\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|V|}\int_{V}\kappa(S)(\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}S)\,d\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa(S)}{|V|}\left(\int_{V}\boldsymbol{v}\,d\boldsymbol{v}\right)\cdot\nabla_{\xi}S$ $\displaystyle=0,$ inasmuch as $V$ is symmetric with respect to the origin and, consequently, $\int_{V}\boldsymbol{v}\,d\boldsymbol{v}=0$ .
Therefore the chemotactic velocity ( 2.22 ) reduces to $\boldsymbol{w}_{c}=\frac{1}{|V|}\int_{V}\boldsymbol{v}{\mathcal{F}}_{0}\big(\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{v},\hat{S})\big)\,d\boldsymbol{v}=-\,\frac{\lambda_{0}^{-1}}{|V|}\int_{V}\boldsymbol{v}\kappa(S)(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}S)\,d\boldsymbol{v}=-\mathbb{X}\nabla_{\xi}S,$ where $\mathbb{X}:=\frac{\kappa(S)}{\lambda_{0}|V|}\int_{V}\boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{v}\,d\boldsymbol{v},$ (3.6) is the chemotactic sensitivity tensor . In view that $V=s\mathbb{S}^{n-1}$ we readily obtain $\mathbb{X}(\rho,S)=\left(\frac{s^{2}}{\mu_{0}n}\right)\kappa(S)\rho S\,\mathbb{I}_{n}.$ (3.7) Therefore, the chemotactic velocity takes the form $\boldsymbol{w}_{c}=-\left(\frac{s^{2}}{\mu_{0}n}\right)\kappa(S)\rho S\nabla_{\xi}S.$ (3.8) The scalar function $\kappa=\kappa(S)$ can be interpreted as the standard chemotactic sensitivity
function . If $\kappa<0$ then the chemotactic signal is attractive and the chemical is known as a
chemo-attractant. If $\kappa>0$ then the signal is repulsive and the substance is known as a
chemo-repellent. As expected, the attractive/repellent behaviour of the chemical signal can be modelled
by the choice of the sign of the function $\kappa$ . Notice that, from the form of ( 3.3 ) and since $\lambda_{1}$ has frequency units, the function $\kappa$ must have dimensions of $1/[M]$ ( $1/$ chemical
concentration moles). Upon substitution into the resulting equation ( 2.23 ) for $\bar{p}_{0}=|V|^{-1}\rho$ we arrive at the
following parabolic, cross-diffusion equation for the density function $\rho$ : $\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\tau}=\nabla_{\xi}\cdot\left(\Big(\frac{s^{2}}{\mu_{0}n}\Big)\,\rho\,S\,\nabla_{\xi}\rho\right)+\nabla_{\xi}\cdot\left(\Big(\frac{s^{2}}{\mu_{0}n}\Big)\,\rho^{2}\,S\,\kappa(S)\nabla_{\xi}S\right).$ (3.9) Remark 3.2 . It should be observed that the quantity $D(\rho,S):=\Big(\frac{s^{2}}{\mu_{0}n}\Big)\rho S,$ (3.10) has dimensions of square length over time ( $\,[L]^{2}/[T]$ ) and, thus, it may be interpreted as an effective
diffusion coefficient for the population density $\rho$ . Moreover, equation ( 3.9 ) underlies the
experimental (yet, phenomenological) observation by Ben-Jacob and his group [ 22 , 5 ] . Indeed,
if we define the chemotactic flux as $\boldsymbol{J}_{c}:=-\Big(\frac{s^{2}}{\mu_{0}n}\Big)\kappa(S)\rho^{2}S\nabla_{\xi}S,$ then it clearly follows the empirical rule ( 1.5 ), where the bacterial
response function $\zeta$ is proportional to the product of $\rho$ with the effective diffusion coefficient, $\zeta(\rho,S)=\Big(\frac{s^{2}}{\mu_{0}n}\Big)\rho^{2}S=\rho D(\rho,S).$ Finally, as to the chemotactic sensitivity function is concerned, $\kappa=\kappa(S)$ will be assumed to be
attractive ( $\kappa(S)<0$ ) and to follow the Lapidus-Schiller model or “receptor law” [ 36 ] , $\kappa(S)=-\,\frac{\chi_{0}K_{d}}{(K_{d}+S)^{2}},$ (3.11) where $\chi_{0}>0$ is a dimensionless constant measuring the strength of the chemotaxis, and $K_{d}>0$ is the
receptor-ligand binding dissociation constant, which has nutrient concentration units $[M]$ (it represents
the nutrient level needed for half receptor to be occupied), has a unique value that depends on the bacterial
strain and the chemical signal under consideration, and must be determined experimentally. The
receptor law was designed to fulfill experimental observations (see [ 43 ] ) which indicate that, for
very high concentrations of the chemical signal, the chemotactic response of bacteria vanishes due to
saturation of the receptors. We also recall that it has been reported from experiments (cf. Block et
al. [ 9 ] ) that the turning frequency depends on the rate of change of the fraction $S/(K_{d}+S)$ ,
and that is why the chemotactic sensitivity is encoded in the expression for the turning frequency. Upon substitution of the receptor law ( 3.11 ) into ( 3.9 ) we arrive at the following
cross-diffusion equation $\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\tau}=\nabla_{\xi}\cdot\left(\Big(\frac{s^{2}}{\mu_{0}n}\Big)\,\rho\,S\,\nabla_{\xi}\rho\right)-\nabla_{\xi}\cdot\left(\Big(\frac{s^{2}}{\mu_{0}n}\Big)\,\rho^{2}\,S\,\frac{\chi_{0}K_{d}}{(K_{d}+S)^{2}}\nabla_{\xi}S\right).$ (3.12) Finally, the boundary condition ( 2.27 ) takes the form $\Big(\rho S\nabla_{\xi}\rho-\rho^{2}S\frac{\chi_{0}K_{d}}{(K_{d}+S)^{2}}\nabla_{\xi}S\Big)\cdot\hat{\nu}(\xi)=0,\qquad\xi\in\partial\widetilde{\Omega},\;\tau>0.$ (3.13) 3.2. Equation for the chemical signal, reaction term and non-dimensionalization The pure movement velocity jump process expressed by equation ( 2.4 ) is Markovian, that is, the
probability that agents reach the position $x$ at time $t$ does not depend on previous times, $t-T$ , with $T>0$ . In other words, there is no history dependence. In our case, this property holds because the jumps
are governed by a Poisson process (see, e.g., Feller [ 19 ] ). Hence, it is legitimate to account for
population growth/production by simply adding a reaction function to the right hand side of equation
( 2.4 ). Here we adopt the kinetics considered by Kawasaki et al. [ 32 ] and by Leyva et al. [ 39 ] , for which the chemical signal is due to nutrient concentration. If $\widetilde{{\mathcal{G}}}(p,S)$ represents the consumption rate of the nutrient by the bacteria, then the growth rate
of the former is $\theta\widetilde{{\mathcal{G}}}(p,S)$ , where $\theta>0$ is a (dimensionless) conversion constant
or conversion rate of consumed nutrient to bacterial growth. This consumption rate function or kinetic
function is chosen to follow the Michaelis-Menten rule, $\widetilde{{\mathcal{G}}}(p,S)=\frac{kpS}{1+\gamma S},$ whereupon $k>0$ is the intrinsic consumption rate and $\gamma>0$ is a saturation constant ( $k/\gamma$ is
the maximum consumption rate by one single cell.) This function can be approximated by $\widetilde{{\mathcal{G}}}(p,S)=kpS,$ (3.14) for low nutrient concentrations (see [ 32 ] ). It measures the probability of encounters between
bacterial agents with nutrient molecules. We now make one further assumption, and suppose that the number of directional changes outnumber the
birth events , implying that the pure movement velocity jump process occurs on a much faster scale than the
production events due to kinetics (see, e.g., Hillen [ 25 ] ). Since $t$ is the fast time variable and $\tau$ denotes the slow time scale, the pure kinetics are governed by an equation of the form $p_{\tau}=\widetilde{{\mathcal{G}}}(p,S)$ , that is, $p_{t}=\epsilon^{2}\widetilde{{\mathcal{G}}}(p,S)$ . Hence we define, ${{\mathcal{G}}}(p,S):=\epsilon^{2}\widetilde{{\mathcal{G}}}(p,S)=\epsilon^{2}kpS,$ and the resulting scaled velocity jump process equation with reaction term reads $\displaystyle\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}p(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v})+\epsilon\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla_{\xi}p(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v})=$ $\displaystyle-\lambda p(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v})+\int_{V}\lambda T(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})p(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})\,d\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}+$ (3.15) $\displaystyle+\epsilon^{2}kp(\xi,\tau,\boldsymbol{v})S(\xi,\tau).$ It is to be observed that the kinetic term appears at order $O(\epsilon^{2})$ . If we perform the Hilbert
expansion approximation on equation ( 3.15 ) as in section 2.2 , the kinetic term shows up at
the right hand side of the order $O(\epsilon^{2})$ -equation ( 2.17 ), in the form $k\theta\bar{p}_{0}(\xi,\tau)S(\xi,\tau)$ . The solvability condition ( 2.18 ) is then modified to read $0=\int_{V}\Big[\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\bar{p}_{0}(\xi,\tau)+{\mathcal{R}}_{1}\big({\mathcal{F}}_{0}\big({\mathcal{R}}_{1}(\bar{p}_{0}(\xi,\tau))\big)\big)+k\theta\bar{p}_{0}(\xi,\tau)S(\xi,\tau)\Big]\,d\boldsymbol{v}.$ Since $\int_{V}k\theta\bar{p}_{0}(\xi,\tau)S(\xi,\tau)\,d\boldsymbol{v}=|V|k\theta\bar{p}_{0}(\xi,\tau)S(\xi,\tau)$ , then
the resulting parabolic limit equation ( 2.23 ) now incorporates the kinetic term as follows, $\frac{\partial\bar{p}_{0}}{\partial\tau}=\nabla_{\xi}\cdot\big(\mathbb{D}\,\nabla_{\xi}\bar{p}_{0}\big)-\nabla_{\xi}\cdot\big(\bar{p}_{0}\boldsymbol{w}_{c}\big)+k\theta\bar{p}_{0}S.$ Specialized to the choices of the turning frequency and kernel in ( 3.4 ) and ( 3.1 ), last
equation leads to the following bacterial density equation with kinetic term, $\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial\tau}=\nabla_{\xi}\cdot\Big(\sigma\rho\,S\,\nabla_{\xi}\rho\Big)-\,\nabla_{\xi}\cdot\left(\sigma\rho^{2}S\frac{\chi_{0}K_{d}}{(K_{d}+S)^{2}}\nabla_{\xi}S\right)+k\theta\rho S,$ (3.16) where $\sigma:=\frac{s^{2}}{\mu_{0}n}.$ (3.17) Remark 3.3 . If the medium is very hard then the typical velocity of agents $s$ is small, and the turning frequency
per square unit mass $\mu_{0}$ is large (in soft, non-resistant media the bacteria tend to continue with
same velocity in the absence of an external chemotactic signal). In this fashion, lower values of $\sigma$ indicate harder substrates. In the same fashion, since the boundary condition for $\bar{p}_{0}$ is determined by the $O(\epsilon)$ -no mass flux
condition for $p_{1}$ , the kinetic term plays no role and the boundary condition for $\rho$ is given by
( 3.13 ) (details are left to the reader). Finally, the dynamics of the chemical (or nutrient)
concentration $S=S(\xi,\tau)$ is governed by a standard reaction-diffusion equation (which is well-known to
derive as the diffusion limit of a stochastic positional jump process) of the form $\frac{\partial S}{\partial\tau}=D_{S}\Delta_{\xi}S-k\rho S,\qquad\xi\in\widetilde{\Omega},\;\tau>0,$ (3.18) and subject to Neumann (no-flux) boundary conditions, $\nabla_{\xi}S\cdot\hat{\nu}(\xi)=0,\qquad\xi\in\partial\widetilde{\Omega},\,\tau>0.$ (3.19) Here $D_{S}>0$ denotes the diffusion constant associated to the nutrient concentration and $-k\rho S$ is
the consumption rate of the nutrient by the bacteria. The system of equations is endowed with initial
conditions of the form $\rho(\xi,0)=\rho_{0}(\xi),\quad S(\xi,0)=S_{0}(\xi),\qquad\xi\in\widetilde{\Omega},$ (3.20) being $\rho_{0},S_{0}$ , known functions. The resulting system is given by equation ( 3.16 ) for the
bacterial density, equation ( 3.18 ) for the chemical signal, the boundary conditions ( 3.13 )
and ( 3.19 ), and the initial conditions ( 3.20 ). It is clear that we have arrived at system of
equations ( 1.1 ) - ( 1.3 ) (after relabelling the variables for the bacterial and chemical
concentration densities). For analytical and numerical purposes we perform a further non-dimensionalization procedure. We apply
the same transformation as in [ 39 ] , and define, with a slight abuse of notation, the non-dimensional
independent space and time variables, $x:=\left(\frac{\theta K_{d}k}{D_{S}}\right)^{1/2}\xi,\qquad t:=\theta kK_{d}\tau,$ the chemical and agent densities, $v:=\frac{S}{K_{d}},\qquad u:=\frac{\rho}{\theta K_{d}},$ as well as the non-dimensional constants $\sigma_{0}:=\left(\frac{\theta K_{d}^{2}}{D_{S}}\right)\sigma,\qquad\widetilde{s}:=\frac{s}{(\theta kK_{d}D_{S})^{1/2}}.$ Upon substitution we arrive at a system of equations for the rescaled bacterial density, $u=u(x,t)$ , and
the
nutrient concentration, $v=v(x,t)$ , namely, $\begin{aligned} u_{t}&=\nabla\cdot(\sigma_{0}uv\nabla u)-\nabla\cdot\left(\sigma_{0}\chi_{0}\frac{u^{2}v}{(1+v)^{2}}\nabla v\right)+uv,\\
v_{t}&=\Delta v-uv,\end{aligned}\qquad x\in\Omega,\;t>0,$ (3.21) with boundary conditions, $\begin{aligned} \left(uv\nabla u-\chi_{0}\frac{u^{2}v}{(1+v)^{2}}\nabla v\right)\cdot\hat{\nu}=0,\\
\nabla v\cdot\hat{\nu}=0,\end{aligned}\qquad x\in\partial\Omega,\;t>0,$ (3.22) and initial conditions $u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad v(x,0)=v_{0}(x),\qquad x\in\Omega,$ (3.23) where now $\Omega$ denotes the rescaled bounded, open domain in the new non-dimensional variables. Remark 3.4 . Under the non-dimensionalization procedure considered here, system ( 3.21 ) - ( 3.23 )
contains the minimum number of free physical parameters, which in our case reduce to the chemotactic
sensitivity $\chi_{0}>0$ , a dimensionless constant coefficient measuring the intensity of the chemotactic
signal, and $\sigma_{0}>0$ , a non-dimensional parameter measuring the hardness of the substrate (agar). As a final observation, we notice that the chemotactic sensitivity function may be chosen freely, depending on
the species of bacteria and of the chemical under consideration. Even in the case of a single species like B. subtilis one may consider (besides the Lapidus-Schiller receptor law) the classical
Keller-Segel model [ 34 , 35 ] , the Rivero-Tranquillo-Buettner-Lauffenberger (or RTBL) chemotactic
flux [ 52 ] , or the recently proposed finite range log-sensing model [ 42 ] . They all
appear indicated as the function $\kappa=\kappa(S)$ in both the density equation ( 3.12 ) and the
boundary conditions ( 3.13 ). After the same non-dimensionalization procedure the resulting system of
equations can be expressed more generally in terms of an arbitrary chemotactic sensitivity function $\chi=\chi(v)$ . Recall that, for the chemotactic flux of the form $\boldsymbol{J}_{c}=-\zeta(u,v)\chi(v)\nabla v$ to be attractive, one requires the chemotactic sensitivity function to be negative, $\chi(\cdot)<0$ ,
provided, of course, that $\zeta(\cdot)>0$ . Thus, one may change its sign in the expressions with no harm.

v_{t}&=\Delta v-uv,\end{aligned}\qquad x\in\Omega,\;t>0,$ (3.24) together with boundary and initial conditions of the form $\begin{aligned} \big(uv\nabla u-u^{2}v\chi(v)\nabla v\big)\cdot\hat{\nu}=0,\\
\nabla v\cdot\hat{\nu}=0,\end{aligned}\qquad x\in\partial\Omega,\;t>0,$ (3.25) and $u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),\quad v(x,0)=v_{0}(x),\qquad x\in\Omega,$ (3.26) respectively, constitute the formal parabolic limit when $\epsilon\to 0^{+}$ of the solutions to a velocity

the form $\epsilon^{2}q_{t}+\epsilon\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla q=-\lambda q+\int_{\overline{V}}\lambda T_{0}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime})q\,d\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}+\epsilon^{2}qv,$ (3.27) and the standard reaction diffusion equation for the (non-dimensional) chemical signal concentration $v=v(x,t)$ in ( 3.24 ), where $u(x,t)=\int_{\overline{V}}q(x,y,\boldsymbol{v})\,d\boldsymbol{v},$ is the zero velocity moment or marginal density. Here $\overline{V}=\widetilde{s}\,\mathbb{S}^{n-1}:=\{\widetilde{s}\,\boldsymbol{v}\,:\,|\boldsymbol{v}|=1,\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\},$ is the uniform set of non-dimensional velocities, the turning kernel and turning frequency admit asymptotic

function $\chi=\chi(v)$ is understood as the chemotactic sensitivity function, and it is positive

concentration,
and $u_{0}(x):=u(x,0)=\int_{\overline{V}}q(x,0,\boldsymbol{v})\,d\boldsymbol{v},$ is the initial distribution of agents for all possible velocities. 4. Discussion In this paper, a formal macroscopic limit (in the parabolic regime) of a velocity jump
process in order to arrive at systems of equations ( 1.1 ) - ( 1.3 ) was performed. The
system is endowed with a doubly degenerate cross-diffusion term and a nutrient taxis drift term. Both fluxes
(the diffusive and the chemotactic) are density-dependent, degenerate, and related to each other. The
derivation makes precise the microscopic interpretation of the phenomenological bacterial response function
introduced by Ben-Jacob and his group in terms of the turning frequency at the level of the kinetic transport
equation. The latter is supposed to be perturbed from a turning rate which does not depend on the velocity of
the cells, but does depend on the chemical concentration. The first order perturbation of the turning
frequency is assumed to be of Schnitzer type, that is, it depends on the velocity of the cells; in addition,
it encodes the response to the chemotactic signal as well. The turning kernel corresponds to a uniform
reorientation in velocity space. These choices for the turning frequency and kernel are compatible with the
observed frequency of tumbles as a function of time, position and direction, keeping both the run speed and
the distribution of changes in direction essentially constant. Moreover, the decreasing functional form of
the unperturbed turning frequency in terms of the chemical concentration obeys recent experimental
observations. It is also decreasing as a function of the bacterial density in order to model aggregation, as
suggested by Othmer et al. [ 47 ] . Its functional form is arbitrary and suitably chosen for our
needs, but it may be of course modified to fit other observed phenomena, or different responses in other
parameter regimes. In other words, the procedure presented here can be applied to more general situations. Up to our knowledge this is the first derivation of a doubly degenerate cross-diffusion model with
density-dependent (and related to the diffusion) chemotactic drift term from a velocity jump process.
This derivation is formal, but helpful to understand the interplay of the microscopic description of both
diffusion and taxis, as well as the main features of the mean field model. From the mathematical viewpoint,
systems of the form ( 1.1 ) pose technical challenges which are of interest to the community working
on partial differential equations, such as the existence and regularity of global solutions (for recent
advances, see [ 15 , 60 , 59 , 61 ] ). Another open mathematical question is the rigorous
scaling limit, in the spirit of the analysis by Chalub et al. [ 12 , 11 ] , of the solutions
to the transport equation converging to the weak solutions of the macroscopic diffusion-drift system
recently obtained in [ 60 ] . This is an interesting topic for further investigation. Finally, from
the biological viewpoint, it is of course of interest to explore the consequences of allowing turning
frequencies to depend on density distributions in order to incorporate more complex spatial and temporal
dependencies of microscopic quantities into models of related phenomena, such as macromolecular crowding [ 16 ] , aggregation [ 41 ] , or microscopic exclusion processes of intracellular transport [ 20 ] , just to mention a few.
