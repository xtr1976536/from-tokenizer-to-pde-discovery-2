Yamazoe) Date: August 24, 2026 Abstract. We study bifurcations and spectral stability of solitary waves in coupled nonlinear Schrödinger (CNLS) equations on the line.
We assume that the coupled equations possess a solution of which one component is identically zero, and call it a fundamental solitary wave .
By using a result of one of the authors and his collaborator, pitchfork bifurcations of the fundamental solitary wave are detected.
We utilize the Hamiltonian-Krein index theory and Evans function technique to determine the spectral or orbital stability of the bifurcated solitary waves as well as that of the fundamental one under some nondegenerate conditions which are easy to verify, compared with those of the previous results.
We apply our theory to CNLS equations with a cubic nonlinearity and give numerical evidences for the theoretical results. Key words and phrases: bifurcation, coupled nonlinear Schrödinger equations, solitary wave, spectral stability 2010 Mathematics Subject Classification 35B32, 35B35, 35Q55, 37K45, 37K50 1. Introduction We consider coupled nonlinear Schrödinger (CNLS) equations of the form $\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}^{2}u+\partial_{1}F(|u|^{2},|v|^{2};\mu)u=0,\\
&\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}v+\partial_{x}^{2}v+\partial_{2}F(|u|^{2},|v|^{2};\mu)v=0,\end{aligned}$ (1.1) where $(u,v)=(u(t,x),v(t,x))$ are complex-valued unknown functions of $(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$ , $\mu$ is a real parameter, $F:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is a given function, and $\partial_{j}F$ represents the derivative of $F$ with respect to the $j$ -th argument for $j=1,2$ .
Our special attention is paid to the cubic nonlinearity, $\displaystyle F(\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2};\beta_{1})=\frac{1}{2}\zeta_{1}^{2}+\beta_{1}\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}+\frac{\beta_{2}}{2}\zeta_{2}^{2},$ (1.2) where $\beta_{1},\beta_{2}$ are real constants and $\beta_{1}$ is taken as the control parameter $\mu$ below.
In applications, ( 1.1 ) arises when considering propagation of two interacted waves in nonlinear media.
For example, in nonlinear optics, it describes nonlinear pulse propagation in birefringent fibers.
See [ 2 , 27 , 34 , 39 ] and references therein.
( 1.1 ) has a Hamiltonian structure with the Hamiltonian $\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(u,v;\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Bigl(|\partial_{x}u(x)|^{2}+|\partial_{x}v(x)|^{2}-F(|u|^{2},|v|^{2};\mu)\Bigr)\mathrm{d}x,$ and the Cauchy problem of ( 1.1 ) is well-posed in the Sobolev space $H^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{2})$ (see, e.g., Section 4 of [ 7 ] ). Here we are interested in solitary waves of the form $\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &u(t,x)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\omega t+cx-c^{2}t+\theta)}U(x-2ct-x_{0}),\\
&v(t,x)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(st+cx-c^{2}t+\phi)}V(x-2ct-x_{0}),\end{aligned}$ (1.3) such that the real-valued functions $(U,V)=(U(x),V(x))$ satisfy $U(x),V(x)\to 0$ as $x\to\pm\infty$ ,
where $c$ , $x_{0}$ , $\theta$ , $\phi$ , and $\omega,s>0$ are real constants.
Henceforth, without loss of generality, we take $c,x_{0},\theta,\phi=0$ by scaling the independent variables if necessary, since ( 1.1 ) is invariant under the Galilean transformations $\displaystyle(u(t,x),v(t,x))\mapsto\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(cx-c^{2}t)}(u(t,x-2ct),v(t,x-2ct)),\quad c\in\mathbb{R},$ the spatial translations $\displaystyle(u(t,x),v(t,x))\mapsto(u(t,x-x_{0}),v(t,x-x_{0})),\quad x_{0}\in\mathbb{R},$ (1.4) and the gauge transformations $\displaystyle(u(t,x),v(t,x))\mapsto(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}u(t,x),\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}v(t,x)),\quad\theta,\phi\in\mathbb{R}.$ (1.5) So $(U,V)=(U(x),V(x))$ solves $\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &-U^{\prime\prime}+\omega U-\partial_{1}F(U^{2},V^{2};\mu)U=0,\\
&-V^{\prime\prime}+sV-\partial_{2}F(U^{2},V^{2};\mu)V=0,\end{aligned}$ (1.6) where the prime represents the differentiation with respect to $x$ .
Throughout this paper we assume that ( 1.6 ) possess a homoclinic solution of the form $(U,V)=(U_{0}(x),0)$ .
This assumption holds with $U_{0}(x)=\sqrt{2\omega}\sech(\sqrt{\omega}x)$ for the particular nonlinearity ( 1.2 ).
We refer to the solitary wave of the form $\displaystyle(u(t,x),v(t,x))=(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}U_{0}(x),0),$ (1.7) as a fundamental solitary wave .
Obviously, $(U,V)=(-U_{0}(x),0)$ is also a homoclinic solution to ( 1.6 ) but it gives the same solitary wave ( 1.3 ) as ( 1.7 ) if $\theta=\pi$ is taken.
So we only treat ( 1.7 ). Blázquez-Sanz and Yagasaki [ 6 ] studied bifurcations of homoclinic orbits in a large class of ordinary differential equations using Gruendler’s version of Melnikov’s method [ 17 ] , and applied their theory to show that infinitely many pitchfork bifurcations of $(U,V)=(U_{0}(x),0)$ occur in ( 1.6 ) with ( 1.2 ) when $\beta_{1}$ is taken as a control parameter.
Moreover, in a general situation, they proved that if bifurcations of homoclinic orbits occur, then the associated variational equations, i.e., linearized equations, around the homoclinic orbits are integrable in the meaning of differential Galois theory [ 32 ] .
Homoclinic orbits born at the pitchfork bifurcations in ( 1.6 ) correspond to solitary waves of the form ( 1.3 ) with nonzero $U$ - and $V$ -components, which are often called vector solitons, bifurcated from the fundamental solitary waves ( 1.7 ) in ( 1.1 ). Once establishing their existence, the stability problem of these bifurcated solitary waves naturally arises.
Ohta [ 28 ] proved the orbital stability of the fundamental solitary waves in ( 1.1 ) with ( 1.2 ) and $\beta_{2}=1$ .
Subsequently, his result was extended to more general nonlinearity cases by several authors [ 5 , 8 , 25 , 26 , 27 ] .
All of these results are based on variational characterizations of solitary waves.
On the other hand, the spectral stability of vector solitons in ( 1.1 ) with ( 1.2 ) or the saturable nonlinearity was discussed by Pelinovsky and Yang [ 31 ] .
They employed the Hamiltonian-Krein index theory, which was established by Pelinovsky [ 30 ] and Kapitula et al. [ 18 , 19 ] independently, and carried out some formal calculations to claim that any purely imaginary embedded eigenvalue with a positive (resp. negative) Krein signature disappears (resp. changes to a pair of eigenvalues with nonzero real parts) after bifurcations.
This suggests that the bifurcated solitary waves of ( 1.1 ) with ( 1.2 ) are spectrally stable and unstable, respectively, when they have positive and sign-changing $V$ -components.
A rigorous analysis was given by Cuccagna et al. [ 10 ] using a technique based on the analytic continuation of the resolvent.
Moreover, some nondegenerate conditions which are generically satisfied but difficult to verify for concrete examples were assumed there. Li and Promislow [ 24 ] also studied the spectral stability of solitary waves in CNLS equations with a different special nonlinearity, using the Evans function technique.
This technique was first proposed in Evans’ earlier works about the stability of pulse solutions in nerve axon equations [ 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 ] , and subsequently discussed in a topological framework by Alexander et al. [ 3 ] . In this paper, we study pitchfork bifurcations of the fundamental solitary waves detected by the Melnikov analysis of [ 6 ] in ( 1.1 ) and determine the spectral and/or orbital stability of the fundamental and bifurcated solitary waves under some nondegenerate conditions which are easy to verify, compared with those of [ 10 , 31 ] .
Here the terminology “pitchfork bifurcation” is used with caution:
a pair of homoclinic solutions to ( 1.6 ),
which correspond to the same family of solitary waves of the form ( 1.3 )
in ( 1.1 ), are born at the bifurcation point.
Our main tool to determine their spectral stability
is the Evans function technique as in [ 24 ] but more careful treatments are required.
The Hamiltonian-Krein index theory is also used as in [ 31 ] .
We apply our theory to the particular nonlinearity case of ( 1.2 ):
pitchfork bifurcations of the fundamental solitary waves are detected
and the spectral or orbital stability of the fundamental and bifurcated solitary waves
are determined for almost all parameter values.
In particular, the nondegenerate conditions for bifurcations and spectral stability are given explicitly.
The theoretical results are also demonstrated with numerical computations
obtained by the computer tool AUTO [ 11 ] . The outline of this paper is as follows:
In Section 2, we state our assumptions and main results
on pitchfork bifurcations of the fundamental solitary waves in ( 1.1 ).
In Section 3, we use the Melnikov analysis of [ 6 ] for ( 1.6 )
and prove the main result on pitchfork bifurcations.
For the reader’s convenience, in our setting,
the Melnikov analysis is briefly reviewed. In Section 4, we present the Hamiltonian-Krein index theory and Evans function technique
in appropriate forms.
Applying these approaches,
we prove the main results on the stability of the fundamental and bifurcated solitary waves
in Sections 5 and 6, respectively.
Finally, we illustrate our theory for the particular nonlinearity ( 1.2 )
along with some numerical computations in Section 7. 2. Main Results In this section, we give our assumptions and main results
on bifurcations of the fundamental solitary wave ( 1.7 )
and the stability of the fundamental and bifurcated solitary waves
in the CNLS equations ( 1.1 ). 2.1. Bifurcations of the Fundamental Solitary Wave We first make the following assumptions. (A1) $F:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is a $C^{3}$ function satisfying $\displaystyle F(0,0;\mu)=\partial_{1}F(0,0;\mu)=\partial_{2}F(0,0;\mu)=0,\quad\partial_{\mu}F(\zeta,0;\mu)=0,$ for any $\zeta,\mu\in\mathbb{R}$ .
Henceforth we suppress the dependence of $\partial_{1}^{j}F(\zeta,0;\mu)$ on $\mu$ and write $\partial_{1}^{j}F(\zeta,0)$ for $j=0,1,2,3$ . (A2) When $V=0$ , the first equation of ( 1.6 ), $\displaystyle-U^{\prime\prime}+\omega U-\partial_{1}F(U^{2},0)U=0,$ has a homoclinic solution $U_{0}(x)\not\equiv 0$ such that $\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}U_{0}(x)=0$ and $U_{0}^{\prime}(0)=0$ . (A3) For some $\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{R}$ , the normal variational equation of ( 1.6 )
around the homoclinic orbit $(U,V)=(U_{0}(x),0)$ , $\displaystyle-\delta V^{\prime\prime}+s\delta V-\partial_{2}F(U_{0}(x)^{2},0;\mu_{0})\delta V=0,$ (2.1) has a nonzero bounded solution $\delta V=V_{1}(x)$ such that $\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}V_{1}(x)=0$ .
Henceforth we take $\mu_{0}=0$ by shifting the value of $\mu$ if necessary without loss of generality. Remark 2.1 . (i) (A2) holds if and only if $\displaystyle\zeta_{0}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\inf\{\zeta>0\mid F(\zeta^{2},0)=\omega\zeta^{2}\}>0$ exists and $\partial_{1}F(\zeta_{0}^{2},0)>\omega$ .
In addition, $U_{0}(x)$ decays exponentially as $x\to\pm\infty$ and is unique up to translations.
We can also take $U_{0}(x)$ such that it is a positive even function and satisfies $U_{0}(0)=\zeta_{0}$ and $U_{0}^{\prime}(x)<0$ for $x>0$ .
See Section 6 of [ 4 ] .
Henceforth, we always assume these properties for $U_{0}(x)$ . (ii) In (A3) , $V_{1}(x)$ is either even or odd.
Actually, when (A3) holds, since the space of all solutions to ( 2.1 ) decaying as $x\to\pm\infty$ is of dimension one and $V_{1}(-x)$ is such a solution to ( 2.1 ),
we have $V_{1}(x)=cV_{1}(-x)$ for some $c\in\mathbb{R}$ .
Hence, if $V_{1}(0)\neq 0$ , then $c=1$ .
Otherwise we have $c=-1$ since $V_{1}^{\prime}(x)=-cV_{1}^{\prime}(-x)$ and $V_{1}^{\prime}(0)\neq 0$ . Define the two integrals $\displaystyle\bar{a}_{2}=$ $\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\partial_{2}\partial_{\mu}F(U_{0}(x)^{2},0;0)V_{1}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,$ (2.2) $\displaystyle\bar{b}_{2}=$ $\displaystyle-2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\partial_{1}\partial_{2}F(U_{0}(x)^{2},0;0)U_{0}(x)U_{2}(x)V_{1}(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x$ $\displaystyle-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\partial_{2}^{2}F(U_{0}(x)^{2},0;0)V_{1}(x)^{4}\,\mathrm{d}x,$ (2.3) where $\displaystyle U_{2}(x)=$ $\displaystyle-\phi_{11}(x)\int_{x}^{\infty}\phi_{12}(y)\partial_{1}\partial_{2}F(U_{0}(y)^{2},0;0)U_{0}(y)V_{1}(y)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y$ $\displaystyle-\phi_{12}(x)\int_{0}^{x}\phi_{11}(y)\partial_{1}\partial_{2}F(U_{0}(y)^{2},0;0)U_{0}(y)V_{1}(y)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y.$ (2.4) Applying the Melnikov analysis of [ 6 ] reviewed in Section 3.1, we have the following bifurcation result. Theorem 2.2 . Suppose that (A1) - (A3) and $\bar{a}_{2},\bar{b}_{2}\neq 0$ hold.
Then a pitchfork bifurcation of the fundamental solitary wave ( 1.7 ) occurs at $\mu=0$ .
In addition, it is supercritical or subcritical, depending on whether $\bar{a}_{2}\bar{b}_{2}<0$ or $>0$ .
Moreover, the bifurcated solitary waves are written as $\displaystyle(u(t,x),v(t,x))=(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}U_{\varepsilon}(x),\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}st}V_{\varepsilon}(x))$ (2.5) with $\displaystyle U_{\varepsilon}(x)=U_{0}(x)+\varepsilon^{2}U_{2}(x)+O(\varepsilon^{4}),\quad V_{\varepsilon}(x)=\pm(\varepsilon V_{1}(x)+O(\varepsilon^{3})),$ (2.6) where $\varepsilon>0$ is a small parameter such that $\displaystyle\mu=\bar{\mu}\,\varepsilon^{2},\qquad\bar{\mu}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=-{\bar{b}_{2}}/{\bar{a}_{2}}.$ (2.7) A proof of Theorem 2.2 is given in Section 3. 2.2. Stability of the Fundamental and Bifurcated Solitary Waves We turn to the stability of the fundamental and bifurcated solitary waves.
We begin with the definition of orbital stability. Definition 2.3 . The solitary wave ( 2.8 ) is orbitally stable if for any $\varepsilon>0$ there exists a constant $\delta>0$ such that for all $(u_{0},v_{0})\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{2})$ with $\displaystyle\inf_{x_{0},\theta,\phi\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl(\|u_{0}-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}U(\cdot-x_{0})\|_{H^{1}}+\|v_{0}-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}V(\cdot-x_{0})\|_{H^{1}}\bigr)<\delta,$ the solution $(u(t),v(t))\in C(\mathbb{R},H^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{2}))$ to ( 1.1 ) with $(u(0),v(0))=(u_{0},v_{0})$ satisfies $\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\inf_{x_{0},\theta,\phi\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl(\|u(t)-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}U(\cdot-x_{0})\|_{H^{1}}+\|v(t)-\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}V(\cdot-x_{0})\|_{H^{1}}\bigr)<\varepsilon.$ Otherwise, it is orbitally unstable . The linearized operator $\mathscr{L}:H^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{4})$ $\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{4})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{4})$ around the solitary wave $\displaystyle(u(t,x),v(t,x))=(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}U(x),\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}st}V(x))$ (2.8) is given by $\displaystyle\mathscr{L}=-\mathrm{i}\Sigma_{3}\left[-\partial_{x}^{2}I_{4}+\begin{pmatrix}\omega I_{2}&O_{2}\\
O_{2}&sI_{2}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}B_{11}(x)&B_{12}(x)\\
B_{21}(x)&B_{22}(x)\end{pmatrix}\right]$ (2.9) where $I_{n}$ and $O_{n}$ , respectively, represents the $n\times n$ identity and zero matrices,
and $\displaystyle\sigma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad\Sigma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{3}&O_{2}\\
O_{2}&\sigma_{3}\end{pmatrix},$ $\displaystyle B_{11}(x)=\partial_{1}^{2}F(U(x)^{2},V(x)^{2})U(x)^{2}(I_{2}+\sigma_{1})+\partial_{1}F(U(x)^{2},V(x)^{2})I_{2},$ $\displaystyle B_{12}(x)=B_{21}(x)=\partial_{1}\partial_{2}F(U(x)^{2},V(x)^{2})U(x)V(x)(I_{2}+\sigma_{1}),$ $\displaystyle B_{22}(x)=\partial_{2}^{2}F(U(x)^{2},V(x)^{2})V(x)^{2}(I_{2}+\sigma_{1})+\partial_{2}F(U(x)^{2},V(x)^{2})I_{2}.$ See Section 4.1 for the derivation of $\mathscr{L}$ .
We easily show that the essential spectrum of $\mathscr{L}$ is given by $\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(\mathscr{L})=\mathrm{i}(-\infty,-\min\{\omega,s\}]\cup\mathrm{i}[\min\{\omega,s\},\infty).$ Moreover, $\sigma(\mathscr{L})$ is symmetric with respect to the real and imaginary axis: if $\lambda\in\sigma(\mathscr{L})$ , then $-\lambda,\pm\lambda^{*}\in\sigma(\mathscr{L})$ ,
where the superscript ‘ $\ast$ ’ represents the complex conjugate. Definition 2.4 . The solitary wave ( 2.8 ) in ( 1.1 ) is spectrally stable if the spectrum $\sigma(\mathscr{L})$ of $\mathscr{L}$ is contained in the closed left half plane.
Otherwise, it is spectrally unstable . For the fundamental and bifurcated solitary waves,
we have the following theorems. Theorem 2.5 . For any $\mu\in\mathbb{R}$ , the fundamental solitary wave ( 1.7 ) is orbitally and spectrally stable if $\partial_{\omega}\|U_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}>0$ , and it is orbitally and spectrally unstable if $\partial_{\omega}\|U_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}<0$ . Theorem 2.6 . Let $\ell$ be the number of zeros of $V_{1}(x)$ .
For $\varepsilon>0$ sufficiently small, the bifurcated solitary waves ( 2.5 ) are orbitally and spectrally stable if $\partial_{\omega}\|U_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}>0$ and $\ell=0$ , and it is orbitally and spectrally unstable if $\partial_{\omega}\|U_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}<0$ . Proofs of Theorem 2.5 and 2.6 are given in Sections 5 and 6.1, respectively.
Note that Theorem 2.6 says nothing about the stability of the bifurcated solitary waves with $\ell>0$ when $\partial_{\omega}\|U_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}>0$ .
We assume that $\partial_{\omega}\|U_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}>0$ and discuss the stability of bifurcated solitary waves in the rest of this section. Let $\mathscr{L}_{0}$ and $\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}$ , respectively,
denote the linearized operator around the fundamental and bifurcated solitary waves.
To determine the spectral stability of the bifurcated solitary waves,
we need to discuss how purely imaginary eigenvalues of $\mathscr{L}_{0}$ change
when $\varepsilon>0$ .
Let $E(\lambda,\varepsilon)$ denote the Evans function for $\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}$ ,
which allows us to describe the changes of these eigenvalues for $\varepsilon>0$ .
See Section 4.3 for its definition.
Especially, a zero of $E(\lambda,\varepsilon)$ is typically an eigenvalue
of $\mathscr{L}_{\varepsilon}$ but is not necessarily so,
and it is called a resonance pole if not (e.g., Chapter 9 of [ 20 ] ).
We also see that the Evans function $E(\lambda,\varepsilon)$ may not be analytic at $\lambda\in\{\pm\mathrm{i}\omega,\pm\mathrm{i}s\}$ ,
which is a branch point for a square root function
in ( 2.14 ) or ( 2.17 ) below (see also Section 4.3)
and at which the corresponding Jost solutions may lose their analyticity in $\lambda$ .

where the upper or lower sign is taken simultaneously,
and refer to $\widetilde{E}(\gamma,\varepsilon)$ as the extended Evans function .
See Sections 4.3 and 4.4 for more details. Here we compute the Evans function $E(\lambda,0)$ for $\mathscr{L}_{0}$ .
