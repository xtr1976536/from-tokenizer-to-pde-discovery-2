The term cross diffusion is used when in a system of parabolic PDEs, at least one of the diffusion rates in the equation for one component depends on the value of one or more of the other components. We focus here on the case when the system can be written under the form $\left\{\begin{lgathered}\partial_{t}u-\Delta\big[(d_{1}+p(u,v))u\big]=0,\\
\partial_{t}v-\Delta\big[(d_{2}+q(u,v))v\big]=0,\end{lgathered}\right.$ (1) where $d_{1},d_{2}>0$ are two constants, and $p,q$ are two elements of $\mathbf{R}_{+}[X,Y]$ (polynomials of two variables with real nonnegative coefficients) vanishing at $(0,0)$ , and we consider periodic boundary conditions, that is the system is considered on the torus $\mathbf{T}^{d}$ . It can be checked, at least formally, that a solution of these systems remain nonnegative for all $t\geq 0$ whenever it is initially nonnegative. In the sequel of this paper, we systematically denote $U:=(u,v)$ . Note that the classical Shigesada-Kawasaki-Teramoto (called SKT in the rest of the paper) system (see [ 27 ] ), where $p$ and $q$ are linear w.r.t $u,v$ , enters in this category (if the zero-th order competition terms are dropped). Many natural extensions of this system where $p,q$ are not linear any more also enter this category. In particular, such solutions are unique (among those satisfying the assumptions required in Theorem 2 ). SKT-like systems have been studied for a long time. As they were originally introduced in [ 27 ] for the emergence of patterns formation, the first articles focused on their stationary solutions, see [ 21 , 22 , 28 ] for instance. Existence of solutions for these systems has been the source of a large literature which interestingly enough only started 5 years after the publication of [ 27 ] . Indeed, the first existence result seems to be the one of Kim in [ 17 ] , which established that there are global (strong) solutions but only for equal diffusion rates, in dimension $1$ . After several partial results of this kind, an important step was made by Amann [ 1 , 2 ] , paving the way for a systematic local existence theory (with a blowup criterion) under a mild elliptic condition on the system (also known as « normal ellipticity », that is Petrovskii’s condition for parabolic systems). In the wake of Amann’s theory, a substantial number of articles attempted to prove the existence of global strong solutions of SKT-like systems through a uniform control of Amann’s blowup criterion, generally at the price of restrictive constraints on the system. For instance, in the so-called triangular case, that is when cross diffusion is present only in one of the two equations of the system, Lou, Ni and Wu obtained existence and regularity in dimension $2$ in [ 20 ] , whereas later Choi, Lui and Yamada first got rid of the restriction on the dimension in [ 26 ] , at the price of considering small cross diffusion rates. More recently, [ 14 ] established the existence of global smooth solutions for triangular SKT-like systems when some self diffusion is present, and [ 13 ] proved the same kind of results for natural extensions of triangular SKT-like systems. In the general (non triangular) case, global weak solutions of the SKT system were proven to exist in full generality in [ 6 ] ; a few years later [ 9 , 10 ] , similar global solutions were built for some systems having a structure like ( 1 ), with possibly non-polynomial diffusion rates (see also [ 19 , 7 , 8 ] for multi-species cases). One common aspect of all these works is that one assumes the existence of an entropy structure for the studied system, which gives rise to a convex Lyapunov functional. Its dissipation allows a control of the gradients of the solutions. Be it for local, global, weak or strong solutions, in all the previous results, the considered systems always satisfy the normal ellipticity condition. Articles dealing with parabolic systems potentially failing to satisfy this condition are less frequent in the literature (see [ 12 , 29 ] for recent examples). In a perturbative setting, it was first noticed in [ 5 ] (in dimension $1$ ), that one can obtain global strong solutions, when the initial data are small. There, the very same energy estimate that one wishes to obtain thanks to some uniform bound can precisely be used to prove those uniform bounds ; this is due to the embedding $\textnormal{H}^{1}(\mathbf{T})\hookrightarrow\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\mathbf{T})$ and it allows to prove Theorem 2.1 in [ 5 ] that we reproduce here in the specific case of (conservative) system ( 1 ) for the sake of clarity (we use the notation $\lesssim$ for “less than a constant times” here and in the rest of the paper). Theorem 3 . (consequence of [ 5 , Theorem 2.1] )
In dimension $d=1$ , there exists $\delta>0$ such that, for any smooth initial data $U_{\mathrm{in}}:=(u_{\mathrm{in}},v_{\mathrm{in}})$ satisfying $\|U_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{\infty}+\|\nabla U_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{2}\leq\delta$ , any smooth solution $U:=(u,v)$ to system ( 1 ) defined on $\mathbf{R}_{+}\times\mathbf{T}^{d}$ (initialized by $U_{in}$ ) satisfies $\displaystyle\sup_{t\geq 0}\|U(t)\|_{\infty}\lesssim\delta,\qquad\sup_{t\geq 0}\|\nabla U(t)\|_{2}\lesssim\delta.$ Remark 3 . This estimate allows to build global uniformly bounded solutions for all times, after introducing an adequate approximate scheme. Note that our Theorem 1 can be seen as an extension of Theorem 3 in the case of dimension $d\geq 1$ , since it is possible to take $k=2$ when $d=1$ , so that the norms used in the two theorems become equivalent. We explain here how our result could probably be generalized. — First, the fact that $p,q$ are polynomials vanishing at point $(0,0)$ with nonnegative coefficients is not essential to our analysis (though it helps greatly in the presentation of the results). It is indeed sufficient to suppose that $p,q$ are nonnegative functions of class $\mathscr{C}^{1}((\mathbf{R}_{+})^{2})$ which vanish at point $(0,0)$ (note that the explicit estimate of stability has then to be slightly changed). — Secondly, we think that our analysis could be reproduced, without much change, when systems of more than two equations are considered
(in this direction, see for instance [ 8 ] for the existence of global weak solutions for multi-species system). — Finally, we insist that our methods cannot work (at least when one looks for global in time solutions) if reaction terms are introduced in the cross diffusion systems, except in very specific cases. For example, if one considers competition terms of Lotka-Volterra type (such as they naturally appear in SKT-type models), one cannot hope to have solutions which remain small when the time becomes large, except if the coefficients appearing in the competition terms are such that associated steady solutions are themselves small. We however think that our result can be extended in at least one general situation, namely when the reaction terms are respectively of the form $-u\,s_{1}(u,v)$ , $-v\,s_{2}(u,v)$ , where $s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbf{R}_{+}[X,Y]$ . Such a form can correspond for example, in terms of modeling, to situations in population dynamics in which no new individuals are introduced (no births). Let us first notice that when no Lyapunov functional is known for the system (apart from obvious ones, such as the $\textnormal{L}^{1}$ norm of the two equations), no source of strong compactness for sequences of (approximated) solutions seems to appear (at least without further assumptions on $p,q$ ), since the usual a priori estimates performed on classical (non cross) diffusion systems do not seem to hold. Let us focus on a more favorable case, that is when some Lyapunov functional (also called energy in this context) is known to exist. When it is convex, we recall that the existence of weak solutions can often be obtained [ 6 , 9 , 10 , 19 ] . However, there are cases when an energy exists,
but is not convex. Such a situation has indeed been pointed out in [ 5 ] , where the following example is presented: $\left\{\begin{lgathered}\partial_{t}u-\Delta\big[(1+v^{2})u\big]=0,\\
\partial_{t}v-\Delta\big[(1+u^{2})v\big]=0.\end{lgathered}\right.$ (18) One checks indeed that the space integral of $(1+u^{2})(1+v^{2})$ (we consider here periodic boundary conditions) decays along time.
More precisely, one can check that for any smooth solution, the following bound holds: $\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbf{T}^{d}}(1+u^{2})(1+v^{2})=-\int_{\mathbf{T}^{d}}\Big[|\nabla(u(1+v^{2}))|^{2}+|\nabla(v(1+u^{2}))|^{2}\Big]\leq 0.$ (19) This dissipation of the energy controls in this situation only a part of the gradients of the solution. To highlight this lack of control of the whole gradients,
we present a short proposition (in which we omit the time variable) which shows the type of pathologies which could prevent strong compactness. Proposition 3 . There exist two sequences of functions $(u_{n})_{n}$ and $(v_{n})_{n}$ taking positive values on $\mathbf{T}^{d}$ such that : $\bullet$ $(u_{n})_{n}$ and $(v_{n})_{n}$ are bounded in $\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\mathbf{T}^{d})$ ; $\bullet$ $(u_{n}(1+v_{n}^{2}))_{n}$ and $(v_{n}(1+u_{n}^{2}))_{n}$ are bounded in $\textnormal{H}^{1}(\mathbf{T}^{d})$ ; $\bullet$ neither $(u_{n})_{n}$ nor $(v_{n})_{n}$ is relatively compact in $\textnormal{L}^{2}(\mathbf{T}^{d})$ . Proof. The polynomial $\displaystyle P(X)$ $\displaystyle:=(X^{2}-3X+1)(X^{3}+X-3)$ $\displaystyle=(X^{2}+1)^{2}X-3(X^{2}+1)^{2}+9X$ admits (at least) two distinct positive roots: $r_{1}<r_{2}$ . Consider the function $\psi:=\mathbf{1}_{(0,1/2)}-\mathbf{1}_{(1/2,1)}$ . Its $1$ -periodization $\Psi:=\sum_{k\in\mathbf{Z}}\tau_{k}\psi$ is constant on each half-integer interval $(k,k+1)/2$ for $k\in\mathbf{Z}$ , alternates values $1$ and $-1$ on those, so it has a vanishing integral on each integer interval $(k,k+1)$ . Define for $n\in\mathbf{N}^{\star}$ the function $\Psi_{n}:x\mapsto\Psi(2^{n}x)$ which is now constant on each dyadic interval $(k,k+1)/2^{n+1}$ and alternates values $1$ and $-1$ on those. In particular, for $m<n$ , $\Psi_{n}$ has a vanishing integral on each dyadic interval $(k,k+1)/2^{m+1}$ . We claim that $(\Psi_{n})_{n}$ (restricted to $(0,1)$ ) is an orthonormal family in $\textnormal{L}^{2}(0,1)$ : each function is certainly of norm $1$ (since its takes $\pm 1$ as value), and if $m<n$ , we can (essentially) cover $(0,1)$ by a finite family $\mathscr{F}$ of dyadic intervals $(k,k+1)/2^{m+1}$ , so that $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\Phi_{n}\Phi_{m}=\sum_{\textnormal{I}\in\mathscr{F}}\int_{\textnormal{I}}\Phi_{n}\Phi_{m},$ and as noticed before, on each element of $\mathscr{F}$ , the function $\Phi_{m}$ is constant (by definition),
whereas $\Phi_{n}$ has a vanishing integral : we have $\Phi_{n}\perp\Phi_{m}$ in $\textnormal{L}^{2}(0,1)$ .
A similar construction can be done on $\mathbf{T}^{d}$ , that we still denote $(h_{n})_{n}$ .
The sequence $v_{n}:=r_{2}\frac{1+h_{n}}{2}+r_{1}\frac{1-h_{n}}{2}$ takes therefore its values in $\{r_{1},r_{2}\}$ (so that $P(v_{n})=0$ ) and does not admit a subsequence converging almost everywhere. If $\Phi_{n}:=u_{n}(1+v_{n}^{2})$ and $\Psi_{n}:=v_{n}(1+u_{n}^{2})$ , the assumptions of Proposition 3 imply that both $(\Phi_{n})_{n}$ and $(\Psi_{n})_{n}$ are bounded in $\textnormal{H}^{1}(\mathbf{T}^{d})$ . For $m\in\mathbf{N}^{\star}$ , $k\in[1,+\infty)$ , $f\in\textnormal{V}_{m}$ , one has $\|f\|_{\infty}\lesssim(2m)^{\frac{d}{k}}\|f\|_{k}$ . It can be obtained by estimating the $L^{k^{\prime}}$ norm of the Dirichlet kernel (on $\mathbf{T}^{d}$ ) and using
Young’s inequality for convolutions. We then state the Lemma 5 . There exists $\textnormal{c}>0$ such that, for $p\in[1,\infty]$ , any $m\in\mathbf{N}^{\star}$ and $f\in\textnormal{V}_{m}$ , the following estimate holds: $\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0,\qquad\left\|e^{t\Delta}f\right\|_{p}\lesssim e^{-\textnormal{c}tm^{2}}\|f\|_{p}.$ Proof. There exists $\varphi\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbf{R}^{\star})$ such that for any $g\in\textnormal{V}_{m}$ , $g=\sum_{k\in\mathbf{Z}^{d}}c_{k}(g)\varphi(|k|/m)e^{ik\cdot x}.$ This is in particular true for $g=e^{t\Delta}f$ for $t\geq 0$ , and we thus infer $e^{t/m^{2}\Delta f}=\sum_{k\in\mathbf{Z}^{d}}c_{k}(f)e^{-t|k|^{2}/m^{2}}\varphi(|k|/m)e^{ik\cdot x}=f\star\widecheck{\mathrm{d}_{m}\varphi_{t}},$ where $\varphi_{t}(x):=e^{-tx^{2}}\varphi(x)$ , $\mathrm{d}_{m}\varphi_{t}(x)=\varphi_{t}(x/m)$ , and for any test function $\psi\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbf{R})$ , $\widecheck{\psi}(x):=\sum_{k\in\mathbf{Z}^{d}}\psi(|k|)e^{ik\cdot x},$ so that here $\widecheck{\mathrm{d}_{m}\varphi_{t}}(x):=\sum_{k\in\mathbf{Z}^{d}}\varphi(|k|/m)e^{-t|k|^{2}/m^{2}}e^{ik\cdot x}.$ Thanks to Young’s inequality for convolutions, we only need to prove $\|\widecheck{\mathrm{d}_{m}\varphi_{t}}\|_{1}\lesssim e^{-\textnormal{c}t}.$ This can be checked using [ 15 , Lemma 1.1] ,
from which we can extract the following result:
For any $\psi\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbf{R})$ and $m\in\mathbf{N}^{\star}$ , $\|\widecheck{\mathrm{d}_{m}\psi}\|_{1}\lesssim\|\psi^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}$ . In the particular case of $\psi=\varphi_{t}$ , since $\varphi$ vanishes away from $0$ , one has $\|\varphi_{t}^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\lesssim e^{-\textnormal{c}t}$ and the estimate follows. ∎ We infer from the two previous estimates
the following one, for the heat flow generated by a source term in $\textnormal{V}_{m}$ . Lemma 6 . For $T>0$ , $m\in\mathbf{N}^{\star}$ , $k\in[1,+\infty)$ and $f\in\textnormal{L}^{k}(Q_{T})$ such that $f(t,\cdot)\in\textnormal{C}_{m}$ for all $t\in[0,T]$ , we have the following estimate: $\displaystyle\left\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right\|_{\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(Q_{T})}\lesssim_{d}m^{\frac{d}{k}-\frac{2}{k^{\prime}}}\|f\|_{\textnormal{L}^{k}(Q_{T})}.$ Proof. For $t\in[0,T]$ and $x\in\mathbf{T}^{d}$ we have, using Bernstein’s Lemma 4 , $\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}f(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{(t-s)\Delta}f(s)\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq(2m)^{\frac{d}{k}}\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{(t-s)\Delta}f(s)\|_{k}\,\mathrm{d}s.$ Now using the decay of the heat flow given in Lemma 5 , we infer $\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}f(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{(t-s)\Delta}f(s)\|_{\infty}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq(2m)^{\frac{d}{k}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)4\textnormal{c}m^{2}}\|f(s)\|_{k}\,\mathrm{d}s,$ and the estimate follows directly from Hölder’s inequality. ∎ We now can prove the following Proposition 4 . Fix $T>0$ , $k>1+\frac{d}{2}$ . For all $f\in\textnormal{L}^{k}(Q_{T})$ having a vanishing mean at all times, one has $\displaystyle\left\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right\|_{\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(Q_{T})}\lesssim_{d,k}\|f\|_{\textnormal{L}^{k}(Q_{T})}.$ Before starting the proof, we introduce a few notations related to the Littlewood-Paley decomposition for elements defined on the torus $\mathbf{T}^{d}$ . Any $f\in\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbf{T}^{d})$ decomposes uniquely as $\displaystyle f:=\sum_{k\in\mathbf{Z}^{d}}c_{k}(f)e^{ik\cdot x}.$ For $j\in\mathbf{N}$ , we introduce the corresponding dyadic block $\displaystyle f_{j}:=\sum_{k\in\textnormal{C}_{2^{j}}}c_{k}(f)e^{ik\cdot x}.$ If $f$ has zero mean (that is, $c_{0}(f)=0$ ), we have (at least in $\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbf{T}^{d})$ ) $\displaystyle f=\sum_{j\in\mathbf{N}}f_{j},$ and this decomposition is orthogonal in $\textnormal{L}^{2}(\mathbf{T}^{d})$ if $f$ belongs to this space.
We first state a result about a norm (which is actually of Besov type) which naturally appears when dealing with the previous decomposition: Lemma 7 . For $k\in[2,+\infty]$ and $f\in\textnormal{L}^{k}(\mathbf{T}^{d})$ having a vanishing mean, introducing the sequence $b_{f}:=(\|f_{j}\|_{k})_{j\in\mathbf{N}}$ , one has $\|b_{f}\|_{\ell^{k}(\mathbf{N})}\lesssim_{k}\|f\|_{k}.$ Proof. For $k=2$ there is in fact (up to some constant) equality: this is simply Parseval’s Theorem together with the orthogonality of the dyadic blocks. For $k=+\infty$ , the inequality holds thanks to the continuity of $f\mapsto f_{j}$ from $\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\mathbf{T}^{d})$ to itself, with a norm uniformly bounded w.r.t. $\,j\in\mathbf{N}$ . All the intermediate cases follow then from Riesz-Thorin interpolation Theorem. ∎ Proof of Propositon 4 . We use the Littlewood-Paley decomposition on $f(s)$ at each time $s\in[0,T]$ to infer $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=\sum_{j\in\mathbf{N}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}f_{j}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.$ Using the triangular inequality and the estimate of Lemma 6 for the heat flow generated by a source supported on an annulus, we get $\displaystyle\left\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right\|_{\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(Q_{T})}\lesssim_{d}\sum_{j\in\mathbf{N}}(2^{j})^{\frac{d}{k}-\frac{2}{k^{\prime}}}\|f_{j}\|_{\textnormal{L}^{k}(Q_{T})}.$ The assumption $k>1+\frac{d}{2}$ is equivalent to $\frac{d}{k}-\frac{2}{k^{\prime}}<0$ , so that we get by Hölder’s inequality the estimate $\displaystyle\left\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\right\|_{\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(Q_{T})}\lesssim_{d,k}\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbf{N}}\|f_{j}\|_{\textnormal{L}^{k}(Q_{T})}^{k}\right)^{1/k},$ and the conclusion eventually follows from Lemma 7 . ∎ We end the proof of Theorem 5 by using Proposition 4 . Proof of Theorem 5 . Introducing $\widetilde{\Phi}:=\Phi-e^{t\Delta}\Phi(0)$ and $f:=\partial_{t}\Phi-\Delta\Phi$ , we have in particular $\widetilde{\Phi}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.$ For a function $\varphi$ on the torus, we introduce the notation $\underline{\varphi}:=\varphi-\int_{\mathbf{T}^{d}}\varphi,$ with an obvious extension for functions depending also on the time variable. We therefore have $\displaystyle\underline{\widetilde{\Phi}}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}\underline{f(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s.$ Using Proposition 4 , we infer for $k>1+\frac{d}{2}$ $\displaystyle\|\underline{\widetilde{\Phi}}\|_{\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(Q_{T})}\lesssim_{k,d}\|\underline{f}\|_{\textnormal{L}^{k}(Q_{T})}\lesssim\|f\|_{\textnormal{L}^{k}(Q_{T})}.$ On the other hand, we have $\displaystyle\underline{\Phi}=\underline{e^{t\Delta}\Phi(0)}+\underline{\widetilde{\Phi}},$ and the maximum principle for the heat equation implies the bound $\displaystyle\|\underline{e^{t\Delta}\Phi(0)}\|_{\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(Q_{T})}\leq 2\|\Phi(0)\|_{\infty},$ so that the announced estimate follows. ∎ Appendix D Classical SKT system: another method yielding global uniform a priori estimates In this short part of the Appendix, we propose another method enabling to obtain a global uniform estimate for solutions to the SKT system, which uses standard Sobolev norms. This method could be adapted to the general case of systems like ( 1 ), but since it involves taking many derivatives, we have chosen to write it in the specific case when $p$ and $q$ are both linear, that is in the case of the classical SKT system (without reaction terms): $\left\{\begin{lgathered}\partial_{t}u-\Delta\big[(d_{1}+a_{1}u+b_{1}v)u\big]=0,\\
\partial_{t}v-\Delta\big[(d_{2}+a_{2}u+b_{2}v)v\big]=0,\end{lgathered}\right.$ (22) where $a_{i},b_{i},d_{i}$ are nonnegative constants. In the sequel, for any function $f$ defined on $\mathbf{T}^{d}$ , we use the following notation $\underline{f}:=f-\int_{\mathbf{T}^{d}}f.$ Proposition 5 . For $k>d/2$ , there exists $\delta>0$ depending on the coefficients $a_{i},b_{i},d_{i}$ and on $k,d$ , such that any smooth nonnegative solution $(u,v)$ to ( 22 ) satisfying $\displaystyle\|\underline{u}(0),\underline{v}(0)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}\leq\delta/2,$ satisfies furthemore $\displaystyle\|\underline{u}(t),\underline{v}(t)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|\underline{u}(s),\underline{v}(s)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k+1}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\delta,$ for all positive time $t$ . In particular, due to the Sobolev embedding $\textnormal{H}^{k}(\mathbf{T}^{d})\hookrightarrow\textnormal{L}^{\infty}(\mathbf{T}^{d})$ , this entails a global uniform bound for the solution. Proof. Fix $\alpha\in\mathbf{N}^{d}$ and denote $\partial^{\alpha}$ the corresponding (spatial) differential operator. Applying it to the first equation of ( 22 ), we get $\displaystyle\partial_{t}\partial^{\alpha}u-\Delta\big[(d_{1}+a_{1}u+b_{1}v)\partial^{\alpha}u\big]=\Delta\left[\sum_{0<\beta\leq\alpha}c_{\alpha,\beta}\partial^{\beta}(a_{1}u+b_{1}v)\partial^{\alpha-\beta}u\right],$ where we used Leibniz formula in the r.h.s. (with the partial order $\beta_{i}\leq\alpha_{i}$ , for all $i$ on $\mathbf{N}^{d}$ ). Using Lemma 1 with $z=\partial^{\alpha}u$ and $\mu=d_{1}+au+bv$ , for all $\alpha\in\mathbf{N}^{d}$ satisying $0<|\alpha|\leq k+1$ , and taking the sum over all those multi-indices, we get $\|\underline{u}(t,\cdot)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\underline{u}(s,\cdot)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k+1}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\\
\leq\|\underline{u}(0,\cdot)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}^{2}+\frac{1}{d_{1}}\sum_{0<|\alpha|\leq k+1}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{T}^{d}}\left[\sum_{0<\beta\leq\alpha}c_{\alpha,\beta}\partial^{\beta}(a_{1}u+b_{1}v)\partial^{\alpha-\beta}u\right]^{2}.$ A similar estimate holds for $v$ , and summing both, we get for $\underline{U}:=(\underline{u},\underline{v})$ (and for a symbol $\lesssim$ which depends only on the parameters $a_{i},b_{i},d_{i}$ ): $\|\underline{U}(t,\cdot)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}^{2}-\|\underline{U}(0,\cdot)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\underline{U}(s,\cdot)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k+1}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\\
\lesssim\sum_{0<|\alpha|\leq k+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta+\gamma=\alpha\\
0<|\beta|\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{T}^{d}}|\partial^{\beta}U|^{2}\cdot|\partial^{\gamma}U|^{2}\\
\lesssim\sum_{0<|\alpha|\leq k+1}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{T}^{d}}|\partial^{\alpha}U|^{2}\cdot|U|^{2}\\
+\sum_{0<|\alpha|\leq k+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta+\gamma=\alpha\\
0<|\beta|,|\gamma|<|\alpha|\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial^{\beta}\underline{U}(s)\|_{2p}^{2}\|\partial^{\gamma}\underline{U}(s)\|_{2p^{\prime}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,$ (23) for any pair of conjugate exponents $1\leq p,p^{\prime}\leq\infty$ . In the final r.h.s., all the terms of the first sum are bounded by $\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|\underline{U}(s)\|_{\infty}^{2}\,\int_{0}^{t}\|\underline{U}(s)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k+1}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.$ Our goal is to obtain a similar bound for the double sum appearing on the last line. In order to do so, we will use the Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev interpolation inequality. For $0<|\beta|<|\alpha|$ , this estimate implies $\displaystyle\|\partial^{\beta}f\|_{2\frac{|\alpha|}{|\beta|}}\lesssim_{d,|\alpha|,|\beta|}\|\textnormal{D}^{|\alpha|}f\|_{2}^{\frac{|\beta|}{|\alpha|}}\|f\|_{\infty}^{1-\frac{|\beta|}{|\alpha|}},$ since $\displaystyle\frac{|\beta|}{2|\alpha|}=\frac{|\beta|}{d}+\frac{|\beta|}{|\alpha|}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{|\alpha|}{d}\right).$ Now, getting back to the last double sum of ( 23 ), if $\beta\neq 0$ is linked to $\gamma\neq 0$ through $\beta+\gamma=\alpha$ , the conjugate of $p=\frac{|\alpha|}{|\beta|}$ is $p^{\prime}=\frac{|\alpha|}{|\gamma|}$ . We have therefore the following estimate for any $|\alpha|\leq k+1$ : $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial^{\beta}\underline{U}(s)\|_{2p}^{2}\|\partial^{\gamma}\underline{U}(s)\|_{2p^{\prime}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s$ $\displaystyle\lesssim_{d,k}\int_{0}^{t}\|\textnormal{D}^{|\alpha|}\underline{U}(s)\|_{2}^{2}\|\underline{U}(s)\|_{\infty}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s$ $\displaystyle\leq\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|\underline{U}(s)\|_{\infty}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|\underline{U}(s)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k+1}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.$ At the end of the day, we infer from estimate ( 23 ) the following one: $\|\underline{U}(t,\cdot)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}^{2}-\|\underline{U}(0,\cdot)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\underline{U}(s,\cdot)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k+1}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\\
\lesssim\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|\underline{U}(s)\|_{\infty}^{2}\,\int_{0}^{t}\|\underline{U}(s)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k+1}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,$ where this time $\lesssim$ depends on the coefficients $a_{i},b_{i},d_{i}$ and also on $k$ and $d$ . Defining $A(t):=\|\underline{U}(t,\cdot)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\underline{U}(s,\cdot)\|_{\textnormal{H}^{k+1}(\mathbf{T}^{d})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,$ we see that we can write $A(t)-A(0)\lesssim A(t)^{2},$ and the proof can be concluded thanks to Lemma 3 of Appendix B. ∎ References [1] Herbert Amann.
